## Core Content

Delegation is often framed as a time-management technique — freeing up a leader's calendar to focus on higher-value work. Here's the "why" this framing misses the deeper stakes: this curriculum's BLUE track covered key person risk — the danger of a business depending too heavily on one individual whose sudden absence would create serious operational and financial disruption. A leader who fails to delegate meaningfully isn't just managing their calendar poorly; they're concentrating critical knowledge, relationships, and decision-making authority in a single point of failure — themselves.

This is why effective delegation should be evaluated by a different question than "does this free up my time": does this reduce the organization's dependency on any single person, including me, for critical functions to continue operating? A leader who delegates only routine, low-stakes tasks while retaining every genuinely important decision hasn't actually reduced key person risk at all, regardless of how much calendar time they've freed up.

If you were suddenly unavailable for a month, what would actually break in your organization, and does your current delegation pattern address that specific vulnerability or just your day-to-day schedule?

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A leader delegates routine administrative tasks to free up time, feeling this represents good delegation practice. [ASSUMPTION] Every significant client relationship, strategic decision, and piece of critical operational knowledge remains concentrated entirely with this leader — when they become unexpectedly unavailable for an extended period, the organization discovers that the delegation that had freed up their calendar did nothing to address the actual key person risk concentrated in their undelegated relationships and knowledge, echoing exactly the risk the BLUE track's key person insurance discussion was designed to address.
