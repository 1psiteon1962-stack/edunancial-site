By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

- Explain why hiring often receives less rigorous evaluation than its actual stakes warrant

## Core Content

A single hiring decision can affect a business for years — a strong hire compounds value through their own output and their effect on team dynamics; a poor hire compounds cost through their own underperformance, the management time spent addressing it, and the damage to team morale while the problem persists. Here's the "why" this makes hiring an unusually high-leverage decision: few other individual choices a leader makes carry this combination of long duration and compounding effect, whether positive or negative.

Here's the mismatch this lesson highlights, connecting to the investment-evaluation rigor covered throughout the GOLD and ORANGE tracks: many businesses apply more rigorous evaluation to a moderate equipment purchase or software subscription than to a hiring decision that will cost far more over its tenure and compound in ways a piece of equipment never will. Applying the same risk-adjusted, evidence-based evaluation rigor covered elsewhere in this curriculum to hiring decisions specifically would treat interviewing and reference-checking with the same seriousness as evaluating any other significant capital commitment.

How does the rigor of your current hiring process compare to the rigor you'd apply to a purchase of similar total cost? If hiring gets less scrutiny, what does that mismatch suggest about how the decision's true leverage is being underweighted?

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A business applies extensive evaluation, multiple approvals, and careful cost-benefit analysis before purchasing a piece of equipment costing a specific amount, while its hiring process for a role with a multi-year expected tenure and a total compensation cost far exceeding that equipment purchase involves a much lighter, less structured evaluation. [ASSUMPTION] Given hiring's unusually high leverage and compounding effect, this mismatch in evaluation rigor represents a genuine underinvestment in the decision that actually carries more total financial stakes and longer-term consequence than the equipment purchase that received far more careful scrutiny.

## Key Takeaways
- A hiring decision carries unusually high leverage, compounding over years through the person's output and effect on team dynamics.
- The same evaluation rigor applied to significant purchases or investments elsewhere in this curriculum should apply to hiring decisions.
- Many organizations underinvest in hiring rigor relative to the decision's actual, compounding financial stakes.
