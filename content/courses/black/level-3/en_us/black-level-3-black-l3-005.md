## Core Content

Lesson 1 established that people decisions carry financial weight, whether or not that's explicitly named. Here's a specific, common application of that principle: a leader facing a genuinely underperforming employee often delays the difficult termination decision, hoping for improvement, avoiding the discomfort of the conversation, or simply putting off an unpleasant task. Here's the "why" this delay generally makes the eventual outcome worse, not better: the underperformance continues affecting team output and morale during the entire delay period, other team members' frustration compounds the longer the issue visibly goes unaddressed, and the eventual transition cost (finding and onboarding a replacement) doesn't shrink by waiting — it simply arrives later, after additional accumulated cost.

This mirrors a pattern covered elsewhere in this curriculum: delaying a necessary but uncomfortable action generally compounds its eventual cost rather than avoiding it. A leader who acts decisively once a genuine, fair evaluation confirms the problem is real generally produces a better outcome — for the team, and often even for the departing employee, who's spared a prolonged period of visible underperformance and associated stress — than one who delays indefinitely hoping the problem resolves itself.

Is there a difficult personnel decision you're currently delaying? What is the actual, compounding cost of that delay, versus the discomfort of addressing it now?

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A leader has genuine, well-documented evidence that a specific employee isn't meeting the role's requirements, but delays the termination conversation for months, hoping the situation will improve on its own. [ASSUMPTION] During those months, team morale visibly erodes as other team members absorb the extra work and notice the unaddressed issue, and when the termination eventually happens anyway, the business faces the same replacement and onboarding cost it would have faced months earlier — plus the accumulated morale and productivity cost of the delay itself, illustrating why the decisive action, once genuinely warranted, generally produces a better total outcome than prolonged avoidance.

1. Because the underperformance, team frustration, and morale erosion continue throughout the delay period, while the eventual replacement cost doesn't shrink — it simply arrives later, with additional accumulated cost on top.
2. Continued underperformance affecting team output, growing frustration among other team members, and morale erosion, all compounding before the eventual, unavoidable transition cost.
3. Because it spares the departing employee a prolonged period of visible underperformance, associated stress, and awkward team dynamics that delay only extends.

1. This is the central "why" the lesson is built around.
2. This checks understanding of the specific compounding costs the lesson identifies.

## Key Takeaways
- Delaying a genuinely warranted termination decision generally compounds its total cost rather than avoiding it.
- Team morale, productivity, and frustration erode throughout the delay, while the eventual transition cost still arrives regardless.
- Decisive action, once a fair evaluation confirms the problem is real, often produces a better outcome for the team and even for the departing employee.
