## Core Content

Lesson 5 established that delaying a difficult termination decision generally compounds its cost rather than avoiding it. Here's the broader "why" this same pattern applies to conflict avoidance generally, not just termination decisions specifically: unaddressed tension between team members, an unspoken disagreement about direction, or a leader's own unaddressed frustration with a colleague's behavior doesn't disappear when avoided — it tends to fester, often becoming more entrenched and harder to resolve the longer it's left unaddressed, exactly the compounding pattern Lesson 5 described.

Here's the "why" conflict avoidance can be particularly hard to recognize in oneself: it often masquerades as patience, diplomacy, or giving a situation "time to work itself out" — framings that feel reasonable and even virtuous in the moment, while functioning identically to the costly delay pattern this track has repeatedly warned against. A leader avoiding a direct conversation isn't necessarily being patient; they may simply be avoiding short-term discomfort at the cost of a larger, more entrenched problem later.

Is there a piece of unaddressed tension or disagreement in your team right now that you've been framing as "giving it time" rather than recognizing as avoidance? What would change if you named it honestly?

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A leader notices growing tension between two team members but avoids addressing it directly, telling themselves they're giving the situation space to resolve naturally. [ASSUMPTION] Months later, the tension has hardened into open conflict, affecting the broader team's dynamics and productivity — a problem that would have been far easier to address when it first emerged, illustrating that the leader's "patience" functioned identically to the compounding delay pattern Lesson 5 warned against, just applied to interpersonal conflict rather than a termination decision.

## Key Takeaways
- Unaddressed conflict tends to become more entrenched and harder to resolve the longer it's avoided, echoing the compounding-delay pattern from Lesson 5.
