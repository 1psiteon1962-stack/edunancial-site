## Core Content

This curriculum's GOLD track established that a sound investment thesis should be falsifiable, specifying in advance what evidence would indicate it's wrong — and warned against the unfalsifiable reasoning pattern of absorbing any outcome as confirmation. Here's the "why" a leader's ego creates exactly this same unfalsifiable pattern, but with organizational money and time at stake: a leader who has publicly committed to a strategy may resist acknowledging clear evidence that it's failing, not because the evidence is genuinely ambiguous, but because admitting the mistake feels like a personal defeat.

This connects directly to a well-documented pattern sometimes called the sunk cost fallacy — continuing to invest in a failing course of action specifically because of what's already been invested, rather than because of what continuing is actually likely to produce. A leader's ego can turn this fallacy from an individual bias into an organizational one, since the organization's resources keep flowing toward a strategy the leader can't bring themselves to admit isn't working.

Is there a strategy or decision you're currently continuing to support, at least in part, because reversing course would feel like admitting you were wrong? What would a genuinely disinterested evaluator say about whether that strategy is actually working?

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A leader publicly champions a specific strategic direction, and as evidence accumulates that it isn't working, continues defending and funding it rather than acknowledging the mistake. [ASSUMPTION] Echoing the GOLD track's caution about unfalsifiable reasoning, this leader treats every piece of disconfirming evidence as a reason to adjust tactics rather than reconsider the underlying strategy, continuing to commit organizational resources to a failing direction specifically to avoid the personal discomfort of admitting error — a direct, measurable financial cost created by ego rather than by the strategy's own merits.
