## Core Content

This curriculum's PURPLE track established that a fiduciary duty requires acting in another party's best interests, with corporate directors and officers cited as a common example of a fiduciary relationship. Here's the "why" a genuine board of directors exists specifically to provide oversight and accountability, not simply to validate and support whatever leadership proposes: a board's fiduciary duty runs to the organization and its shareholders or stakeholders, not to the comfort or convenience of the executive leadership it oversees — meaning a board that only ever agrees with leadership's decisions isn't fulfilling its actual governance function, regardless of how smoothly board meetings proceed.

This connects directly to Lesson 10's discussion of the value of genuine pushback against leadership overconfidence: a well-functioning board is meant to be exactly the kind of structural check that lesson described, asking hard questions, challenging assumptions, and being willing to disagree with executive leadership when the evidence warrants it — a board that never does this has become, in substance, an extension of executive leadership rather than a genuine oversight body.

Does your organization's board (or equivalent oversight structure) actually challenge leadership decisions with any regularity, or has it become a body that simply ratifies whatever leadership proposes?

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A company's board of directors consistently approves every proposal from executive leadership without substantive pushback or independent challenge, priding itself on smooth, harmonious meetings.
