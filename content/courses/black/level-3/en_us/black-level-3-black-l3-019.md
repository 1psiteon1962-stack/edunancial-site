## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
- Explain why deliberately building a team that disagrees improves decision quality
- Connect this concept to Lessons 10, 11, and 18's discussion of overconfidence and structural pushback
- Explain why comfortable agreement can be a warning sign, not evidence of a good team

## Core Content

Lessons 10, 11, and 18 have each, in different ways, established the value of genuine pushback against a leader's judgment — countering overconfidence, enabling psychological safety, and fulfilling a board's actual oversight function. This lesson closes that arc with a practical, actionable synthesis: a leader should deliberately build a team that includes people willing and able to disagree with them, rather than surrounding themselves with people who tend to agree easily.

Here's the "why" comfortable agreement can actually be a warning sign rather than evidence of a strong team: if every significant decision receives quick, easy agreement from everyone in the room, either the decisions are genuinely uncontroversial (unlikely for most significant choices), or the team has learned, consciously or not, that disagreement isn't welcome — echoing exactly the psychological safety concern from Lesson 11. A leader serious about decision quality needs to actively recruit, promote, and protect people who will tell them when they're wrong.

Look at your current leadership team or closest advisors. Who among them regularly disagrees with you, and when was the last time that disagreement actually changed a decision?

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A leader notices that every proposal presented to their leadership team receives quick, unanimous agreement, and initially interprets this as evidence of a strong, aligned team. [ASSUMPTION] On closer reflection, connecting to the psychological safety concern from Lesson 11, this leader recognizes that easy agreement might actually reflect a team that has learned disagreement isn't welcome, rather than genuine consensus — prompting a deliberate effort to recruit team members known for independent judgment and to actively invite and reward dissenting views, rather than continuing to interpret comfortable agreement as a sign of team strength.
