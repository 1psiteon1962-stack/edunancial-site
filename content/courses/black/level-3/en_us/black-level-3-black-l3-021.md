## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
- Explain why a leader's time should be evaluated with the same discipline applied to financial capital
- Connect this concept to the opportunity cost discussion covered in this curriculum's GOLD and ORANGE tracks
- Explain why "is this worth my time" deserves the same rigor as "is this worth my money"

## Core Content

This curriculum's GOLD and ORANGE tracks established that every dollar of capital, and every marketing dollar, carries an opportunity cost — what else that same resource could have accomplished. Here's the "why" a leader's time deserves the exact same evaluative discipline: unlike money, a leader's time can't be borrowed, saved for later, or replenished once spent — every hour committed to one activity is an hour definitively unavailable for anything else, making it, if anything, a scarcer and less recoverable resource than capital.

This is why "is this worth my time" deserves the same rigorous, opportunity-cost-aware evaluation this curriculum has repeatedly applied to financial decisions — a leader who evaluates spending decisions carefully while accepting every meeting, every request, and every commitment without the same scrutiny is applying inconsistent discipline to two resources that deserve equal rigor, particularly given that time, unlike money, can never actually be recovered once spent.

Look at your calendar from the past week. Which commitments would have survived the same risk-adjusted, opportunity-cost evaluation you'd apply to a significant financial decision, and which wouldn't have?

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A leader carefully evaluates every significant financial expenditure using rigorous cost-benefit analysis, while accepting nearly every meeting request and commitment on their calendar without applying anything close to the same scrutiny. [ASSUMPTION] Applying the same opportunity-cost discipline from the GOLD and ORANGE tracks to time specifically, this leader recognizes that many calendar commitments wouldn't survive the same evaluation a comparable financial expenditure would face — prompting a more deliberate, disciplined approach to time allocation that treats hours with the same rigor previously reserved only for dollars.

## Answer Key

1. Because every hour committed to one activity is definitively unavailable for anything else, making inconsistent evaluative rigor between time and money a genuine gap in decision discipline.
2. This lesson extends the opportunity-cost principle applied to financial capital and marketing spend elsewhere in this curriculum directly to a leader's own time and attention.
3. Because unlike money, time can't be borrowed, saved for later, or replenished once spent, making it an even less recoverable resource than capital.

## Answers and Explanations

1. This is the central "why" the lesson is built around.
2. This explicitly connects the lesson back to the GOLD and ORANGE tracks' opportunity-cost framework.
3. This reinforces the specific reason time may deserve even more careful evaluation than money.

## Key Takeaways
- A leader's time deserves the same risk-adjusted, opportunity-cost-aware evaluation applied to financial decisions elsewhere in this curriculum.
- Time is, if anything, a scarcer resource than money, since it can't be borrowed, saved, or replenished once spent.
- Applying the same discipline to calendar commitments that's applied to financial decisions often reveals commitments that wouldn't otherwise survive scrutiny.
