## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
- Explain why what leadership tolerates defines culture more powerfully than what it announces
- Connect this concept to Lesson 3's discussion of incentives shaping culture
- Explain why exceptions made for high performers carry outsized cultural cost

## Core Content

Lesson 3 established that incentive structures shape culture more powerfully than stated values. Here's a closely related "why": what leadership actually tolerates — the behaviors it allows to continue without consequence, even when they contradict stated values — sends an equally, if not more, powerful signal than any announced policy, because tolerance is observed behavior, while a policy is just words until it's actually enforced or not.

Here's the "why" this carries particular weight when the tolerated behavior comes from a high performer: an organization that enforces its stated standards consistently against average performers, but makes exceptions for someone whose results are valuable enough to seem worth protecting, teaches everyone watching that the real standard is "follow the rules unless you're valuable enough not to have to" — a lesson that erodes trust in leadership's stated values far more than a single violation from an average performer ever would.

Is there a behavior in your organization that contradicts a stated value but continues because the person exhibiting it is otherwise high-performing? What is that exception actually teaching everyone else who's watching?

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An organization has a clearly stated value around respectful communication, enforced consistently across most of the team. [ASSUMPTION] A high-performing team member regularly violates this stated value, but leadership tolerates it, reasoning that this person's results are too valuable to risk a difficult conversation or consequence. Other team members observe this exception and correctly conclude that the real, operative standard isn't the stated value — it's "follow the rules unless your results make you exempt," a far more damaging lesson to organizational trust than the original behavior itself, echoing Lesson 3's principle that actual tolerance shapes culture more than announced values.
