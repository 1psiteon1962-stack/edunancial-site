## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
- Explain why a leader's first instinct during a crisis is often unreliable
- Connect this concept to the recency bias and overconfidence themes covered in this curriculum's WHITE and GOLD tracks
- Explain why building in a deliberate pause improves crisis decision quality without sacrificing needed speed

## Core Content

Lesson 16 established that crisis leadership requires faster decision-making than steady-state leadership. Here's an important refinement to that principle: speed doesn't mean acting on the very first instinct that arises — this curriculum's WHITE track covered recency bias (overweighting the most recent, emotionally salient information) and the GOLD track covered overconfidence, both of which tend to be especially pronounced under the acute stress a genuine crisis produces.

Here's the "why" a brief, deliberate pause — even a few minutes, not the extensive consultation Lesson 16 correctly warned against — generally improves crisis decision quality: a first instinct formed under acute stress is disproportionately influenced by the most alarming, recent information available, rather than a fuller, calmer assessment of the actual situation. A brief pause to ask "is this genuinely my best judgment, or is this the first thing my stress response produced" catches exactly the kind of distortion these behavioral biases predict, without requiring the extended delay that would actually undermine effective crisis response.

The next time you feel a strong, immediate instinct during a stressful situation, what would a brief, deliberate pause — even just a few minutes — actually cost you, compared to what it might catch?

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A leader facing an unfolding crisis feels a strong, immediate instinct to respond in a specific way, driven largely by the most alarming piece of information that just arrived. [ASSUMPTION] Taking a brief, deliberate pause — a few minutes, not an extended delay — to ask whether this instinct reflects genuinely sound judgment or simply an acute stress reaction to the most recent, alarming information, this leader recognizes the instinct was disproportionately shaped by recency bias, and adjusts to a more measured response that still meets the speed requirements Lesson 16 described, while avoiding the specific distortion this brief pause was designed to catch.

## Key Takeaways
- A leader's first instinct during a crisis is often distorted by acute stress, echoing the recency bias and overconfidence concepts covered elsewhere in this curriculum.

- Speed and thoughtful judgment aren't mutually exclusive during a crisis; a few minutes of deliberate reflection can improve decision quality significantly.
