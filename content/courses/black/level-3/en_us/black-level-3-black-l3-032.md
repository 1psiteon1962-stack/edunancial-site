## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
- Extend the incentive-shapes-behavior principle from Lessons 3 and 19 to leadership's own compensation structure
- Explain why leadership compensation carries an outsized signaling effect throughout an organization
- Explain why misalignment between leadership incentives and stated organizational priorities undermines trust

## Core Content

Lessons 3 and 19 established that incentive structures shape behavior and signal genuine priorities more powerfully than stated values. Here's the "why" this applies with particular, outsized force to leadership's own compensation structure specifically: employees generally pay close attention to how leadership is compensated, since it reveals, in concrete terms, what the organization actually rewards at its highest level — a leader compensated heavily on short-term metrics while publicly emphasizing long-term sustainability sends a signal that, echoing Lesson 22's discussion of tolerated exceptions, employees are likely to notice and draw conclusions from regardless of what's officially announced.

This is why leadership compensation deserves the same scrutiny for alignment with stated organizational priorities that this curriculum has applied to sales commission structures throughout — if long-term customer relationships, employee development, or sustainable growth are genuinely organizational priorities, leadership's own compensation structure should reflect and reward exactly those things, not simply short-term metrics that happen to be easier to measure.

Does your own compensation structure, or your organization's top leadership compensation structure, actually reward what you publicly claim matters most, or does it reward something else entirely that employees can plainly see?

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A company's leadership publicly emphasizes long-term sustainable growth and employee development as core priorities, while executive compensation is structured almost entirely around short-term quarterly financial metrics. [ASSUMPTION] Employees throughout the organization notice this misalignment, drawing the reasonable conclusion that the stated long-term priorities are less genuine than the short-term metrics actually driving leadership's own financial incentives — echoing exactly the incentive-versus-speech gap discussed in Lessons 3 and 19, now applied to leadership's own compensation rather than a sales team's.
