## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
- Explain why optimizing purely for short-term results can damage long-term organizational health
- Connect this concept to the sequence of returns risk discussed in this curriculum's WHITE track
- Explain why genuine leadership requires holding both timeframes in view simultaneously, not choosing one

## Core Content

This curriculum's WHITE track covered sequence of returns risk — how the timing of investment outcomes matters as much as their average, particularly during a withdrawal period. Here's a related "why" leadership decisions carry a similar timing dimension: a leader under pressure to show strong short-term results can make decisions — cutting genuinely valuable but slower-return investments like leadership development (Lesson 24) or employee morale investments, pushing sales in ways that damage long-term customer relationships (echoing the ORANGE track's growth-versus-unit-economics discussion) — that improve this quarter's numbers while quietly damaging the organization's longer-term health.

Here's the "why" genuine leadership requires holding both timeframes simultaneously, not simply choosing one: an organization that survives only by consistently sacrificing long-term health for short-term results is, in effect, borrowing against its own future in a way that eventually comes due, similar to how a poor sequence of returns can undermine an otherwise sound long-term financial plan, as the WHITE track described. Sustainable leadership requires evaluating decisions against both timeframes, not treating short-term pressure as an excuse to ignore longer-term consequences.

Look at a recent decision made primarily to hit a short-term target. What longer-term cost, if any, did that decision create, and was that trade-off actually made deliberately, or did short-term pressure simply crowd out the longer-term consideration entirely?

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A leader facing pressure to show strong quarterly results cuts spending on leadership development and employee engagement initiatives, improving the current quarter's financial figures. [ASSUMPTION] Over subsequent quarters, the erosion in management capability and employee morale that these cuts quietly created begins showing up in higher turnover and declining performance — a delayed cost the leader didn't fully weigh against the immediate short-term benefit, echoing the WHITE track's point that timing and sequence matter as much as the average outcome, applied here to leadership decision-making rather than investment returns.
