## Main Lesson

### Topic 1: What Profit Margin Measures
Profit margin expresses profit as a percentage of revenue: (Profit ÷ Revenue) × 100. It answers a different question than raw profit dollars do — not "how much did we keep," but "what percentage of every dollar of sales did we actually keep."

### Topic 2: Why Percentage Beats Raw Dollars Alone
[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] Business A has $1,000,000 in revenue and $50,000 in profit (a 5% margin). Business B has $200,000 in revenue and $50,000 in profit (a 25% margin). Both kept the same $50,000, but Business B is converting sales into profit five times more efficiently.

### Topic 3: What a Low Margin Signals
A low profit margin can mean high costs relative to sales, heavy price competition, or an inefficient operation — and it leaves very little cushion if costs rise or sales dip even slightly.

### Topic 4: What Margin Doesn't Tell You
[ESTIMATE] Margin alone doesn't tell you the total dollar amount available to reinvest or take home — a large business with a modest margin can still generate more total profit than a small business with a high margin. Margin and raw profit answer different questions and are both useful together.

## Key Definitions
- **Profit Margin:** Profit expressed as a percentage of revenue, calculated as (Profit ÷ Revenue) × 100.
- **High-Margin Business:** A business that keeps a large percentage of each sales dollar as profit.
- **Low-Margin Business:** A business that keeps a small percentage of each sales dollar as profit, often relying on high volume.

## Worked Examples
[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A restaurant with $30,000 in monthly revenue and $3,000 in profit has a 10% margin. A software company with $30,000 in monthly revenue and $21,000 in profit has a 70% margin — a common real-world pattern, since software has lower per-sale costs than food service.

## Common Mistakes
- Comparing two businesses' profit dollars without checking their margins
- Assuming a high-revenue business is automatically more profitable, percentage-wise
- Ignoring that margin alone doesn't reveal total available cash

## FAQ
**Q: Is a higher margin always better?**
A: [OPINION] Generally more resilient, but not automatically "better" — some very high-volume, low-margin businesses (like grocery retail) are extremely successful precisely because of their model.

**Q: What's considered a "good" profit margin?**
A: [ESTIMATE] This varies enormously by industry — margin benchmarks that are strong in one industry can be weak in another, so comparisons should generally stay within the same type of business.

## Key Takeaways
- Profit margin is profit as a percentage of revenue
- Two businesses can have equal profit dollars but very different margins
- Low margin means less cushion against rising costs or falling sales
- Margin and raw profit dollars answer different, complementary questions

2. **Concept 1 — Definition:** What does profit margin actually measure?
3. **Follow-up:** How is it calculated?
4. **Concept 2 — Percentage vs. Dollars:** Why might a smaller business with a higher margin be more efficient than a bigger one with a lower margin?
5. **Follow-up:** Does that mean the smaller business made more total money?
6. **Concept 3 — Low Margin Risk:** What does a low margin signal about a business's cushion against rising costs?
7. **Follow-up:** Why is that cushion important?
8. **Concept 4 — Limits of Margin:** What does margin NOT tell you about a business?
9. **Synthesis:** If you had to choose between a high-margin small business and a low-margin large business, what else would you want to know?
10. **Reflection:** Why do margin and raw profit dollars need to be looked at together, not separately?
