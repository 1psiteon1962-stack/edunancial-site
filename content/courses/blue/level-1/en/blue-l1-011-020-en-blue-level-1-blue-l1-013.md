## Main Lesson

### Topic 1: What Overhead Is
Overhead refers to the ongoing costs of running a business that aren't tied directly to producing a specific product or service — rent, insurance, utilities, administrative salaries, and similar recurring expenses.

### Topic 2: Overhead vs. Direct Costs
Direct costs rise and fall with sales volume — more units sold generally means more raw materials purchased. Overhead, by contrast, tends to stay roughly the same whether the business sells one unit or a thousand in a given period.

### Topic 3: Common Examples
[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] Rent for a storefront, a business insurance policy, a bookkeeper's monthly retainer, and basic utilities are classic overhead examples — none of them change simply because sales went up or down last month.

### Topic 4: Why Overhead Exists Regardless of Sales
A business commits to many of these costs (a lease, an insurance policy, a salaried employee) in advance, independent of how any single month's sales perform. That's precisely what makes overhead a fixed, ongoing obligation rather than something that scales automatically with revenue.

---

## Key Definitions
- **Overhead:** Ongoing business costs not directly tied to producing a specific product or service, which persist regardless of sales volume.
- **Direct Cost:** A cost directly tied to producing a specific unit of product or delivering a specific service, which rises and falls with volume.

## Worked Examples
[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A small bakery pays $2,000/month in rent and insurance (overhead) regardless of whether it sells 200 loaves or 2,000 loaves that month, while flour and sugar costs (direct costs) rise and fall directly with how much is baked and sold.

## Common Mistakes
- Confusing overhead with all business costs generally
- Underestimating overhead when pricing products, leading to prices that don't actually cover the full cost of staying in business
- Assuming overhead can be reduced instantly — many overhead commitments (leases, insurance terms) are locked in for a period

## FAQ
**Q: Does overhead ever change?**
A: Yes, but usually not in direct response to a single month's sales — overhead changes when the underlying commitments change (a new lease, a new hire, a canceled service).

**Q: Is overhead always bad?**
A: [OPINION] No — some overhead (like a good location or reliable software) directly supports the ability to sell in the first place. The goal isn't zero overhead, but overhead that's justified by what it enables.

## Key Takeaways
- Overhead is ongoing cost not tied to a specific unit of product or service
- It persists regardless of sales volume, unlike direct costs
- Common examples include rent, insurance, utilities, and administrative salaries
- Overhead should be accounted for when pricing, or prices may not actually cover the cost of staying in business
