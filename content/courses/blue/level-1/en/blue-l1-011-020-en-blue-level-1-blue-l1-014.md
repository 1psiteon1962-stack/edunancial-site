## Executive Summary

A business plan usually has more than one intended reader, and confusing who it's actually for is a common reason plans end up unfocused. This lesson explains what a business plan is meant to do and clarifies its real audiences.

## Main Lesson

### Topic 1: What a Business Plan Actually Does
A business plan is a written document that describes what a business does, how it intends to make money, who its customers are, and how it plans to operate and grow. At its core, it forces the owner to think through the business logically before spending real money on it.

### Topic 2: Audience One — The Owner Themselves
Often overlooked: the first and most important reader of a business plan is the person writing it. Putting the idea into a structured document forces gaps and unanswered questions to the surface before they become expensive mistakes.

### Topic 3: Audience Two — Lenders and Investors
[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A bank evaluating a loan application, or an investor considering funding, reads a business plan to assess whether the business idea is viable and whether the money is likely to be repaid or grow in value. This audience typically wants clear financial projections and evidence of market demand.

### Topic 4: Audience Three — Potential Partners or Key Hires
A business plan can also help convince a potential co-founder, key early employee, or strategic partner that the opportunity is real and worth their time and risk — a different kind of persuasion than convincing a bank.

---

## Key Definitions
- **Business Plan:** A written document describing what a business does, how it makes money, who its customers are, and how it intends to operate and grow.
- **Financial Projections:** Estimated future financial performance (revenue, costs, profit) included in a business plan, typically labeled as ESTIMATE since actual results are uncertain.

## Worked Examples
[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] The same underlying business idea — a mobile pet-grooming service — might need a plan emphasizing personal clarity and cash-flow assumptions when written just for the owner, but emphasizing loan repayment ability and market size when submitted to a bank.

## Common Mistakes
- Writing a single generic plan and assuming it works equally well for every audience
- Treating the business plan purely as a formality for outside parties, ignoring its value for the owner's own thinking
- Including financial projections without clearly labeling them as estimates

## FAQ
**Q: Do I need a business plan if I'm not seeking outside funding?**
A: [OPINION] Many would argue yes — even without an outside audience, the process of writing one out for yourself often reveals gaps in the idea that are cheaper to fix on paper than in practice.

**Q: Should every business plan be a long, formal document?**
A: Not necessarily — the appropriate length and formality often depends on the audience and purpose; a plan written purely for personal clarity can be much shorter than one prepared for a bank or investor.

## Key Takeaways
- A business plan describes what a business does, how it earns money, and how it will grow
- The owner is often the plan's most important, and most overlooked, reader
- Lenders and investors typically want financial viability and repayment/return evidence
- Partners and key hires may need a different kind of persuasion within the same underlying plan

## Practical Exercise
Pick a business idea. Write two or three sentences describing what you'd emphasize differently if writing this plan for yourself versus writing it for a bank loan officer.

## Socratic Teaching Guide
`BLUE-L1-014-SOCRATIC` (Level 1 — "What")

1. **Opening Question:** Who do you think a business plan is written for?
2. **Concept 1 — Purpose:** What does a business plan force an owner to do, structurally?
3. **Follow-up:** Why might that process be valuable even with no outside reader at all?
4. **Concept 2 — Owner as Audience:** How can writing a business plan reveal gaps before money is spent?
5. **Follow-up:** Give an example of a gap that might only become obvious once written down.
6. **Concept 3 — Lenders/Investors:** What does this audience typically want to see in a plan?
7. **Follow-up:** Why does this audience care especially about financial projections?
8. **Concept 4 — Partners/Hires:** How is persuading a potential co-founder different from persuading a bank?
9. **Synthesis:** Could the same underlying plan need different emphasis depending on who's reading it?
10. **Reflection:** If you were writing a plan purely for yourself, what would you focus on that you might skip for a bank?
