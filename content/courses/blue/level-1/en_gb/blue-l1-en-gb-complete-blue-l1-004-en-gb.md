# BLUE-L1-004: Gross Margin vs. Net Margin

## Learning Objectives
1. Define gross margin and net margin.
2. Explain what each margin reveals about a business.
3. Calculate a simple gross margin.

## Core Content
Gross margin measures how much money is left from sales after subtracting only the direct cost of producing what was sold (cost of goods sold). *VERIFIED FACT:* Gross Margin = (Revenue − Cost of Goods Sold) ÷ Revenue, expressed as a percentage.

Net margin goes further, subtracting *all* expenses — including rent, wages, marketing, and taxes — not just the direct cost of goods. *ASSUMPTION:* A business can have a healthy gross margin but a poor net margin if its overheads (rent, staff, marketing) are too high relative to sales — the two figures tell different parts of the story.

## Worked Example
*ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE:* A clothing shop sells a jacket for $100 that cost $40 to manufacture. Gross Margin = ($100 − $40) ÷ $100 = 60%. That looks healthy. But once rent, staff wages, and marketing are factored in, the business might only keep $8 of that $100 as actual profit — a net margin of just 8%, revealing that overheads are eating most of the gross profit.

## Practice Quiz
1. What is the formula for gross margin?
2. What does net margin subtract that gross margin doesn't?
3. True/False: A healthy gross margin always means a healthy net margin.
4. If revenue is $200 and cost of goods sold is $80, what is the gross margin?

## Answer Key
1. (Revenue − Cost of Goods Sold) ÷ Revenue   2. All other expenses, like rent, wages, and marketing   3. False   4. 60%

## Answers and Explanations
1. This is the standard gross margin calculation.
2. Net margin accounts for the full cost of running the business, not just producing the product.
3. A business can have a strong gross margin but weak net margin if overheads are too high.
4. ($200 − $80) ÷ $200 = 0.60 = 60%.

## Key Takeaways
- Gross margin measures profitability after direct production costs only.
- Net margin measures profitability after all expenses.
- A strong gross margin doesn't guarantee a strong net margin — overheads matter too.
