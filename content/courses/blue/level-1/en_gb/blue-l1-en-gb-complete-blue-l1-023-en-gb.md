# BLUE-L1-023: Reading a Cash Flow Statement

## Learning Objectives
1. Identify the basic structure of a cash flow statement.
2. Distinguish the three main categories of cash flow.
3. Explain why this statement matters alongside profit and loss.

## Core Content
*VERIFIED FACT:* A cash flow statement generally organises cash movement into three categories: operating activities (cash from day-to-day business), investing activities (cash spent on or received from assets like equipment), and financing activities (cash from loans, investment, or repayments).

*ASSUMPTION:* This statement is commonly considered essential alongside the profit and loss statement, because it shows actual cash movement rather than accounting profit — a business can look profitable while its cash flow statement reveals it's actually short on available cash.

## Worked Example
*ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE:* A business shows $20,000 in cash from operating activities (sales collected minus expenses paid), spends $15,000 buying new equipment (investing activities), and receives $10,000 from a new loan (financing activities). Net cash change = $20,000 − $15,000 + $10,000 = $15,000, meaning the business's cash position improved by $15,000 over the period, even though the equipment purchase was a large individual outflow.

4. Why is the cash flow statement considered essential alongside profit and loss?

## Answers and Explanations
1. This is the general purpose of a cash flow statement.
2. These are the three standard categories of cash flow.
3. Profit and cash flow can differ significantly due to timing and non-cash items.
4. Cash flow shows what profit and loss alone cannot — actual liquidity.

## Key Takeaways
- A cash flow statement organises cash movement into operating, investing, and financing activities.
- It shows actual cash position, distinct from accounting profit.
- It's essential alongside profit and loss for understanding a business's real financial health.
