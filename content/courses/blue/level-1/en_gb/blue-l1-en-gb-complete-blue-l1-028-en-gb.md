# BLUE-L1-028: Employees vs. Independent Contractors

## Learning Objectives
1. Distinguish an employee from an independent contractor.
2. Identify why the distinction matters legally and financially.
3. Recognise that classification rules vary by jurisdiction.

## Core Content
*REGION-SPECIFIC RULE:* The specific legal tests for distinguishing employees from independent contractors vary significantly by country and can carry serious legal and tax consequences if misapplied; this lesson describes the general concept only.

An **employee** generally works under the direction and control of the business, often with set hours and integrated into the business's operations, with the business responsible for certain taxes and protections. An **independent contractor** generally operates their own business, controls how the work gets done, often works for multiple clients, and is responsible for their own taxes.

## Worked Example
*ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE:* A graphic designer who works exclusively for one company, follows a set schedule, and uses equipment the company provides looks, in substance, like an employee — regardless of what any contract calls them. A designer who takes on projects from several different clients, sets their own hours, and uses their own equipment looks more like an independent contractor.

## Practice Quiz
1. What generally characterises an employee?
2. What generally characterises an independent contractor?
3. True/False: Simply calling someone a "contractor" in a contract determines their legal classification.
4. Why does the distinction between employee and contractor matter?

## Answer Key
1. Working under the business's direction and control, often with set hours   2. Operating their own business, controlling how work is done, often for multiple clients   3. False   4. It carries legal and tax consequences that vary by jurisdiction

## Answers and Explanations
1. This is the general characterisation of an employee.
2. This is the general characterisation of an independent contractor.
3. Classification generally depends on the substance of the working relationship, not just the label used.
4. Misclassification can carry significant legal and tax consequences.

## Key Takeaways
- Employees generally work under the business's direction and control; contractors generally run their own operation.
- The label in a contract doesn't determine classification — the substance of the relationship does.
- Classification rules and consequences vary by jurisdiction and carry real legal weight.
