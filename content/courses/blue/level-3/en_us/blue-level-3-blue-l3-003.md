## Core Content

**EBITDA** stands for Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation, and Amortization. It's a commonly used measure of a business's operating profitability, calculated by starting with net income and adding back interest expense, tax expense, depreciation, and amortization.

Here's the "why" this specific combination is useful for comparing businesses: interest expense depends on how a specific business happens to be financed (its debt structure), tax expense can vary based on the business's specific legal structure and tax situation, and depreciation/amortization are accounting entries that don't reflect actual cash leaving the business (echoing the depreciation discussion from this curriculum's RED track). By adding these back, EBITDA attempts to strip out factors that vary based on financing and accounting choices, leaving a figure that better reflects the business's core, comparable operating performance.

[VERIFIED FACT] This is exactly why EBITDA is commonly used as the basis for valuation multiples (a topic covered in a later lesson) — it allows buyers to compare businesses with different capital structures and tax situations on a more standardized, apples-to-apples basis, even though EBITDA itself deliberately excludes real costs, like interest on debt actually owed and eventual capital expenditures, that any real buyer still needs to account for separately.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] Two similar businesses generate the same underlying operating performance, but Business A has taken on significant debt (with meaningful interest expense) while Business B is debt-free. Comparing their net income alone would make Business B look more profitable, purely due to the financing difference. Comparing their EBITDA instead — adding back Business A's interest expense — reveals that their core operating performance is actually quite similar, allowing a more meaningful comparison between the two businesses independent of how each happens to be financed.

## Practice Quiz

1. What does EBITDA stand for?
2. Why does EBITDA add back interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization?
3. What does EBITDA deliberately leave out that a real buyer still needs to consider?

## Answer Key

1. Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation, and Amortization.
2. To strip out factors that vary based on a business's specific financing structure, tax situation, and accounting choices, leaving a more comparable measure of core operating performance.
3. Actual interest costs on any debt the buyer takes on, and eventual real capital expenditures needed to maintain or grow the business — both real costs not reflected in the EBITDA figure itself.

## Answers and Explanations

1. This is the foundational definition for the lesson.
2. This is the central "why" explaining EBITDA's usefulness as a comparison tool.
3. This is an important caution against treating EBITDA as a complete picture of a business's actual cash economics.

## Key Takeaways
- EBITDA adds back interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization to net income, aiming to reflect core operating performance.
- This makes it useful for comparing businesses with different financing structures and tax situations on a standardized basis.
- EBITDA deliberately excludes real costs like actual interest and capital expenditures, which a buyer must still account for separately.
