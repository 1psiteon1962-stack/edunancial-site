## Learning Objectives

## Core Content

Lesson 2 distinguished between asset value and a business's overall going-concern value, driven by earning power. **Goodwill** is the specific accounting concept used to capture that gap: it's generally calculated as the purchase price paid for a business, minus the fair value of its identifiable net assets (assets minus liabilities).

Here's the "why" this figure matters: goodwill represents the value of everything intangible that contributes to a business's earning power but isn't a specific, separately identifiable asset — brand reputation, customer relationships, an established location, trained employees, or operational systems that would be difficult and costly for a competitor to replicate quickly. [ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A well-known local business with a loyal customer base might sell for well above its physical asset value, with that difference recorded as goodwill, reflecting the real, if intangible, value of that reputation and customer loyalty.

[VERIFIED FACT] Goodwill is recorded as an asset on the buyer's books after the acquisition, and its treatment for tax and accounting purposes (including how or whether it's expensed over time) follows specific accounting and tax rules that vary and should be evaluated with professional guidance for a specific transaction.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A buyer pays $500,000 for a business whose identifiable net assets (equipment, inventory, cash, minus any liabilities) are valued at $300,000. The remaining $200,000 of the purchase price is recorded as goodwill — representing the value the buyer is paying for the business's reputation, established customer relationships, and other intangible factors contributing to its earning power, beyond what the physical assets alone would explain.
