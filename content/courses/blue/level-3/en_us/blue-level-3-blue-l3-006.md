## Core Content

A **Letter of Intent (LOI)** is a document outlining the preliminary terms both parties have agreed to in principle before a full, formal purchase agreement is drafted and signed. It typically covers the proposed price, general deal structure, and key terms both sides expect to include in the final agreement.

Here's the "why" it matters despite generally not being a binding sale commitment: [VERIFIED FACT] most of an LOI's substantive terms — price, structure, and similar business terms — are typically non-binding, meaning either party can still walk away before signing a final agreement, while certain specific provisions (such as confidentiality obligations or an agreed period of exclusivity, during which the seller agrees not to negotiate with other buyers) are commonly structured to be binding, even within an otherwise non-binding LOI.

This structure serves a real purpose: it lets both parties align on the major terms and commit meaningful time and resources to due diligence (Lesson 7) and drafting a final agreement, without either side being legally locked into a deal that might later fall apart based on findings during that process.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A buyer and seller sign an LOI outlining an agreed price and general deal structure, along with a binding 60-day exclusivity period during which the seller agrees not to negotiate with other potential buyers. [ASSUMPTION] During that 60-day period, the buyer's due diligence uncovers a significant, previously undisclosed issue with the business's finances. Because the substantive price and structure terms in the LOI were non-binding, the buyer can renegotiate the price or walk away entirely — while the exclusivity clause itself remains binding and enforceable, having already served its purpose of protecting the buyer's investment of time during the diligence period.

## Practice Quiz

1. What is the general purpose of a Letter of Intent?
2. Which parts of an LOI are typically non-binding, and which parts are commonly binding?
3. Why is this structure useful for both parties, even though most of the LOI isn't legally enforceable?

## Answer Key

1. To outline the preliminary terms both parties have agreed to in principle before drafting a full, formal purchase agreement.
2. Substantive business terms like price and deal structure are typically non-binding; provisions like confidentiality and exclusivity periods are commonly structured to be binding.
3. Because it lets both parties align on major terms and invest in due diligence and contract drafting without being legally locked into a deal that might change based on what diligence reveals.

## Answers and Explanations

## Key Takeaways
- An LOI outlines preliminary, agreed-upon terms before a full purchase agreement is drafted.
- Substantive terms like price are typically non-binding; specific provisions like confidentiality and exclusivity are commonly binding.
- This structure lets both parties invest in due diligence and drafting without being legally locked into the deal's final terms.
