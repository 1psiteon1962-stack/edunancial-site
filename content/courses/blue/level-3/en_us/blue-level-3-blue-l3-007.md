## Core Content

**Due diligence** is the structured process, following the signing of an LOI (Lesson 6), during which a buyer thoroughly investigates a business's financial records, legal standing, contracts, operations, and other relevant details before finalizing a purchase.

Here's the "why" this process exists as a distinct, deliberate step: a seller, understandably, presents their business in the most favorable light during initial negotiations — this doesn't necessarily mean deception, but sellers aren't obligated to proactively volunteer every piece of unfavorable information unless specifically asked or required by law. Due diligence exists to independently verify claims made during negotiations and to actively uncover issues — undisclosed liabilities, pending legal disputes, customer concentration risk (a later lesson), or inconsistencies in financial records — that wouldn't necessarily surface through conversation alone.

[VERIFIED FACT] Due diligence commonly covers financial records (verifying reported revenue and expenses), legal matters (contracts, pending litigation, regulatory compliance), operational details (key employee dependencies, supplier relationships), and any other area specifically relevant to the particular business being acquired.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A buyer negotiating to purchase a business is told the company has strong, stable revenue and no significant legal issues. During due diligence, a careful review of financial records reveals that a meaningful portion of recent revenue came from a single large customer contract that's set to expire soon and isn't likely to renew — information that wasn't specifically volunteered during initial conversations but that materially affects the business's actual future prospects. [ASSUMPTION] This finding, surfaced specifically through the due diligence process, gives the buyer a basis to renegotiate the price or reconsider the deal entirely, connecting to Lesson 6's discussion of how non-binding LOI terms allow for this kind of adjustment.

## Practice Quiz

1. What is due diligence, and when does it typically occur in the acquisition process?
2. Why does due diligence exist as a distinct, deliberate step, rather than relying on the seller's own representations?
3. Name three categories of information due diligence commonly covers.

## Answer Key

1. A structured investigation of a business's financial, legal, and operational details, occurring after an LOI is signed but before a final purchase agreement.
2. Because sellers present their business favorably and aren't necessarily obligated to volunteer every unfavorable detail, so due diligence independently verifies claims and actively uncovers issues that might not otherwise surface.
3. Any three of: financial records, legal matters (contracts, litigation, compliance), operational details (key employees, supplier relationships), or other business-specific factors.

## Answers and Explanations

1. This is the foundational definition and timing for the lesson.
2. This is the central "why" explaining the process's necessity.
3. This checks understanding of the practical scope of a due diligence investigation.

## Key Takeaways
- Due diligence is a structured investigation occurring after an LOI, before a final purchase agreement.
- It exists to independently verify claims and uncover issues that might not otherwise surface during negotiations.
- It commonly covers financial, legal, and operational details specific to the business being acquired.
