## Core Content

An **earnout** is a deal structure where part of the total purchase price is contingent on the business achieving specific performance targets after the sale closes, rather than being paid entirely upfront. It's often used alongside other financing methods, including seller financing (Lesson 1).

Here's the "why" earnouts exist: buyer and seller often disagree about a business's likely future performance — the seller may be optimistic about growth prospects the buyer is more skeptical of, particularly if that growth depends on plans the seller hasn't yet executed. An earnout resolves this specific disagreement by structuring part of the price to only be paid if the optimistic projections actually materialize, letting both parties agree to a deal without either side needing to fully accept the other's view of the future upfront.

[VERIFIED FACT] Earnouts introduce a real risk for the seller, however: because the business's performance during the earnout period is typically now controlled by the buyer (as the new owner), a seller depends on the buyer's decisions and management to actually achieve the targets that determine the seller's final payout — a dynamic worth addressing carefully in how the earnout terms and any relevant seller involvement or oversight are structured.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A seller believes her business is about to significantly grow due to a new product she's been developing, and wants a price reflecting that expected growth. The buyer is more cautious, preferring to pay based on the business's current, proven performance. They agree to an earnout: a base price reflecting current performance, plus an additional payment contingent on the business hitting specific revenue targets over the next two years. [ASSUMPTION] If the new product succeeds as the seller expected, she receives the additional payment; if it doesn't, the buyer isn't stuck having overpaid based on projections that didn't materialize — but the seller now depends on how well the buyer executes and supports that new product after taking ownership.

## Key Takeaways
- An earnout ties part of a business's purchase price to specific future performance targets after closing.
- It helps resolve disagreements between buyer and seller about the business's likely future performance.
- It introduces a real risk for the seller, whose payout depends on the buyer's post-sale decisions and management.
