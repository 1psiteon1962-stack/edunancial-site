## Core Content

A **non-compete clause** in a business acquisition context typically restricts the seller from starting or joining a competing business within a certain geographic area and time period after the sale closes.

Here's the "why" this clause is often essential, connecting directly back to Lesson 5's goodwill discussion: much of what a buyer is paying for — customer relationships, reputation, trained employee relationships — is closely tied to the seller personally, especially in a smaller, closely held business. Without a non-compete, the seller could theoretically pocket the sale proceeds and then immediately start a new, competing business nearby, taking many of the same customers and relationships with them — effectively undermining the very goodwill value the buyer just paid for.

[VERIFIED FACT] The enforceability of non-compete clauses varies significantly by jurisdiction, with some places imposing strict limits on their scope, duration, or enforceability altogether, and rules can differ specifically for non-competes tied to a business sale compared to those in an employment context — this is an area requiring careful, jurisdiction-specific legal drafting rather than a generic template.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A buyer purchases a local service business, paying a premium reflecting the seller's strong reputation and loyal customer base — in other words, significant goodwill value, per Lesson 5. Without a properly drafted and enforceable non-compete clause, the seller could open a new, similar business a few blocks away shortly after the sale, likely drawing away many of the same customers based on the personal relationships and reputation the buyer specifically paid for. [ASSUMPTION] A well-drafted non-compete, reasonable in scope and duration for the relevant jurisdiction, protects the buyer's investment in that goodwill by preventing this specific scenario.
