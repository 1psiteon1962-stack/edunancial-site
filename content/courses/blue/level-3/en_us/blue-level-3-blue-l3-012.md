## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
- Define working capital in a business context
- Explain why a buyer needs working capital beyond the purchase price itself
- Explain what can happen if working capital needs are underestimated

## Core Content

**Working capital** generally refers to the funds a business needs to cover its short-term operating needs — payroll, inventory purchases, rent, and other ongoing expenses — separate from the purchase price paid to acquire the business itself.

Here's the "why" this is easy to underestimate: a buyer focused on negotiating and financing the purchase price can overlook that the business will still need cash to operate from day one of new ownership — payroll doesn't pause for a transition, and suppliers still expect payment on their usual terms. If a buyer has stretched their financing entirely toward the purchase price itself, with nothing left over for these ongoing needs, the business can face a cash crunch immediately after closing, even if the purchase itself was reasonably priced and financed.

[VERIFIED FACT] This is why experienced buyers and their advisors typically build a specific working capital reserve into the overall acquisition financing plan, separate from the purchase price, and why due diligence (Lesson 7) often includes a careful review of the business's typical working capital needs and cycles.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A buyer successfully finances the $500,000 purchase price for a business, combining a bank loan, seller financing, and his own cash down payment, but doesn't set aside additional funds for ongoing operations. [ASSUMPTION] In the weeks immediately following closing, the business faces a normal seasonal dip in incoming cash while payroll and supplier payments continue as usual — without a working capital reserve built into his financing plan, the buyer finds himself scrambling for short-term cash almost immediately after taking ownership, despite having successfully closed on a fairly priced acquisition.

## Practice Quiz

1. What does working capital generally refer to in a business context?
2. Why can a buyer's acquisition financing plan fail even if the purchase price itself is fairly negotiated and financed?
3. What should an acquisition financing plan typically include, beyond just the purchase price?

## Answer Key

1. The funds a business needs to cover short-term operating needs like payroll, inventory, and rent, separate from a purchase price.
2. Because ongoing operating expenses don't pause during a transition, and if all available financing was directed toward the purchase price alone, the business can face an immediate cash crunch after closing.
3. A specific working capital reserve, separate from the purchase price, sized based on the business's typical ongoing operating needs and cash flow cycles.

## Answers and Explanations

1. This is the foundational definition for the lesson.
2. This is the central caution the lesson is built around.
3. This reinforces the practical planning step needed to avoid the scenario described.

## Key Takeaways
- Working capital covers a business's short-term operating needs, separate from the purchase price paid to acquire it.
- Ongoing expenses don't pause during a transition, so a financing plan focused only on the purchase price can leave a new owner short on operating cash.
- A dedicated working capital reserve should be built into the overall acquisition financing plan from the start.
