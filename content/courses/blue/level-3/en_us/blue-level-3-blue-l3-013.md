By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

## Core Content

**Profit** (or net income) is an accounting measure of revenue minus expenses over a period, often recorded when a sale is made or an expense is incurred (under accrual accounting), regardless of exactly when cash actually changes hands. **Cash flow** measures the actual movement of cash in and out of the business during that same period — a genuinely different figure.

Here's the "why" this gap matters so much practically: a business can be profitable on paper — recording a sale as revenue the moment it's made — while the actual cash payment from that sale hasn't arrived yet, perhaps because the customer was extended 60 days to pay (a topic connected to Lesson 14's cash conversion cycle). Meanwhile, the business still needs to pay its own employees and suppliers in real cash, on their own schedules, regardless of whether its customers have paid yet.

[VERIFIED FACT] This gap is a leading, well-documented cause of small business failure — a business can show a profit on its income statement while genuinely running out of the actual cash needed to meet its immediate obligations, a situation often described as being "profitable but cash poor."

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A business completes a large project and records $100,000 in revenue and $70,000 in profit for the month, since its expenses for the project were $30,000. However, the customer isn't required to pay for 60 days under the agreed contract terms. [ASSUMPTION] In the meantime, the business still needs to pay its employees and suppliers in cash immediately — if it doesn't have enough cash reserves or other funding to bridge that 60-day gap, it could face a genuine cash shortage despite showing a strong, real profit for the same period on paper.

## Practice Quiz

1. What is the key difference between profit and cash flow?
2. Why can a profitable business still run into a genuine cash shortage?
3. What does "profitable but cash poor" describe?

## Answer Key

1. Profit is an accounting measure of revenue minus expenses, often recorded independent of exact payment timing; cash flow measures the actual movement of cash in and out of the business.
2. Because revenue can be recorded before the actual cash payment arrives (for example, when customers are given extended payment terms), while the business still needs to pay its own obligations in real cash on a different schedule.
3. A situation where a business shows a genuine profit on its income statement but still runs short of the actual cash needed to meet its immediate obligations.

## Key Takeaways
- Profit and cash flow measure different things — profit is an accounting figure, cash flow tracks actual cash movement.
- Timing differences between recording revenue and receiving actual payment can create a genuine cash shortage even in a profitable business.
- Being "profitable but cash poor" is a well-documented, leading cause of small business failure.
