## Core Content

**Fixed costs** are business expenses that stay roughly the same regardless of how much a business sells (rent, insurance, salaried staff). **Variable costs** change directly with sales volume (materials, per-unit production costs). The **break-even point** is the sales level at which total revenue exactly equals total costs (fixed plus variable) — the point where the business is neither making nor losing money.

Here's the "why" this calculation is a useful, early gut-check on a business idea: it translates an abstract business plan into a concrete, answerable question — how many units, or how much revenue, does this business actually need to generate just to cover its costs, before any profit begins? If that number seems unrealistic given the actual market size or reasonable sales expectations, it's a signal to reconsider the plan's assumptions before investing significant capital.

[VERIFIED FACT] The break-even point in units is generally calculated as fixed costs divided by the difference between the price per unit and the variable cost per unit (this difference is sometimes called the contribution margin per unit) — a formula that directly ties a business's cost structure to a specific, testable sales target.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A business has $50,000 in annual fixed costs, sells its product for $25 per unit, and has variable costs of $15 per unit — a contribution margin of $10 per unit. Its break-even point is $50,000 divided by $10, or 5,000 units per year. [ASSUMPTION] If the business's realistic market research suggests it can reasonably sell only 2,000 units per year in its target market, this break-even calculation reveals a serious problem with the plan as currently structured, before any capital has been committed — prompting a reconsideration of pricing, cost structure, or the overall business model.
