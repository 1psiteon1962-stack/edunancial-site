## Core Content

**Unit economics** examines the direct revenues and costs associated with a single unit of a business's core activity — often a single customer, transaction, or product sold. It answers a specific question: does each individual unit, on its own, generate a profit or a loss, before considering the business's broader fixed costs and overhead?

Here's the "why" this matters more than overall revenue or even total gross margin (Lesson 16) in some cases: if the unit economics for acquiring and serving a single customer are actually negative — meaning it costs more to acquire and serve that customer than the business earns from them — then growing the customer base doesn't solve the business's underlying problem. It accelerates it. More customers, each individually losing money, means larger total losses, not a path toward profitability.

[VERIFIED FACT] This is a distinct concept from overall gross margin: a business can show a positive overall gross margin on paper while still having negative unit economics on a specific customer segment or growth channel, if the true costs of acquiring and serving that segment aren't fully captured in a simple margin calculation.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A subscription business spends $150 to acquire a typical new customer through advertising, and that customer generates only $100 in total revenue over their time as a customer before canceling. Each individual customer, on a unit economics basis, is a $50 loss for the business. [ASSUMPTION] If the business responds to slowing growth by aggressively increasing its marketing spend to acquire even more customers, without first fixing this underlying unit economics problem, it will simply accelerate its losses — each new customer added continues to lose $50, meaning "growth" in this specific case makes the business's financial position worse, not better.

## Practice Quiz

1. What does unit economics examine?
2. Why can growing a business's customer base make its financial position worse, if unit economics are negative?
3. How does unit economics differ from the overall gross margin discussed in Lesson 16?

## Answer Key

1. The direct revenues and costs associated with a single unit of a business's core activity, such as one customer, transaction, or product.
2. Because if each individual customer costs more to acquire and serve than they generate in revenue, adding more customers simply multiplies the loss rather than moving the business toward profitability.
3. Unit economics looks specifically at a single unit's true cost and revenue, which can reveal a loss even when the overall gross margin figure looks acceptable, since gross margin may not fully capture true acquisition and service costs for a specific segment.

## Answers and Explanations

1. This is the foundational definition for the lesson.
2. This is the central, counterintuitive insight the lesson is built around — growth isn't automatically good.
3. This distinguishes unit economics from the related but different concept covered in the previous lesson.

## Key Takeaways
- Unit economics examines whether a single customer, transaction, or product is profitable on its own.
- If unit economics are negative, growing the customer base accelerates losses rather than solving them.
- Unit economics can reveal problems that an overall gross margin calculation might not fully capture.
