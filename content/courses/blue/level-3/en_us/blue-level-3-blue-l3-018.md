## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
- Define customer acquisition cost (CAC) and lifetime value (LTV)
- Explain why these two figures must be compared together, not evaluated separately
- Connect this concept directly to the unit economics discussion in Lesson 17

## Core Content

**Customer Acquisition Cost (CAC)** is the average cost of acquiring a new customer, typically calculated by dividing total sales and marketing spend by the number of new customers acquired over a period. **Lifetime Value (LTV)** estimates the total revenue (or profit) a business expects to earn from a typical customer over the entire span of their relationship with the business.

Here's the "why" these two figures are essentially meaningless evaluated in isolation, directly extending Lesson 17's unit economics concept: a high CAC isn't automatically bad if LTV is proportionally even higher, and a low CAC isn't automatically good if LTV is even lower. What matters is the relationship between them — commonly expressed as an LTV-to-CAC ratio — which reveals whether the cost of acquiring a customer is justified by what that customer is expected to be worth over time.

[VERIFIED FACT] While specific target ratios can vary by industry and business model, a business generally wants LTV to meaningfully exceed CAC — a ratio too close to 1:1 (or below) suggests the unit economics problem described in Lesson 17: customers may not be generating enough value to justify what it costs to acquire them, especially once other operating costs are considered beyond just acquisition.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A business spends $200 to acquire an average new customer (CAC) and estimates that customer will generate $800 in profit over their lifetime as a customer (LTV) — a 4:1 ratio, suggesting a reasonably healthy relationship between acquisition cost and customer value. A different business spends the same $200 CAC but estimates only $220 in lifetime value — a ratio barely above 1:1. [ASSUMPTION] Even though both businesses have the exact same CAC, the second business's unhealthy ratio signals the same fragile unit economics problem described in Lesson 17, while the first business's healthier ratio suggests its growth spending is likely being justified by the value each customer brings.

## Practice Quiz

1. What do CAC and LTV each measure?
2. Why are CAC and LTV essentially meaningless evaluated separately, rather than together?
3. What does a ratio close to 1:1 (or below) between LTV and CAC generally suggest?

## Answer Key

1. CAC measures the average cost of acquiring a new customer; LTV estimates the total revenue or profit a business expects from a typical customer over their entire relationship.
2. Because a given CAC or LTV figure alone doesn't reveal whether acquisition spending is justified — only the relationship between the two shows whether a customer's expected value actually exceeds what it costs to acquire them.
3. It suggests the same fragile unit economics problem described in Lesson 17 — customers may not be generating enough value to justify their acquisition cost, especially once other operating costs are factored in.

## Key Takeaways
- CAC measures the cost of acquiring a customer; LTV estimates that customer's total expected value over time.
- These figures must be compared together — the ratio between them, not either number alone, reveals whether a growth strategy is financially sound.
- A ratio too close to 1:1 signals the same fragile unit economics risk covered in Lesson 17.
