## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
- Explain the fundamental structural difference between debt and equity financing
- Explain why equity financing can become more expensive than debt if a business succeeds greatly
- Explain why debt financing carries a different kind of risk than equity

## Core Content

**Debt financing** involves borrowing money that must be repaid, typically with interest, over a defined period — once repaid, the lender has no further claim on the business's future profits or ownership. **Equity financing** involves selling a percentage of business ownership to investors in exchange for capital — that ownership stake, and the investor's claim on future profits and value, continues indefinitely, for as long as the investor holds their shares.

Here's the "why" equity can end up being the more expensive form of financing specifically when a business succeeds greatly: a debt lender's return is capped — they receive their principal back plus the agreed interest, regardless of how successful the business becomes. An equity investor's return is not capped in the same way — if the business becomes highly successful and valuable, their percentage ownership stake grows in value right along with it, potentially delivering a return far larger than a capped debt repayment ever would have cost.

[VERIFIED FACT] This doesn't mean debt is automatically the better choice — debt carries its own distinct risk (a required, fixed obligation to repay regardless of how the business is actually performing, which can be severe if the business struggles) that equity financing doesn't carry in the same way, since equity investors generally share in downside risk alongside the founder rather than requiring fixed repayment regardless of performance.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A founder needs $200,000 to grow her business. Option A: borrow $200,000 as debt, repaying it over five years with a fixed total interest cost of, say, $50,000 — a known, capped cost regardless of how successful the business becomes. Option B: sell 20% of the business to an investor for the same $200,000. [ASSUMPTION] If the business grows to be worth $10 million within several years, that 20% equity stake is now worth $2 million — a vastly larger "cost" of that original $200,000 in capital than the fixed $50,000 interest cost under the debt option would have been, even though the debt option carried its own repayment risk regardless of the business's success along the way.

## Practice Quiz

1. What is the fundamental structural difference between debt and equity financing?
2. Why can equity financing end up more expensive than debt financing if a business succeeds greatly?
3. What distinct risk does debt financing carry that equity financing generally doesn't?

## Answer Key

1. Debt must be repaid, typically with interest, after which the lender has no further claim; equity involves selling an ongoing ownership stake, with the investor's claim on value and profits continuing indefinitely.
2. Because a debt lender's return is capped at principal plus interest, while an equity investor's return grows proportionally with the business's success, with no similar cap.
3. A required, fixed obligation to repay debt regardless of how the business is actually performing — a risk equity investors generally don't impose, since they typically share in downside risk alongside the founder.

## Answers and Explanations

1. This is the foundational structural distinction for the lesson.
2. This is the central, somewhat counterintuitive insight the lesson is built around.
3. This provides balance, showing debt's own distinct risk rather than presenting equity as simply "worse."

## Key Takeaways
- Debt must be repaid with a capped cost (principal plus interest); equity grants an ongoing ownership stake with no similar cap on investor return.
- If a business succeeds greatly, equity financing can end up costing far more in total value given up than debt would have.
- Debt carries its own distinct risk — a fixed repayment obligation regardless of business performance — that equity financing generally doesn't impose in the same way.
