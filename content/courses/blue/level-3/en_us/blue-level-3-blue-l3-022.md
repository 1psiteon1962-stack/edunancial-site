## Core Content

A **capitalization table (cap table)** is a record of a company's ownership structure — who owns what percentage of the business, and in what form (common shares, preferred shares, options, and similar instruments).

Here's the "why" this needs to be more sophisticated than a simple list of current percentages: a cap table typically needs to account for potential future changes to ownership that haven't happened yet but are already agreed to or reasonably anticipated — outstanding stock options that employees haven't yet exercised, convertible notes that will convert into equity under specific future conditions, and reserved but unissued shares set aside for future employees or investors. This is often referred to as accounting for the business on a "fully diluted" basis — showing what ownership percentages would look like if all these potential future conversions and exercises actually happened.

[VERIFIED FACT] Keeping a cap table accurate and current matters significantly for future fundraising rounds (a topic covered in later lessons): investors evaluating a potential investment need a clear, accurate picture of the company's full ownership structure, including these fully diluted considerations, to understand exactly what percentage of the company their investment would actually represent.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A startup's simple current ownership list shows the founders holding 100% of issued shares. However, the company has also granted stock options to several employees (not yet exercised) representing what would become an additional 10% of ownership if fully exercised, and has a convertible note from an early investor that will convert into another 5% of ownership under specific future conditions. [ASSUMPTION] A cap table maintained on a fully diluted basis would show the founders' effective ownership closer to 85%, not 100% — a more accurate and complete picture that a future investor evaluating the company would need to understand before making an investment decision.

## Key Takeaways
- A cap table records a company's full ownership structure, including different types of equity instruments.
- It needs to account for potential future changes — options, convertible notes, reserved shares — on a fully diluted basis, not just current simple percentages.
- Keeping a cap table accurate is essential for giving future investors a clear, correct picture of the company's ownership structure.
