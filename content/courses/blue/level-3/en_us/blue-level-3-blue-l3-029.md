## Core Content

**Invoice factoring** involves selling a business's outstanding customer invoices (accounts receivable) to a third-party factoring company, in exchange for immediate cash — typically a large majority of the invoice value upfront, with the remainder (minus fees) paid once the factoring company collects payment from the original customer.

Here's the "why" this directly addresses the specific gap described in Lesson 13: rather than waiting the full payment term (30, 60, or 90 days) for a customer to pay an invoice, a business can access most of that cash almost immediately through factoring — directly bridging the gap between recording revenue and actually receiving the corresponding cash.

[VERIFIED FACT] This speed and immediate cash access comes at a real cost: factoring companies charge fees, often structured as a percentage of the invoice value, which can translate into a meaningfully higher effective cost of capital compared to traditional financing options, especially if invoices are factored regularly over an extended period — making it a tool best evaluated against its actual cost, not simply its convenience.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A business has a $50,000 invoice with a customer who won't pay for 60 days, and needs cash sooner to cover payroll and supplier payments. It sells the invoice to a factoring company, receiving $45,000 upfront (90% of the invoice value), with the factoring company collecting the customer's payment directly and remitting the remaining balance, minus its fee, once collected. [ASSUMPTION] This solves the immediate cash flow problem described in Lesson 13, but the effective cost of accessing that cash 60 days early — reflected in the factoring fee — could be substantially higher than the interest rate on a traditional bank loan, if the business had qualified for and had time to arrange one instead.
