Title: Why TAM, SAM, and SOM Aren't the Same Number
Meta Description: Learn what Total Addressable Market, Serviceable Addressable Market, and Serviceable Obtainable Market each measure, and why confusing them overstates a business opportunity.

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
- Define TAM, SAM, and SOM
- Explain why these three figures represent progressively narrower, more realistic market estimates
- Explain why citing only TAM can create a misleadingly optimistic picture of an opportunity

## Core Content

**TAM (Total Addressable Market)** represents the total possible revenue if a product or service captured 100% of its entire relevant market, globally or across its broadest defined category. **SAM (Serviceable Addressable Market)** narrows that down to the portion of the TAM that a specific business could realistically serve, given its actual product, geography, and business model. **SOM (Serviceable Obtainable Market)** narrows further still, to the portion of the SAM a business could realistically capture given actual competition, resources, and go-to-market constraints, typically within a specific timeframe.

Here's the "why" confusing these three figures — particularly citing only the impressively large TAM — can create a misleadingly optimistic picture of a business opportunity: a large TAM says nothing about how much of that market a specific business could actually, realistically reach and capture. A business plan built around "we only need to capture 1% of this huge TAM to succeed" often skips the much harder, more honest work of estimating SAM and SOM specifically, which usually reveals a far more modest, though still potentially viable, realistic opportunity.

[VERIFIED FACT] Investors and experienced business evaluators generally place much more weight on a credible SOM estimate — grounded in realistic assumptions about competition, distribution capability, and go-to-market execution — than on an impressively large TAM figure alone, since TAM alone provides very little information about a specific business's actual realistic prospects.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A startup pitches its opportunity by citing a global TAM of $50 billion for its broad product category. A more careful analysis reveals that, given its specific product, target geography, and business model, its actual SAM is closer to $2 billion — and given realistic competition and its own go-to-market capacity, a credible SOM within its first several years might be closer to $20 million. [ASSUMPTION] The $50 billion TAM figure alone, while technically accurate, would give an investor a wildly inflated sense of the near-term realistic opportunity compared to the much more grounded and useful SOM estimate.

## Key Takeaways
- TAM, SAM, and SOM represent progressively narrower, more realistic estimates of a business opportunity's actual size.
- Citing only an impressive TAM figure can create a misleadingly optimistic picture, skipping the harder work of realistic SAM and SOM analysis.
- A credible SOM estimate, grounded in realistic competitive and execution assumptions, carries far more weight than TAM alone.
