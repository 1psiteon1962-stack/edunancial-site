## Core Content

A **pivot** generally refers to a business making a significant change to its core strategy, product, or target market, typically in response to evidence that the original approach isn't working as hoped. This concept is often discussed in largely strategic or emotional terms — "the market wasn't ready" or "we found a better opportunity" — but here's the "why" it's fundamentally a financial decision as much as a strategic one: a business only has a limited amount of runway (Lesson 19) and capital to test its assumptions before running out of resources entirely.

[VERIFIED FACT] Deciding whether and when to pivot should generally be informed by specific, trackable metrics — unit economics (Lesson 17), CAC-to-LTV ratios (Lesson 18), and remaining runway — rather than solely by general frustration or an untested hunch that "something else" might work better. A founder with three months of runway remaining faces a very different pivot decision, with much less room for further experimentation, than one with eighteen months remaining.

This is why experienced founders and investors often look for a pivot decision to be grounded in a specific, articulated reason tied to concrete evidence gathered from the current approach — echoing this curriculum's RED track discussion of negative leverage requiring a specific, articulated reason rather than a vague hope — not simply frustration or boredom with the current path.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A founder has been testing a specific product approach for eight months, with clear evidence from unit economics analysis (Lesson 17) that customer acquisition costs are unsustainably high relative to lifetime value, and six months of runway remaining. Based on this specific, trackable evidence, she pivots to a different customer segment where early data suggests a much healthier CAC-to-LTV relationship. [ASSUMPTION] This pivot decision, grounded in specific financial evidence and made with meaningful runway still remaining to test the new approach, is very different from a founder who pivots simply out of general frustration with slow initial progress, without first identifying concrete evidence of what specifically isn't working and having runway remaining to test an alternative.

## Practice Quiz

1. What does a business "pivot" generally refer to?
2. Why is deciding to pivot fundamentally a financial decision, connecting to Lesson 19's runway discussion?
3. What should generally ground a pivot decision, rather than general frustration alone?

## Answer Key

1. A significant change to a business's core strategy, product, or target market, typically in response to evidence that the original approach isn't working.
2. Because a business only has limited runway and capital to test its assumptions, and the amount of remaining runway directly shapes how much room exists for further experimentation before a pivot decision must be made.
3. Specific, trackable metrics like unit economics, CAC-to-LTV ratios, and remaining runway, rather than general frustration or an untested hunch.

## Key Takeaways
- A pivot is a significant strategic change made in response to evidence the original approach isn't working.
- This decision is fundamentally financial, since remaining runway directly shapes how much room exists for further experimentation.
- Pivot decisions should be grounded in specific, trackable evidence — unit economics, CAC-to-LTV, runway — not general frustration alone.
