## Core Content

**Intellectual property (IP)** broadly refers to legal protections for creations of the mind — a **patent** generally protects a specific invention or process, a **trademark** generally protects brand identifiers like names and logos, and a **copyright** generally protects original creative works like writing, art, or software code. [VERIFIED FACT] The specific scope, requirements, and duration of protection for each type vary by jurisdiction and are governed by specific intellectual property law.

Here's the "why" this connects directly to the goodwill concept from Lesson 5: a business's brand recognition (protected by trademark), unique inventions or processes (protected by patent), and proprietary creative or technical works (protected by copyright) are often significant, if intangible, contributors to a business's overall value and competitive advantage — value that a buyer in an acquisition, or an investor in a fundraising round, would specifically want to confirm is legally protected and actually owned (not merely used under license) by the business itself.

This is why intellectual property review is commonly a specific component of due diligence (Lesson 7) in both business acquisitions and fundraising: an unprotected or improperly owned key piece of intellectual property represents a real risk to the value being acquired or invested in.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A buyer is evaluating a business whose competitive advantage rests heavily on a unique manufacturing process and a well-recognized brand name. During due diligence, the buyer specifically confirms that the manufacturing process is protected by an existing patent, and the brand name and logo are registered trademarks actually owned by the business itself, not merely used under an informal or unclear arrangement. [ASSUMPTION] If the due diligence process instead revealed that the "unique" process wasn't actually patent-protected, or that the trademark was owned by a departing founder personally rather than the business entity, the buyer would need to seriously reconsider the valuation, since a meaningful part of the assumed goodwill value could be at risk.

## Practice Quiz

1. What do patents, trademarks, and copyrights each generally protect?
2. Why does intellectual property protection connect directly to the goodwill concept from Lesson 5?
3. Why is intellectual property review commonly a specific component of due diligence?

## Answer Key

1. A patent protects a specific invention or process; a trademark protects brand identifiers like names and logos; a copyright protects original creative works like writing, art, or software code.
2. Because brand recognition, proprietary processes, and creative or technical works are often significant, intangible contributors to a business's overall value and competitive advantage — the same kind of value goodwill is meant to capture.
3. Because an unprotected or improperly owned piece of key intellectual property represents a real risk to the value being acquired or invested in, worth specifically confirming before committing capital.

## Key Takeaways
- Patents, trademarks, and copyrights each protect different types of intellectual property.
- This protection contributes directly to a business's goodwill and overall valuation, similar to other intangible value drivers.
- Intellectual property ownership and protection are commonly and specifically reviewed during due diligence in acquisitions and fundraising.
