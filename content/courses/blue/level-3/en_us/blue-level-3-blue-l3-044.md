## Core Content

Two common ways founders and investors eventually achieve liquidity (converting their ownership stake into cash or tradable value) from a successful business are an **acquisition** — the business being purchased by another company — or an **IPO (Initial Public Offering)**, discussed from the buyer's perspective elsewhere in this curriculum's WHITE track, where the business sells shares to the public for the first time.

Here's the "why" these paths differ significantly beyond just the mechanics: an acquisition is typically a private negotiation between the business and a single acquiring company (or a small number of potential acquirers), often resolving more quickly and with less ongoing public disclosure obligation than an IPO. An IPO, by contrast, is a significantly more complex, expensive, and heavily regulated process, resulting in a company that must then operate as a public entity — with extensive ongoing disclosure requirements, quarterly earnings scrutiny, and a much larger and more diffuse set of new shareholders, rather than answering to a single new owner.

[VERIFIED FACT] Historically, acquisitions have been a meaningfully more common exit path than IPOs for most companies that do achieve a successful exit, given the IPO process's higher costs, complexity, and the more limited set of companies that meet the scale and growth profile typically expected of a strong IPO candidate.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A successful, growing company has two potential paths: a larger competitor is interested in acquiring it outright, or the company could pursue an IPO to raise capital and provide liquidity to its founders and early investors by selling shares to the public. [ASSUMPTION] The acquisition path would likely resolve faster, with less ongoing public disclosure and scrutiny, but would mean giving up independent control to the acquiring company. The IPO path would preserve the company's independence but require an expensive, complex process and ongoing life as a public company, complete with the kind of early trading volatility discussed in the WHITE track's IPO lesson — a fundamentally different set of trade-offs than a private acquisition.

## Practice Quiz

1. What is the basic structural difference between an acquisition exit and an IPO exit?
2. Why does an acquisition often resolve more quickly and with less ongoing obligation than an IPO?
3. Why have acquisitions historically been a more common exit path than IPOs for most successful companies?

## Answer Key

1. An acquisition is a private purchase by another company; an IPO is the sale of shares to the public for the first time, resulting in an independent, publicly traded company.
2. Because an acquisition is typically a private negotiation with one or a few parties, while an IPO involves a significantly more complex, expensive, and heavily regulated process resulting in extensive ongoing public disclosure obligations.
3. Because the IPO process's higher costs, complexity, and the more limited set of companies meeting the scale and growth profile typically expected of a strong IPO candidate make it a less accessible path for most companies.

## Answers and Explanations

1. This is the foundational distinction for the lesson.
2. This explains the practical differences in process and ongoing obligation between the two paths.
3. This provides appropriately hedged historical context about the relative frequency of each exit type.

## Key Takeaways
- An acquisition exit is a private purchase by another company; an IPO exit results in an independent, publicly traded company.
- Acquisitions typically resolve faster and with less ongoing disclosure obligation than the significantly more complex IPO process.
- Acquisitions have historically been a more common successful exit path than IPOs for most companies.
