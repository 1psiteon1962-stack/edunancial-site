## Core Content

Lesson 2 established that risk depends significantly on an investor's specific goals and circumstances. **Time horizon** — how long before the invested money is actually needed — is one of the single most important factors shaping what those circumstances actually require.

Here's the "why" a longer time horizon generally supports more risk-taking: an investment with significant short-term volatility needs time to potentially recover from a downturn before the money must actually be withdrawn. An investor with decades before needing the money can generally afford to hold through multiple market cycles, riding out downturns that would be genuinely damaging to someone needing to withdraw funds during that same downturn. [VERIFIED FACT] This is precisely the logic behind the glide path concept covered in this curriculum's WHITE track — gradually shifting toward more conservative investments as a target date approaches, specifically because shrinking time horizon reduces the ability to simply wait out a downturn.

This is why "how much risk should I take" is genuinely incomplete as a question without also specifying "for what goal, and by when do I need this money" — time horizon isn't a minor detail, it's a central input into any reasonable risk assessment.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An investor saving for a goal 25 years away can reasonably accept significant short-term volatility in pursuit of higher long-term growth potential, since she has ample time to recover from any downturns along the way. The same investor, saving for a different goal just one year away, would reasonably choose a much more conservative approach for that specific money, since a downturn in the coming year could directly and immediately damage her ability to meet that near-term goal, with no time remaining to recover before the money is needed. [ASSUMPTION] The same investor, with two different goals on two different timelines, reasonably makes two very different risk decisions — illustrating that time horizon, not personal risk tolerance alone, drives much of what risk level actually makes sense for a specific pool of money.

## Practice Quiz

1. Why does time horizon directly affect how much investment risk makes sense?
2. Why can an investor with decades until a goal generally accept more volatility than one with only a year?
3. How does this lesson connect to the glide path concept covered in the WHITE track?

## Answer Key

1. Because an investment needs time to potentially recover from short-term volatility before the money is actually withdrawn — longer time horizons provide more opportunity for that recovery.
2. Because the longer-horizon investor can ride out multiple market cycles before needing the money, while the shorter-horizon investor has no time remaining to recover from a downturn before it directly affects their goal.
3. A glide path gradually reduces risk as a target date approaches, directly applying this lesson's core principle: shrinking time horizon reduces the ability to absorb volatility, requiring a more conservative approach.

## Key Takeaways
- Time horizon is a central input into how much investment risk reasonably makes sense for a specific pool of money.
- Longer time horizons generally support more risk-taking, since there's more time to recover from short-term volatility.
- The same investor can reasonably make very different risk decisions for different goals with different timelines.
