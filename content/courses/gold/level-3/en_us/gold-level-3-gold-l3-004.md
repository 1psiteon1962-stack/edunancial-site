## Core Content

A stock represents ownership in a business generating profit; a bond represents a loan generating interest; a rental property (as covered in this curriculum's RED track) generates rental income. Each of these is a **productive asset** — its value is fundamentally tied to the income or cash flow it generates over time. **Gold**, by contrast, is generally considered a **non-productive asset**: it doesn't generate income, dividends, interest, or rent simply by being held.

Here's the "why" gold's value proposition is fundamentally different: gold's value comes primarily from being viewed as a store of value — something widely recognized and historically trusted to hold purchasing power over long periods, particularly during times of economic or currency instability — rather than from any income it produces, because it produces none.

[VERIFIED FACT] This means the valuation frameworks used for productive assets — analyzing cash flow, earnings, or rental income, as covered elsewhere in this curriculum — don't apply to gold in the same way. Gold's price is driven by supply and demand dynamics tied to its perceived role as a store of value, a hedge against currency instability, industrial and jewelry demand, and broader investor sentiment, rather than by any calculable stream of future cash flows the way a stock or bond can be analyzed.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An investor analyzing a stock can look at the company's earnings, growth prospects, and cash flow to form a view about whether its price represents good value, using the frameworks covered elsewhere in this curriculum. That same investor analyzing gold has no equivalent earnings or cash flow figure to examine — gold generates nothing while simply being held. [ASSUMPTION] Her decision to hold gold is instead based on a different kind of reasoning entirely: her view of gold's role as a store of value, a potential hedge against currency or economic instability, or simply a form of diversification — a fundamentally different evaluation process than the cash-flow-based analysis used for productive assets.
