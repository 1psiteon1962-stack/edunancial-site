## Core Content

Lesson 4 classified gold as a non-productive asset. **Commodities** more broadly — oil, wheat, industrial metals, and similar raw materials — generally share this same characteristic: owning a barrel of oil or a bushel of wheat doesn't generate any income simply by holding it, unlike owning shares in a company that uses that oil or wheat to produce something.

Here's the "why" commodity prices are driven by an entirely different mechanism than stock or bond prices: since there's no earnings report or interest payment to analyze, commodity prices are driven primarily by the balance of actual supply and demand for that physical resource — weather affecting a crop's yield, geopolitical events affecting oil production or transport, industrial demand shifts, or storage and transportation constraints. [VERIFIED FACT] This means commodity price analysis generally focuses on production levels, inventory data, demand forecasts, and similar supply-and-demand indicators, rather than the earnings-based analysis used for stocks.

This is why commodities are often discussed alongside gold as a distinct category within a broader investment strategy — sharing the non-productive characteristic, but each also carrying its own distinct set of specific price drivers tied to its particular supply and demand dynamics, which can differ significantly from gold's specific drivers.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An investor analyzing an oil company's stock can review the company's production costs, sales revenue, and profit margins to form a view about its value. An investor directly holding oil itself (through a futures contract or similar instrument) has no equivalent earnings figure to analyze — the price of that oil is driven instead by actual global supply and demand factors: production levels from major producers, global economic activity affecting demand, and any disruptions to production or transportation. [ASSUMPTION] These are fundamentally different types of analysis, even though both ultimately relate to the same underlying commodity, illustrating why "investing in oil" through the company's stock and "investing in oil" through the physical commodity itself require genuinely different evaluation approaches.
