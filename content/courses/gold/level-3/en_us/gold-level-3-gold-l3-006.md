## Core Content

Building on Lessons 4 and 5's discussion of non-productive assets: a common but flawed way to evaluate gold is to compare its historical return directly against a growth-oriented investment like a stock index, concluding that gold is a poor investment because its returns have often lagged over long periods.

Here's the "why" this comparison misses the point: gold and a growth-oriented stock investment are generally intended to serve different roles within a portfolio, not to compete head-to-head for the same purpose. A **growth asset** aims primarily to build wealth over time through capital appreciation and, in many cases, income. A **store-of-value asset** like gold aims primarily to preserve purchasing power and provide potential stability, particularly during periods when growth assets might be under stress — not to maximize long-term growth.

[VERIFIED FACT] Evaluating gold purely on a growth-return basis, and finding it lacking compared to stocks over long historical periods, is comparing two assets against a standard only one of them is actually trying to meet. This is why a thoughtful portfolio might reasonably hold both: growth assets to build wealth over time, and some allocation to a store-of-value asset for the different, complementary role it's intended to serve, rather than treating the decision as an either-or choice between competing options for the same job.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An investor compares gold's average annual return over several decades to a stock index's average annual return over the same period, and concludes gold was a poor investment choice because its growth lagged significantly. [ASSUMPTION] This comparison overlooks that gold wasn't held in her portfolio to compete with stocks for growth — it was held specifically for its potential to preserve value and provide stability during periods when her stock holdings might be under significant stress, a role a pure growth-return comparison doesn't capture or credit at all.

## Practice Quiz

1. What is the different purpose served by a store-of-value asset compared to a growth asset?
2. Why does comparing gold's return directly against a stock index's return miss the point?

## Answer Key

1. A growth asset aims primarily to build wealth through appreciation and income; a store-of-value asset aims primarily to preserve purchasing power and provide stability, particularly during stress periods.
2. Because it evaluates gold against a growth standard it was never intended to meet, rather than against the different, complementary role it's actually meant to serve in a portfolio.
3. Because they serve different, complementary purposes — growth assets to build wealth over time, and a store-of-value allocation for stability and preservation during periods when growth assets might struggle.

## Key Takeaways
- Store-of-value assets like gold and growth assets like stocks generally serve different portfolio purposes.
- Comparing gold's return directly against a stock index's growth return evaluates it against a standard it was never intended to meet.
- A thoughtful portfolio might reasonably hold both, valuing each for the different, complementary role it plays.
