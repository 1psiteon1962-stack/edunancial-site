## Core Content

Lesson 6 established that store-of-value and growth assets serve different portfolio purposes. This lesson sharpens that distinction around a specific goal: **inflation hedging** — protecting purchasing power against rising prices — is not the same goal as **growth investing** — building wealth through appreciation over time, though the two are often discussed together, and confused with each other.

Here's the "why" this distinction matters: an asset can reasonably serve as an inflation hedge, tending to hold or increase its value as prices rise generally, while still delivering modest or unremarkable long-term growth compared to other investment options. Gold is frequently discussed in exactly this framing — [VERIFIED FACT] historically sometimes cited as a potential inflation hedge over some periods, while also historically underperforming growth-oriented assets like stocks over many long-term periods — both of these observations can be true at once, because they're answering different questions about the same asset.

This connects directly to this curriculum's WHITE track discussion of how different assets respond differently to inflation — that broader lesson applies here specifically to gold and precious metals, reinforcing that "does this protect against inflation" and "does this build long-term wealth" are separate questions, each requiring its own separate evaluation.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An investor holding gold specifically as a partial inflation hedge sees its value hold up reasonably well during a period of rising prices, fulfilling the specific role she assigned it in her portfolio. [ASSUMPTION] Over that same multi-decade period, her separate growth-oriented stock holdings deliver meaningfully higher total returns than her gold holdings did — this doesn't mean her gold allocation "failed," since it wasn't chosen to compete with her stock holdings for growth; it was chosen for a different, specific purpose that it may have served reasonably well, judged on its own terms rather than against a growth benchmark it was never meant to beat.

## Answer Key

1. Growth investing aims to build wealth through appreciation over time; inflation hedging aims to protect purchasing power against rising prices — these are different goals that can lead to different asset choices.
2. Because these are separate questions about the same asset — an asset's ability to hold value during inflation doesn't determine, and isn't the same as, its long-term growth potential compared to other investments.

## Key Takeaways
- Inflation hedging and growth investing are different goals, even though they're often discussed together.
- An asset can reasonably serve as an inflation hedge while still underperforming growth-oriented investments over the long term.
- These are separate evaluation questions, and an asset shouldn't be judged as a "failure" against a goal it was never intended to meet.
