## Core Content

Lessons 4 through 7 built toward a central point: different types of assets need to be evaluated using different tools, because they generate value in fundamentally different ways. This lesson makes that principle explicit and general.

Here's the "why" a single, universal valuation framework doesn't exist: a stock's value is reasonably tied to its earnings and growth prospects (frameworks like price-to-earnings ratios, as covered in this curriculum's WHITE track). A bond's value is tied to its fixed payments and prevailing interest rates. A rental property's value connects to its rental income and cap rate (as covered in the RED track). Gold and many commodities, having no cash flow, require an entirely different lens — supply, demand, and their role as a store of value, as Lessons 4 and 5 established.

[VERIFIED FACT] Applying a cash-flow-based framework to gold (asking "what's gold's price-to-earnings ratio") produces a nonsensical result, since gold has no earnings. Applying a pure supply-and-demand framework to a stable, profitable business while ignoring its actual earnings would similarly miss the most relevant information for that specific asset type. This is why the first step in evaluating any investment is correctly identifying which category it belongs to and which framework actually applies — a step that's easy to skip but essential to getting a meaningful answer.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An investor new to alternative assets tries to apply a price-to-earnings framework, learned from stock analysis, to evaluate whether gold is currently "cheap" or "expensive." [ASSUMPTION] This attempt fails immediately, since gold has no earnings to calculate a ratio against — the correct framework instead involves looking at gold's historical price behavior relative to inflation, currency conditions, and broader supply and demand factors, an entirely different kind of analysis than the one that works well for evaluating a stock.

2. Any two of: price-to-earnings-type analysis for stocks, cap rate analysis for rental property, fixed-payment and interest-rate analysis for bonds, supply-and-demand analysis for gold and commodities.

## Key Takeaways
- Different asset classes require genuinely different valuation frameworks, since they generate value in fundamentally different ways.
- Applying the wrong framework to an asset — like a cash-flow analysis to gold — produces meaningless or misleading results.
- Correctly identifying an asset's category is the essential first step before applying any valuation framework.
