## Core Content

This curriculum's WHITE track introduced liquidity as it applies to comparing individual stocks. Here's the same concept applied more broadly across asset classes: **liquidity** — how quickly and easily an asset can be converted to cash without significantly affecting its price — varies dramatically not just between individual securities, but across entire categories of investment.

A publicly traded stock can typically be sold within seconds during market hours. Physical gold can generally be sold relatively quickly through established dealers, though with some friction and cost. A private business ownership stake, a piece of real estate, or an interest in a private investment fund can take months, or in some cases years, to sell, if a buyer can be found at all.

[VERIFIED FACT] Here's the "why" this matters as a genuine risk, not just a minor inconvenience: an investor who needs access to cash unexpectedly, but holds primarily illiquid assets, may be forced to sell at an unfavorable price, accept unfavorable terms, or simply be unable to access needed funds in a timely manner at all — a real risk distinct from the investment's underlying quality or expected return.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An investor holds a diversified portfolio, but a significant portion is tied up in a private business investment with no active market and no ability to sell quickly. [ASSUMPTION] If she experiences an unexpected need for cash, her liquid holdings (stocks, gold) can be accessed relatively quickly, but her illiquid private investment cannot — she might need to wait an extended period, accept an unfavorable price from a limited pool of interested buyers, or simply be unable to access that portion of her wealth at all when she needs it, illustrating why liquidity is a real risk factor to consider alongside an investment's expected return and other characteristics.

## Key Takeaways
- Liquidity varies dramatically across investment types, from highly liquid public securities to highly illiquid private investments.
- This difference is a genuine risk factor, separate from an investment's expected return or underlying quality.
- An investor's overall liquidity needs should factor into how much of a portfolio is reasonably allocated to illiquid investments.
