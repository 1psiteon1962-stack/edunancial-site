## Core Content

**Private equity** funds generally acquire ownership stakes in established, often mature private companies, aiming to improve their operations and value before eventually selling. **Venture capital** funds, discussed elsewhere in this curriculum's BLUE track from a founder's perspective, generally invest in early-stage companies with high growth potential, accepting a higher failure rate among individual investments in exchange for the potential of outsized returns from the successes.

Here's the "why" both fund types typically require investors to commit capital for an extended period, often a decade or more: [VERIFIED FACT] both strategies depend on genuine business transformation or growth happening over real time — improving an acquired company's operations, or growing an early-stage company into a mature, successful business — processes that simply can't be rushed or reliably timed to match an investor's individual, potentially shorter-term liquidity needs.

This directly illustrates Lesson 11's concept: the multi-year lock-up isn't an arbitrary inconvenience — it's structurally necessary for the fund to pursue exactly the kind of long-term value creation these strategies are built around, without the risk of being forced into premature exits due to investor withdrawal demands.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An investor commits capital to a private equity fund with an expected multi-year investment horizon. During that period, the fund uses her committed capital, along with other investors' commitments, to acquire and improve several companies, planning to sell them once their value has been meaningfully increased. [ASSUMPTION] If she needed to withdraw her capital early, before the fund's planned exit timeline, she would generally have very limited options to do so — the fund's structure specifically assumes and requires that committed capital, illustrating why this type of investment is appropriate only for capital an investor can genuinely commit for the full expected period without needing earlier access.

## Practice Quiz

1. What is the general difference between what private equity and venture capital funds typically invest in?
2. Why do both fund types typically require investors to commit capital for an extended, multi-year period?
3. How does this lesson directly illustrate the concept introduced in Lesson 11?

## Answer Key

1. Private equity generally acquires stakes in established, mature private companies; venture capital generally invests in early-stage, high-growth-potential companies.
2. Because both strategies depend on genuine business transformation or growth happening over real time, a process that can't be rushed or reliably matched to shorter-term investor liquidity needs.
3. The multi-year lock-up structurally enables the long-term value creation strategy these funds pursue, directly illustrating why illiquidity and return potential are connected, not incidental, as Lesson 11 established.

## Answers and Explanations

1. This is the foundational distinction for the lesson.
2. This is the central "why" explaining the necessity of the lock-up period.

## Key Takeaways
- Private equity generally targets established companies; venture capital generally targets early-stage, high-growth companies.
- Both typically require multi-year capital commitments, since their underlying strategies depend on real time for business transformation or growth.
- This illustrates the direct connection between illiquidity and return potential introduced in Lesson 11.
