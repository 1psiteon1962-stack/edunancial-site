## Core Content

This curriculum's BLUE track covers due diligence in the context of a business acquisition — a thorough investigation before committing capital to a deal that can't be easily unwound. Here's the "why" that same intensity of scrutiny applies broadly to illiquid investments generally, connecting to Lesson 10's liquidity discussion: with a publicly traded stock, a mistake can often be corrected relatively easily and cheaply — if new information emerges or the original thesis proves wrong, the position can typically be sold quickly. With an illiquid investment — a private fund, a direct business stake, or a similar vehicle — no such easy correction exists once capital is committed.

[VERIFIED FACT] This means the due diligence process before committing capital to an illiquid investment needs to be considerably more thorough than for a liquid one, since it may be the primary opportunity to catch a problem before it's too late to meaningfully act on it. For a fund investment specifically, this includes not just evaluating the fund's stated investment strategy, but also the track record, reputation, and specific terms of the fund manager — a topic explored in more depth in a later lesson.

This is why "I'll just sell if it doesn't work out" is not an available safety net for illiquid investments the way it often is for liquid ones — the decision to commit capital needs to be right, or at least carefully considered, largely upfront.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An investor considering a publicly traded stock can start with a modest position, monitor new information as it emerges, and adjust or exit relatively quickly if her original thesis turns out to be wrong. An investor considering a multi-year private equity fund commitment doesn't have this same flexibility — [ASSUMPTION] once capital is committed, she's generally locked in regardless of new information that emerges later, meaning her upfront due diligence on the fund's strategy, manager, and terms needs to be considerably more thorough than the initial research she might do before a smaller, easily adjustable stock purchase.

2. Because the initial due diligence process may be the primary opportunity to catch a problem before it's too late to meaningfully act on it, given the lack of an easy exit later.
3. This applies the same intensity of upfront scrutiny that a business acquisition's due diligence process (BLUE track) requires, generalized to illiquid investments broadly, given the shared characteristic of no easy exit once capital is committed.

## Key Takeaways
- Illiquid investments require considerably more thorough upfront due diligence than liquid ones, since mistakes are much harder to correct after the fact.
- For fund investments specifically, this includes evaluating the fund manager's track record and terms, not just the stated strategy.
- "I'll just sell if it doesn't work out" is generally not an available option for illiquid investments, raising the stakes of the initial decision.
