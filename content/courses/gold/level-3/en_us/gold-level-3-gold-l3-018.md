## Core Content

This curriculum's WHITE track introduced rebalancing as a mechanical way to build "buy low, sell high" discipline into a portfolio. Here's the "why" this discipline is genuinely hardest to maintain, and simultaneously most valuable, during periods of significant market volatility.

During a sharp downturn, rebalancing would generally require selling relatively better-performing assets (perhaps bonds or gold, per Lesson 14's differential response discussion) to buy more of the declining asset (perhaps stocks) — a mechanical action that runs directly against the loss aversion and recency bias covered elsewhere in this curriculum's WHITE track. It can feel deeply uncomfortable to buy more of something that's actively falling in value, even though this is precisely the mechanical "buy low" behavior rebalancing is designed to enforce.

[VERIFIED FACT] This is exactly why maintaining rebalancing discipline specifically during volatile periods — not just during calm, comfortable markets — is where the strategy's real value tends to be tested and, if followed through, potentially realized. Abandoning the plan specifically when it feels hardest to follow defeats much of its intended purpose.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An investor's rebalancing plan calls for selling some of her bond holdings, which have held up relatively well, to buy more stocks, which have declined sharply during a market downturn — exactly the kind of mechanical "buy low" action the rebalancing strategy is designed to produce. [ASSUMPTION] Following through on this plan feels deeply uncomfortable, since it means buying more of an asset that's actively falling in value, directly conflicting with the emotional pull described by loss aversion and recency bias. An investor who abandons her rebalancing plan specifically during this uncomfortable period, waiting for things to "feel safer" before rebalancing, misses exactly the moment the strategy was designed to take advantage of.
