## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
- Explain why evaluating a fund manager requires a different kind of due diligence than evaluating a company directly

- Explain why past performance alone is an incomplete basis for this evaluation

## Core Content

Lesson 13 established that illiquid investments require especially thorough upfront due diligence. Here's the "why" that diligence looks meaningfully different when the investment is a fund managed by someone else, rather than a direct stake in a specific company or property: an investor in a fund isn't just evaluating the underlying assets the fund might eventually hold — many of which may not even be selected yet at the time of the initial investment decision — they're primarily evaluating the manager's judgment, process, and track record for making those future selections well.

[VERIFIED FACT] Relevant factors for evaluating a fund manager generally include their historical track record (though, echoing the WHITE track's caution about past performance, this doesn't guarantee future results), their specific investment process and stated strategy, how their own incentives are structured relative to investor outcomes, the strength and experience of their broader team, and their transparency and communication with investors over time.

This is why fund manager due diligence resembles, in some ways, the due diligence one might apply to a key business partner or key employee (echoing the key person risk concept from this curriculum's BLUE track) more than the cash-flow analysis applied to evaluating a specific business directly — it's fundamentally an evaluation of a person's or team's ongoing judgment and process, not a fixed, existing set of assets.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An investor considering a private investment fund reviews the manager's historical track record across previous funds, but doesn't stop there — she also examines the manager's stated investment process, how the manager's own compensation is structured relative to investor returns (aligning or potentially misaligning incentives), the depth and experience of the broader team beyond just the lead manager, and how transparently the manager has historically communicated with investors during both successful and challenging periods. [ASSUMPTION] This more holistic evaluation gives her a fuller picture of the manager's likely future judgment and process than relying on the historical track record figure alone would provide.

## Practice Quiz

1. Why does evaluating a fund manager require a different kind of due diligence than evaluating a specific company or property directly?
2. Name two factors, beyond historical track record, relevant to evaluating a fund manager.
3. Why is fund manager due diligence compared to evaluating a key business partner or employee?

## Answer Key

1. Because an investor is primarily evaluating the manager's judgment and process for making future investment selections, many of which haven't been made yet, rather than a fixed, existing set of assets.
2. Any two of: investment process and stated strategy, incentive structure relative to investor outcomes, team strength and experience, transparency and communication with investors.
3. Because it's fundamentally an evaluation of a person's or team's ongoing judgment and process, similar to the kind of assessment applied to a key person or partner whose decisions will shape future outcomes.

## Key Takeaways
- Evaluating a fund manager requires assessing judgment, process, and track record, not just analyzing a fixed set of existing assets.
- Relevant factors include track record, investment process, incentive alignment, team strength, and transparency.
- This type of due diligence resembles evaluating a key person or partner more than analyzing a company's cash flow directly.
