## Core Content

**Survivorship bias** occurs when an evaluation only considers the entities that "survived" to be counted, systematically excluding the ones that failed or disappeared along the way — creating a distorted, overly optimistic picture of the group as a whole.

Here's the "why" this specifically distorts how investment strategies and fund categories get evaluated: [VERIFIED FACT] funds that perform poorly are more likely to shut down, merge into other funds, or simply stop reporting their results — meaning that databases of historical fund performance, especially older or less rigorously maintained ones, can systematically exclude many of the poor performers, leaving behind a track record that looks better, on average, than the full universe of funds (including all the ones that failed) actually performed.

This matters especially for evaluating alternative investment categories covered throughout this track, particularly those with historically less standardized or less rigorously enforced reporting requirements than publicly traded securities, where survivorship bias in available historical data can be a genuine, significant distortion — a category's apparent historical success rate might look considerably better than its true, full historical success rate actually was.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An investor researching a specific category of alternative fund reviews historical performance data showing an impressive average track record. [ASSUMPTION] If that database only includes funds that are still operating and actively reporting today, and doesn't include the funds from that same category that failed, shut down, or simply stopped reporting years ago, the impressive average she's seeing reflects only the survivors — the true historical performance across all funds that ever existed in that category, including the failures, was likely meaningfully worse than the survivorship-biased data suggests.
