## Core Content

This curriculum's WHITE track introduced the "behavior gap" — the difference between an investment's own returns and what the average investor in it actually earns, largely attributed to poorly timed emotional decisions. Here's the "why" this lesson reframes managing that gap as a genuine, developable skill, rather than something certain investors simply happen to have or lack: [VERIFIED FACT] behavioral finance research generally treats the tendency toward biases like loss aversion and recency bias (both covered in the WHITE track) as broadly shared, near-universal human tendencies, not a flaw specific to a subset of "undisciplined" investors — meaning managing these tendencies is a skill that can be deliberately practiced and improved, not an innate trait some people simply have and others don't.

Specific practices that can help develop this skill include the written investment plan (covered in the WHITE track), pre-committing to a rebalancing schedule (Lesson 18), understanding the historical patterns and reasoning covered throughout this track well enough to recognize an emotional reaction as it's happening, and deliberately limiting how frequently investment account balances are checked during volatile periods, since more frequent observation of short-term volatility has been associated with increased emotional reactivity in some research.

This is why experienced, disciplined investors aren't necessarily people who don't feel the same emotional pulls as everyone else — they're often people who have specifically developed and practiced tools and habits that help manage those pulls effectively.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An investor experiences the same instinctive fear during a market downturn that any investor might feel. [ASSUMPTION] Rather than treating this feeling as evidence that she's simply "bad at investing emotionally," she recognizes it as the same widely shared human tendency covered throughout this curriculum, and relies on tools she's deliberately put in place — her written investment plan, her pre-committed rebalancing schedule, and a conscious decision to check her account less frequently during this volatile period — to help her act consistently with her long-term plan despite the very real emotional pull she's experiencing.
