## Core Content

Earlier lessons in this track focused on evaluating gold and precious metals as an asset class — their valuation characteristics, role in a portfolio, and behavior during different economic conditions. This lesson addresses a genuinely separate question: verifying the legitimacy of the specific dealer or platform through which a physical purchase is actually made.

Here's the "why" this deserves its own specific attention: [VERIFIED FACT] the precious metals market has historically been associated with certain fraud patterns, including counterfeit products, dealers who fail to actually deliver purchased metal, high-pressure sales tactics pushing overpriced or unnecessary "collector" or "rare" coins at prices far above their actual metal content value, and outright investment scams using precious metals as a superficial cover story.

This is a genuinely different kind of due diligence than evaluating gold's investment merits as an asset class — it's about verifying the specific counterparty's legitimacy, reputation, and actual ability and intention to deliver what's being purchased, similar in spirit to the general fraud-awareness principle relevant across many types of transactions, but with specific patterns worth knowing for this particular market.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An investor decides, based on the reasoning covered throughout this track, that an allocation to physical gold makes sense for her portfolio. Before making a purchase, she researches the specific dealer's reputation, verifies appropriate business credentials and any relevant industry association memberships, compares pricing against transparent, verifiable market benchmarks (avoiding a seller pushing "rare" or "collector" premiums far above the metal's actual content value), and confirms clear, verifiable delivery or storage terms before committing funds. [ASSUMPTION] This due diligence on the specific dealer is entirely separate from, and just as important as, her earlier analysis of gold as an asset class — a sound investment thesis about gold itself doesn't protect against a fraudulent or illegitimate specific transaction.
