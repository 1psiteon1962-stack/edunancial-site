## Core Content

An **investment committee** structure requires significant investment decisions to be reviewed and approved by a group, rather than relying entirely on a single individual's judgment, commonly used by institutional investors, funds, and other organizations managing significant capital.

Here's the "why" this structure tends to improve decision quality, connecting directly to several biases covered earlier in this track: a group discussion naturally surfaces different perspectives and can help counter the overconfidence bias (Lesson 34) or unfalsifiable reasoning (Lesson 37) that a single individual, convinced of their own thesis, might not catch in their own thinking. [VERIFIED FACT] Requiring an investment thesis to be articulated clearly enough to be understood and challenged by others also naturally reinforces the falsifiability discipline discussed in Lesson 37 — a thesis that can't be clearly explained and defended to a group is often a thesis that hasn't been fully, honestly thought through.

This is why this principle applies beyond formal institutional committees: an individual investor can apply a version of the same discipline by discussing significant investment decisions with a trusted, knowledgeable friend, advisor, or professional (echoing the "business is a team sport" principle covered elsewhere in this curriculum) before committing significant capital, rather than relying entirely on their own, potentially biased, individual judgment.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An institutional fund requires any significant investment decision to be presented to and approved by a committee, rather than allowing any single manager to unilaterally commit capital. This process naturally surfaces questions and challenges the original proponent might not have considered, testing the thesis's falsifiability and countering any individual overconfidence before capital is actually committed. [ASSUMPTION] An individual investor considering a significant, unfamiliar investment could apply a version of this same discipline by discussing the decision with a trusted, knowledgeable advisor before committing, gaining a similar check on her own individual judgment and potential biases.
