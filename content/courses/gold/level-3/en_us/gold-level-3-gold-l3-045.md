## Core Content

This curriculum's WHITE track covered sector concentration — many stocks that look diversified by count but share exposure to the same industry-specific risks. This lesson extends that same underlying principle one level further, connecting directly to Lesson 16's correlation discussion: a portfolio can be concentrated in a shared **strategy** or underlying **risk factor**, even while appearing genuinely diversified across different asset classes and vehicle types.

Here's the "why" this is a subtler, easier-to-miss risk than simple holding-count or sector concentration: [ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] an investor might hold a stock portfolio, a real estate investment, and an alternative fund that all happen to depend heavily on continued low interest rates or continued strong economic growth to perform well — different asset classes and vehicle types on the surface, but all sharing the same underlying vulnerability to the same specific risk factor. If that shared risk factor materializes unfavorably, all three holdings could decline together, despite appearing well-diversified by conventional measures.

This is why a genuinely thorough portfolio review looks beyond simply counting asset classes and holdings, and asks a deeper question: what specific underlying economic conditions or risk factors does this portfolio, taken as a whole, actually depend on to perform well — and is that dependence more concentrated than the surface-level diversification suggests?

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An investor holds growth stocks, a leveraged real estate investment, and a venture capital fund — three different asset classes and vehicle types, appearing well-diversified. [ASSUMPTION] On closer inspection, all three holdings depend heavily on continued low interest rates and strong economic growth to perform well: growth stocks' valuations are sensitive to rates, leveraged real estate is directly affected by financing costs, and venture-backed companies often depend on continued access to growth capital that becomes harder to raise in a higher-rate environment. If interest rates rise significantly and economic growth slows, all three holdings could decline together, despite their apparent surface-level diversification — a hidden concentration risk in shared strategy and risk factor, not holdings.
