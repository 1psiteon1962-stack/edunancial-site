## Core Content

This curriculum's RED track established that exit strategy should be decided before financing is chosen for a real estate purchase, since different exit plans favor different financing structures. Here's the "why" this same principle applies with equal, and arguably greater, force to the investments covered throughout this GOLD track: for illiquid alternative investments especially (Lessons 10-13), the eventual path to accessing that capital again — a fund's scheduled distribution timeline, a planned business sale, or simply patiently waiting for a specific market condition — needs to be understood and planned for before capital is committed, not figured out afterward.

[VERIFIED FACT] For a gold or precious metals allocation, exit strategy is more straightforward given its relative liquidity (Lesson 10), but still worth considering: is this allocation meant to be held indefinitely as a long-term store-of-value component, or is there a specific condition (a specific personal goal, or a change in the currency-debasement concerns discussed in Lesson 30) under which it would be sold? For illiquid alternative investments, this planning is considerably more consequential, given the genuine difficulty of exiting these positions on short notice, as covered throughout this track.

This is why the closing synthesis of this track echoes the RED track's closing lesson on this exact topic: nearly every concept covered throughout this GOLD track — time horizon (Lesson 3), liquidity needs (Lesson 10), illiquidity premium expectations (Lesson 26) — ultimately connects back to having a clear, deliberate plan for how and when a given investment will eventually be exited, decided before capital is committed rather than figured out under pressure later.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An investor commits capital to a multi-year private equity fund without having clearly thought through when she might need that capital back, or how the fund's specific distribution timeline aligns with her own personal financial goals. [ASSUMPTION] If a personal need for that capital arises before the fund's planned distribution timeline, she has very limited options, having not planned her exit strategy before committing — a very different, more difficult situation than an investor who specifically confirmed the fund's expected timeline aligned with her own needs before making the initial commitment, echoing exactly the exit-strategy-first principle established for real estate financing elsewhere in this curriculum.
