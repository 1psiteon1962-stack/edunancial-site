## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
- Distinguish between payroll taxes and income taxes
- Explain why payroll taxes are generally tied to specific, designated programs
- Explain why payroll taxes typically apply at a flat rate up to a certain income level, unlike progressive income tax

## Core Content

**Income tax** funds general government operations and is calculated using the progressive bracket structure covered in Lessons 1 through 3. **Payroll taxes** (in the U.S. context, commonly including Social Security and Medicare taxes) are a separate category, typically calculated as a set percentage of wages and specifically designated to fund particular programs.

Here's the "why" these are treated as distinct: payroll taxes are generally structured to directly fund the specific programs they're named for — Social Security and Medicare in the U.S. context — rather than flowing into general government revenue the way income tax does. [VERIFIED FACT] This is also why Social Security tax, in the U.S. context, typically applies only up to a specific wage base limit each year (an amount that's periodically adjusted) — income above that limit isn't subject to the Social Security portion, a structural feature quite different from the unlimited, ever-increasing brackets of the progressive income tax system.

This distinction matters for understanding a full paycheck: income tax withholding and payroll tax withholding are calculated separately, using entirely different rules, and both appear as distinct deductions from gross pay.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An employee's paycheck shows separate deductions: federal income tax withholding, calculated based on progressive brackets and the employee's specific tax situation, and separately, Social Security and Medicare tax withholding, each calculated as a flat percentage of wages. [ASSUMPTION] If this employee's annual wages exceed the Social Security wage base limit for that year, Social Security tax withholding would stop being deducted from paychecks once that limit is reached for the year, even though income tax withholding continues on all remaining wages, illustrating the structurally different treatment of these two tax categories.

## Practice Quiz

1. What is the key structural difference between how income tax and payroll tax are calculated?
2. Why are payroll taxes generally described as designated for specific programs, unlike general income tax?
3. What happens to Social Security tax withholding once wages exceed the annual wage base limit, in the U.S. context?

## Answer Key

1. Income tax uses a progressive bracket structure with rates increasing at higher income levels; payroll taxes are typically calculated as a flat percentage of wages, often only up to a specific limit.
2. Because payroll taxes are structured to directly fund specific, named programs like Social Security and Medicare, rather than flowing into general government revenue.
3. Withholding for that specific tax stops once the wage base limit is reached for the year, since wages above that limit aren't subject to that particular payroll tax.

## Key Takeaways
- Payroll taxes are typically calculated as a flat percentage of wages and designated for specific programs, unlike progressive general income tax.
- In the U.S. context, Social Security tax typically applies only up to an annual wage base limit, a structural feature distinct from income tax's unlimited brackets.
- Income tax and payroll tax withholding are calculated separately, using entirely different rules, and both appear on a paycheck.
