## Core Content

A **tax deduction** reduces the amount of income subject to tax (taxable income) before the tax calculation is applied. A **tax credit** reduces the actual tax owed directly, dollar for dollar, after the tax calculation has already been performed.

Here's the "why" a credit is generally more valuable than a deduction of the identical dollar amount: a deduction's actual value depends on the taxpayer's marginal tax rate (Lesson 1) — a $1,000 deduction only reduces actual tax owed by $1,000 multiplied by the taxpayer's marginal rate, not by the full $1,000. A $1,000 credit, by contrast, reduces tax owed by the full $1,000, regardless of the taxpayer's marginal rate.

[VERIFIED FACT] This is why, when evaluating tax benefits, it's important to distinguish carefully between the two — a "$1,000 tax break" described loosely could mean very different actual savings depending on whether it's structured as a deduction or a credit, and specific credits and deductions have their own eligibility rules and, in some cases, phase-out thresholds (a topic covered in a later lesson) that further affect their actual value for a given taxpayer.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A taxpayer in a 22% marginal tax bracket has a choice (hypothetically) between a $1,000 deduction and a $1,000 credit. The $1,000 deduction reduces her taxable income by $1,000, which at her 22% marginal rate reduces her actual tax owed by $220 (22% of $1,000). The $1,000 credit reduces her actual tax owed by the full $1,000 directly. [ASSUMPTION] Despite both being described as "$1,000 tax breaks," the credit is worth more than four times as much in actual tax savings for this taxpayer, illustrating why the distinction between these two types of tax benefits matters significantly.

## Practice Quiz

1. What is the key structural difference between a tax deduction and a tax credit?
2. Why is a tax credit generally more valuable than a deduction of the same dollar amount?
3. In the worked example, why did the $1,000 deduction only save $220 in actual tax?

## Answer Key

1. A deduction reduces taxable income before the tax calculation is applied; a credit reduces the actual tax owed directly, dollar for dollar, after the calculation.
2. Because a deduction's value depends on the taxpayer's marginal tax rate, reducing actual tax by only a fraction of the deduction amount, while a credit reduces tax owed by its full dollar amount regardless of tax rate.
3. Because the deduction reduced taxable income by $1,000, and at her 22% marginal rate, that reduction in taxable income translated to a $220 reduction in actual tax owed, not the full $1,000.

## Key Takeaways
- A tax deduction reduces taxable income before tax is calculated; a tax credit reduces actual tax owed directly.
- A tax credit is generally more valuable than a deduction of the same dollar amount, since a deduction's value depends on the taxpayer's marginal rate.
- Understanding this distinction is essential for accurately evaluating the actual value of any specific tax benefit.
