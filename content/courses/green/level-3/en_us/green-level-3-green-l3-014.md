## Core Content

Lesson 4 established that payroll taxes (Social Security and Medicare, in the U.S. context) are typically shared between an employee and their employer, each paying a portion. **Self-employment tax** exists to address a specific gap: a self-employed individual doesn't have a separate employer to share this obligation with — they are, in effect, both the employer and the employee.

Here's the "why" self-employment tax combines both shares into a single obligation: [VERIFIED FACT] a self-employed individual's self-employment tax rate is generally structured to cover approximately what would otherwise be both the employee's and the employer's shares of Social Security and Medicare tax combined, since there's no separate employer in this arrangement to contribute the other half. This often surprises new business owners transitioning from traditional employment, where they were only responsible for and aware of their own employee-side share.

[VERIFIED FACT] Some tax relief exists to partially offset this — for example, a deduction for a portion of self-employment tax paid, treated as a business expense in calculating other tax figures — but this doesn't eliminate the underlying reality that self-employment tax is calculated to cover a combined obligation traditional employees don't face directly in the same way.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An employee working for a company sees a specific payroll tax deduction on her paycheck, representing only her employee-side share, while her employer separately contributes a matching share on her behalf without it appearing as a deduction from her own pay. When that same person transitions to self-employment, she's surprised to find her self-employment tax obligation is calculated to cover both shares combined — a noticeably larger percentage than the single share she was accustomed to seeing on her paycheck as an employee, even though the underlying total tax obligation for a comparable income level is conceptually similar.

## Practice Quiz

1. What does self-employment tax generally cover?
2. Why does a self-employed individual owe what amounts to both shares of payroll tax combined?
3. What partially offsets this combined obligation, according to this lesson?

## Answer Key

1. Approximately both the employee's and employer's shares of Social Security and Medicare tax combined, since a self-employed individual has no separate employer to share the obligation with.
2. Because they function as both the employer and the employee in their own business, requiring them to cover both sides of the obligation that would otherwise be split between an employee and their employer.
3. A deduction for a portion of self-employment tax paid, treated as a business expense, though this doesn't eliminate the underlying combined obligation entirely.

## Key Takeaways
- Self-employment tax covers approximately both the employee and employer shares of Social Security and Medicare tax combined.
- This exists because a self-employed individual has no separate employer to share the payroll tax obligation with.
- Partial relief exists through a deduction for a portion of self-employment tax paid, though the combined obligation remains a real and often surprising cost for new business owners.
