## Core Content

This curriculum's RED track covers how depreciation allows property owners to deduct a portion of a property's value each year as a non-cash expense, reducing taxable income while cash flow remains unaffected. **Depreciation recapture** addresses what happens to that accumulated tax benefit when the property is eventually sold.

Here's the "why" recapture exists: depreciation deductions were based on an assumption that the asset was declining in value over time. If the asset is later sold for a gain — meaning it didn't actually decline in value the way the depreciation deductions assumed — [VERIFIED FACT] tax rules in many jurisdictions require a portion of that sale gain, up to the amount of depreciation previously claimed, to be taxed differently (often at a specific, sometimes higher, rate than standard long-term capital gains) — effectively "recapturing" some of the earlier tax benefit that was based on an assumption the eventual sale outcome didn't actually confirm.

This is why depreciation shouldn't be thought of as a permanent, no-strings-attached tax benefit — it's more accurately understood as a benefit that can be partially reversed upon a future, more profitable-than-assumed sale, a nuance directly relevant to Lesson 22's 1031 exchange discussion.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An investor claims depreciation deductions on a rental property over several years of ownership, reducing her taxable income each year based on the assumption the property's structure was gradually declining in value. When she eventually sells the property for a meaningful gain — reflecting real appreciation, not decline — a portion of that gain, up to the total depreciation she previously claimed, is subject to depreciation recapture rules, taxed differently than the remainder of her capital gain. [ASSUMPTION] This recapture effectively claws back some of the tax benefit she received during her ownership years, reflecting the fact that the property didn't actually decline in value the way the depreciation deductions had assumed.

## Practice Quiz

1. What does depreciation recapture generally address?
2. Why does selling a depreciated asset for a gain trigger this specific tax consequence?
3. Why shouldn't depreciation be thought of as a permanent, no-strings-attached tax benefit?

## Answer Key

1. What happens to the accumulated tax benefit from depreciation deductions when a depreciated asset is eventually sold, particularly for a gain.
2. Because depreciation deductions assumed the asset was declining in value, and a sale gain suggests that assumption wasn't accurate, prompting rules that recapture a portion of the earlier tax benefit.
3. Because it can be partially reversed upon a future, more profitable-than-assumed sale, rather than being a fixed, permanently retained benefit.

## Key Takeaways
- Depreciation recapture applies when a depreciated asset is sold for a gain, reversing part of the earlier tax benefit.
- This exists because the original depreciation assumed a decline in value that a sale gain suggests didn't actually happen.
- Depreciation should be understood as a conditional, potentially partially reversible tax benefit, not a permanent one.
