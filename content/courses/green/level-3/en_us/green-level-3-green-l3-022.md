## Core Content

This curriculum's RED track introduced the 1031 exchange as a tool letting an investor defer capital gains tax by reinvesting proceeds into a like-kind property. Here's the "why" this lesson's title uses "defers" specifically, connecting directly to Lesson 11's tax-deferred-versus-tax-free distinction: a 1031 exchange doesn't make the tax liability disappear — it postpones it, generally attaching it to the new property's cost basis, meaning the deferred tax liability follows the investor into the replacement property rather than being eliminated.

[VERIFIED FACT] This includes the depreciation recapture liability discussed in Lesson 21 — a properly executed 1031 exchange typically defers both the capital gains tax and any associated depreciation recapture that would otherwise be triggered by a taxable sale, carrying both forward into the replacement property's basis rather than resolving them at the time of the exchange.

This is why an investor using repeated 1031 exchanges over many years, as covered in the RED track, is building up an increasingly large deferred tax liability with each exchange — a liability that will eventually be triggered if and when the property is ever sold in a fully taxable transaction rather than exchanged again, unless it's ultimately resolved through other means (such as the step-up in basis at death, covered in a later lesson).

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An investor completes a 1031 exchange, deferring $80,000 in capital gains tax and $20,000 in depreciation recapture that would otherwise have been due on a taxable sale — a combined $100,000 in deferred tax liability. [ASSUMPTION] This deferred liability doesn't disappear; it effectively carries forward, attached to the replacement property's adjusted cost basis, meaning if she sells that replacement property in a fully taxable transaction years later without exchanging again, both the original deferred amounts and any additional gain on the new property become due at that time — illustrating why "deferred" is the accurate word, not "eliminated."

## Practice Quiz

1. Why does a 1031 exchange "defer" tax rather than eliminate it?

3. Does a 1031 exchange defer only capital gains tax, or does it also defer depreciation recapture?

## Answer Key

1. Because the tax liability is postponed, generally attaching to the replacement property's cost basis, rather than being made to disappear entirely.
2. This is a direct, real-world example of tax-deferred treatment as defined in Lesson 11 — the tax liability still exists and will eventually come due under certain future circumstances, rather than being tax-free.
3. It defers both — a properly executed 1031 exchange typically defers both the capital gains tax and any associated depreciation recapture from Lesson 21.
