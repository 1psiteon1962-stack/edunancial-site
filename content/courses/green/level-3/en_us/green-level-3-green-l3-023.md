## Core Content

**Ordinary income** generally includes wages, salaries, and other typical earned income, taxed according to the progressive bracket structure covered in Lesson 1. **Capital gains** — profit from selling an investment or other capital asset — are often taxed under a separate set of rules, which can differ meaningfully from ordinary income tax treatment.

Here's the "why" this distinction commonly exists, closely paralleling a concept covered in this curriculum's WHITE track: many tax systems apply different capital gains rates based on **holding period** — how long the asset was owned before being sold — often taxing gains on assets held for a shorter period at rates closer to (or the same as) ordinary income rates, while applying more favorable rates to gains on assets held longer, generally with the stated policy goal of encouraging longer-term investment over frequent short-term trading.

[VERIFIED FACT] The specific holding-period thresholds, applicable rates, and how capital gains interact with ordinary income for overall tax calculation purposes are set by tax law, vary significantly by jurisdiction, and change over time — general familiarity with this distinction is valuable, but exact figures should be verified for a specific situation and jurisdiction.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An investor sells a stock held for a short period before the sale, resulting in a gain generally taxed similarly to her ordinary income, at her regular marginal rate (Lesson 1). A different investor sells a similar stock held for a much longer period, with the resulting gain potentially taxed at a more favorable long-term capital gains rate under many tax systems. [ASSUMPTION] Despite generating an identical dollar amount of gain, these two investors could face meaningfully different tax outcomes purely based on how long each held the asset before selling — directly paralleling the holding-period discussion covered for paper assets elsewhere in this curriculum.
