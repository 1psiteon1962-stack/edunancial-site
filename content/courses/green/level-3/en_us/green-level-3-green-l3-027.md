## Core Content

A property's **assessed value** — the figure a local taxing authority uses to calculate property tax owed — is not the same thing as its **market value**, the price it would likely fetch if sold today. This curriculum's RED track covers property tax appeals from an investor's cash-flow perspective; here's the "why" behind the underlying gap that makes those appeals possible at all.

[VERIFIED FACT] Assessment methods, update frequency, and the specific formulas used vary significantly by jurisdiction — some jurisdictions reassess properties annually, others much less frequently, and some apply specific caps or formulas limiting how quickly assessed value can rise even if market value increases faster. This means assessed value can lag behind (or, less commonly, exceed) actual current market value, sometimes significantly, depending on how recently and how accurately the specific jurisdiction's assessment process reflects real market conditions.

This gap can work in either direction: a property in a rapidly appreciating market might have an assessed value well below its true current market value (generally favorable for the owner's tax bill, at least temporarily), while a property in a declining market, if the assessment hasn't been updated recently, might carry an assessed value well above its actual current market value (unfavorable for the owner, and exactly the kind of situation an appeal, as discussed in the RED track, is designed to address).

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A property is assessed at a certain value during a period of relatively flat local market conditions. Over the following years, the broader local market experiences a significant downturn, but the jurisdiction's assessment isn't updated to reflect this decline for some time. [ASSUMPTION] During this lag period, the property's owner is paying property tax based on an assessed value well above what the property would actually sell for in the current market — exactly the kind of gap between assessed and market value that would justify pursuing a property tax appeal, as discussed in this curriculum's RED track.

## Answer Key

1. Assessed value is the figure a local taxing authority uses to calculate property tax; market value is the price the property would likely fetch if sold today — these are calculated differently and often diverge.
2. Because assessment update frequency and methods vary by jurisdiction, assessed value can lag behind rising market value (favoring the owner) or exceed a declining market value if not updated promptly (disadvantaging the owner).
3. This lesson explains the underlying gap between assessed and market value that makes a property tax appeal — covered in the RED track as a way to improve cash flow — a legitimate and sometimes necessary tool.
