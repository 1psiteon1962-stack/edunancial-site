## Core Content

Many tax credits, including common credits related to dependents in various jurisdictions, don't apply equally at every income level. Instead, they often include a **phase-out** — a mechanism where the credit amount gradually shrinks, and eventually disappears entirely, as a taxpayer's income (often measured using AGI, as introduced in Lesson 10) rises above a certain threshold.

Here's the general "why" this design is common: [VERIFIED FACT] many of these credits are specifically intended to provide targeted relief to lower- and middle-income taxpayers, and a phase-out mechanism is how that targeting is achieved — rather than providing the same benefit regardless of income level, the credit is designed to matter most for those with more modest incomes, and gradually recede as income rises and, arguably, the need for that specific form of relief diminishes.

This is why Lesson 10's point about AGI ripple effects matters directly here: an above-the-line deduction that lowers AGI can sometimes help a taxpayer stay under a specific phase-out threshold for a separate credit, preserving eligibility for a benefit that might otherwise have been reduced or eliminated at a higher AGI figure.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A tax credit related to dependents begins phasing out once a taxpayer's AGI exceeds a certain threshold, gradually reducing the credit amount as income rises further above that point, until it eventually phases out completely at a higher income level. [ASSUMPTION] A taxpayer whose AGI sits just above the phase-out threshold might find that claiming an available above-the-line deduction (Lesson 10) brings her AGI back under that threshold, preserving her full eligibility for the credit — directly illustrating the AGI ripple-effect concept introduced earlier in this track.
