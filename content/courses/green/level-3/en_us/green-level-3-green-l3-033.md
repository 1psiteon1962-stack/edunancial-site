## Core Content

Different countries take different general approaches to what income they tax. Some countries generally tax their residents or citizens on their **worldwide income** — income earned anywhere in the world, not just domestically. Other countries generally use a more **territorial** approach, primarily taxing income earned within their own borders, regardless of the taxpayer's residence or citizenship.

Here's the "why" this creates genuine double-taxation risk for someone earning income internationally: if a person is a tax resident of (or citizen of) a country using a worldwide taxation approach, while also earning income within a different country that separately taxes income earned within its own borders under its own territorial rules, that same income could, without some mechanism to prevent it, potentially be taxed twice — once by each country, under each country's own independent rules.

[VERIFIED FACT] This genuine risk is exactly why mechanisms like foreign tax credits (allowing tax paid to one country to offset tax otherwise owed to another on the same income) and tax treaties (covered in the next lesson) exist — without some coordinating mechanism, cross-border income would routinely face this double-taxation problem, which most countries and international tax frameworks specifically aim to reduce or eliminate through these kinds of tools.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A person who is a tax resident of a country using worldwide taxation earns income working temporarily in a different country that taxes income earned within its own borders under its own separate rules. Without any coordinating mechanism, this income could be taxed once by the country where it was actually earned, and again by the person's home country under its worldwide taxation approach — the same income, taxed twice, by two separate governments each applying their own independent rules. [ASSUMPTION] A foreign tax credit or an applicable tax treaty, discussed in the next lesson, would typically be the tool used to address and reduce this specific double-taxation risk.

## Key Takeaways
- Worldwide taxation approaches tax residents or citizens on income earned anywhere; territorial approaches primarily tax income earned within a country's own borders.
- These differing approaches can create genuine double-taxation risk for someone earning income across multiple countries.
- Foreign tax credits and tax treaties exist specifically to reduce or eliminate this risk.
