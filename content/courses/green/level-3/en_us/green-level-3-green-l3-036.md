## Core Content

Lesson 35 established that an audit can happen to anyone, regardless of whether they've done anything wrong, and that the outcome largely depends on the taxpayer's ability to support what was claimed. Here's the "why" **contemporaneous** records — created at or near the time of the actual transaction or expense — are so much more valuable than records reconstructed later, after an audit notice arrives: contemporaneous records reflect the actual details as they happened, while a reconstruction attempted months or years later relies on memory, which is far less reliable and far less persuasive as supporting evidence.

[VERIFIED FACT] Generally useful categories of records to retain include receipts and invoices supporting claimed deductions, bank and credit card statements, documentation supporting significant transactions (like the purchase or sale of an investment or property), and records supporting any credits claimed. Retention periods for tax records vary by jurisdiction and by the type of record — general guidance suggests retaining most tax records for several years after filing, with longer retention appropriate for records related to property, investments, or other items with long-term tax implications, but specific retention periods should be confirmed for a given jurisdiction rather than assumed.

This is why the best time to organize tax records is throughout the year, as transactions actually happen — not scrambled together after an audit notice has already arrived, when the ability to reconstruct accurate, persuasive documentation is at its weakest.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A taxpayer who maintains organized folders (physical or digital) throughout the year — sorting receipts, saving relevant statements, and documenting significant transactions as they occur — is well-prepared if a specific item is later selected for audit review, since she can quickly produce clear, contemporaneous documentation supporting her original claim. [ASSUMPTION] A different taxpayer who discards receipts and doesn't organize records throughout the year, and then receives an audit notice for the same type of item, faces a much harder task trying to reconstruct comparable documentation from memory or incomplete records — illustrating why proactive, ongoing recordkeeping is a far more reliable defense than reactive reconstruction.

## Key Takeaways
- Contemporaneous records, created at the time of the actual transaction, are far more reliable and persuasive than records reconstructed later.
- Useful records include receipts, statements, and documentation of significant transactions and claimed credits, retained for a period that varies by jurisdiction and record type.
- Organizing tax records throughout the year is the most effective preparation for a possible future audit.
