## Core Content

Many jurisdictions, including the U.S., generally treat cryptocurrency as property for tax purposes, rather than as currency in the traditional sense. This connects directly to Lesson 23's capital gains discussion: [VERIFIED FACT] because cryptocurrency is generally treated as property, transactions involving it — including selling it for traditional currency, but also trading one cryptocurrency for another, or using cryptocurrency to purchase goods or services — can trigger a taxable capital gain or loss, calculated based on the difference between the cryptocurrency's value at the time of the transaction and its original cost basis.

Here's the "why" this surprises many people specifically: a transaction that doesn't involve receiving any traditional cash at all — trading one cryptocurrency directly for another, for example — can still be a taxable event under this property-based treatment, since the trade is treated as if the first cryptocurrency was sold at its current value (potentially realizing a gain or loss) and the proceeds were then used to purchase the second cryptocurrency, even though no traditional currency ever actually changed hands in the transaction itself.

This is why accurate recordkeeping (echoing Lesson 36's broader recordkeeping discussion) for cryptocurrency transactions — tracking the cost basis and value at the time of each transaction — is especially important, given how many everyday cryptocurrency activities can trigger a taxable event that might not be obvious without specifically understanding this property-based tax treatment.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A person trades one cryptocurrency directly for a different cryptocurrency, without ever converting either one to traditional currency at any point in the transaction. [ASSUMPTION] Under a property-based tax treatment, this trade is treated as if the first cryptocurrency was sold at its current market value, and any gain or loss relative to its original cost basis is a taxable event for that year — despite the person never actually receiving any traditional cash from the transaction, illustrating why "I didn't receive any cash" is not a reliable guide to whether a cryptocurrency transaction is taxable.
