## Core Content

[HYPOTHETICAL SCENARIO — ASSUMPTION, NOT VERIFIED FACT] Imagine a government, facing significant fiscal pressure, enacts broad legislation meaningfully raising income tax rates across most brackets, effective in future years. This is a hypothetical stress-test scenario, not a prediction about any specific real policy or country.

In this hypothetical, the Traditional-versus-Roth decision from Lesson 12 shifts meaningfully: taxpayers who took a Traditional deduction at today's lower rates, planning to withdraw and pay tax later, would now face that future withdrawal tax at the new, higher rates — a less favorable outcome than originally anticipated when the contribution was made. Taxpayers who instead chose Roth accounts, paying tax upfront at today's lower rates, would look comparatively better off in hindsight, since their qualifying future withdrawals remain untaxed regardless of the new higher rates.

This same hypothetical rate increase would touch other areas covered throughout this curriculum simultaneously: pass-through business income (Lesson 18), now taxed at the new higher individual rates, might make C-corporation structure (Lesson 19) comparatively more attractive for some businesses, depending on how corporate rates were affected by the same legislation. Capital gains rates (Lesson 23), if also affected, would change the calculus for realizing gains now versus later. The value of the tax-deferred versus tax-free distinction (Lesson 11) becomes sharper — deferral into a higher future rate environment is less advantageous than deferral into a stable or declining one.

## Worked Example

[HYPOTHETICAL — ILLUSTRATIVE, NOT A PREDICTION] A person who split her retirement contributions between Traditional and Roth accounts, following the tax diversification principle from Lesson 45, finds herself in a stronger position under this hypothetical rate-increase scenario than someone who put everything into a Traditional account: her Roth withdrawals remain unaffected by the new higher rates, giving her flexibility to draw more heavily from that account in years when the new rates would otherwise push her Traditional withdrawals into an even higher bracket. Meanwhile, a small business owner reconsiders her pass-through business structure, given that the new individual rate increase, combined with her specific corporate tax comparison, might now favor a different structure than the one that made sense before the hypothetical change.
