## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
- Explain what CAC measures and why it's often treated as a purely marketing metric
- Explain why CAC is really a question about financing and business survival, not just marketing efficiency
- Identify at least two other areas of a business that a change in CAC would affect

## Core Content

Customer Acquisition Cost (CAC), covered in this curriculum's BLUE track alongside lifetime value (LTV), is often discussed as a marketing performance metric — did this campaign, this channel, this ad spend, bring in customers efficiently? Here's the "why" this framing is incomplete: every dollar spent acquiring a customer today is a dollar the business needs to either have on hand or borrow, and it won't be recovered until that customer generates enough revenue to pay it back — meaning CAC is simultaneously a marketing question and a cash flow and financing question.

A business with rising CAC isn't just facing a "marketing problem." It's facing a burn rate problem (BLUE track), a runway problem if it's not yet profitable, and potentially a financing decision about whether to raise capital, cut spending, or accept slower growth. A marketing team optimizing purely for lower CAC without visibility into the company's cash position might make a locally sensible decision that's globally wrong for the business's actual financial situation.

Before reading further: what else, beyond marketing and cash flow, might a sustained increase in CAC put pressure on? Consider what happens to a sales team's compensation plans, to investor confidence, to pricing decisions, and to how much runway the business actually has left.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A company's CAC rises 40% over two quarters due to increased ad competition. The marketing team treats this as a channel-optimization problem to solve with better targeting. [ASSUMPTION] Meanwhile, this same CAC increase is quietly extending the company's payback period on new customers, shrinking its runway (BLUE track) faster than the finance team has flagged, and making the case for a fundraising round more urgent than anyone in the marketing meeting realizes — the same number is a marketing metric, a cash flow signal, and a financing trigger simultaneously.

## Practice Quiz

1. What does CAC measure?
2. Why is CAC not purely a marketing metric?
3. Name two areas of the business, beyond marketing itself, that a sustained rise in CAC could affect.

## Answer Key

1. The average cost of acquiring a new customer, typically total sales and marketing spend divided by new customers acquired.
2. Because every dollar spent on acquisition affects cash flow, runway, and financing needs — a marketing efficiency question is simultaneously a business survival question.
3. Any two reasonable answers, such as: runway and financing timeline, sales compensation structures, pricing strategy, investor relations, or overall growth strategy.

## Key Takeaways
- CAC is simultaneously a marketing metric and a cash flow/financing signal.
- A locally sensible marketing decision can be globally wrong if made without visibility into the business's cash position.
- Sales and marketing decisions ripple into financing, compensation, and strategic decisions elsewhere in the business.
