## Core Content

Pricing is often treated as a marketing and psychology question — what number makes the product feel valuable, what number matches competitors, what number customers will accept. All of that is real. But here's the "why" pricing is inseparable from finance: a price directly determines gross margin (BLUE track) — the percentage of every sale that remains after direct costs — which in turn determines how much room the business has to cover overhead, invest in growth, or absorb a bad month.

A price set too low to win market share can look like a marketing success (more customers, more volume) while quietly starving the business of the margin it needs to survive a downturn, fund payroll, or reinvest in the product. A price set without understanding the actual cost structure behind it isn't a pricing decision at all — it's a guess dressed up as strategy.

Before moving on: if a competitor suddenly cut prices 20%, what would you need to know about your own cost structure before deciding whether to follow them? What would matching that price actually do to your margin, and what would refusing to match it do to your sales volume?

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A company sets its price primarily based on what "feels right" relative to competitors, without first calculating its own gross margin at that price point. [ASSUMPTION] Sales volume looks strong for several months, creating an impression of a winning pricing strategy — but a closer look at the actual margin reveals the business is covering barely more than its direct costs on each sale, leaving almost nothing to cover overhead, marketing spend, or a cushion for slower periods. The pricing "worked" by a marketing measure (volume) while quietly failing by a financial one (margin).

## Practice Quiz

1. What does a price directly determine, connecting to the BLUE track?
2. Why can a pricing decision look successful by a marketing measure while being unsound financially?
3. If a competitor cut prices sharply, what would you need to understand about your own business before responding?

## Answer Key

1. Gross margin — the percentage of each sale remaining after direct costs.
2. Because volume and customer perception can look strong even while margin is too thin to sustainably cover overhead and other costs.
3. Your own cost structure and current margin at the existing price, so you can calculate what matching the competitor's price would actually do to your profitability, not just your sales volume.

## Key Takeaways
- A price decision directly determines gross margin, not just customer perception.
- Volume-based "success" can mask a pricing decision that's financially unsustainable.
- Responding to a competitor's price change requires understanding your own cost structure first, not just matching their number.
