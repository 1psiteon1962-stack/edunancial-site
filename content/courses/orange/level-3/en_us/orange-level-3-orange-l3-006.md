## Core Content

This curriculum's BLUE track established that CAC and LTV must be compared together, since a high CAC can still be justified by a sufficiently high LTV. Here's the "why" LTV itself depends heavily on retention, not just on how much a customer spends per transaction: a customer who stays for years, buying repeatedly, generates far more lifetime value than one who makes a single purchase and never returns — meaning a business's retention rate is doing much of the work behind whatever LTV figure ends up justifying its acquisition spending.

This is why a business experiencing strong new-customer growth can still be in real trouble if its retention is quietly declining: the acquisition numbers look impressive on a dashboard, while the underlying LTV assumption that justified the acquisition spending in the first place is eroding, potentially breaking the CAC-to-LTV ratio the business is depending on without anyone noticing until the effect shows up in overall revenue much later.

What would you want to monitor, beyond new customer count, to know whether your acquisition spending is actually sustainable? Consider what a rising CAC combined with a quietly falling retention rate would mean for the ratio this business depends on.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A business celebrates strong month-over-month growth in new customers, driven by an aggressive acquisition campaign. [ASSUMPTION] Underneath that growth, its customer retention rate has been quietly declining for two quarters, meaning the actual lifetime value per customer is lower than the figure originally used to justify the acquisition spend — the CAC-to-LTV ratio from the BLUE track has silently worsened, even though the growth dashboard looks entirely positive, illustrating why retention needs to be tracked alongside acquisition, not treated as a separate, secondary metric.

## Key Takeaways
- Lifetime value depends heavily on retention, since repeat customers generate far more value than one-time buyers.
- Strong acquisition growth can mask a declining retention rate that's quietly undermining the business's real economics.
- Retention should be tracked alongside acquisition, since both together determine whether the CAC-to-LTV ratio actually holds up over time.
