## Core Content

Marketing copy is written to persuade, but here's the "why" the specific words chosen carry legal weight beyond their persuasive power: a factual claim made in advertising — about a product's performance, safety, results, or comparison to competitors — can generally create a legal obligation to actually back that claim up, connecting to the broader legal principle that misrepresentation can create liability, covered across several contexts in this curriculum's PURPLE track.

Here's why "everyone in this industry exaggerates" isn't a reliable defense: regulatory bodies and consumers can generally pursue claims against a business for false or misleading advertising regardless of whether competitors are doing something similar — industry norms don't determine legal exposure, the specific claim's truthfulness and substantiation do. A specific, measurable claim ("reduces costs by 30%") carries more legal risk than a vague, subjective one ("great value"), precisely because it's more easily proven false.

Before finalizing marketing copy that makes a specific factual claim, what evidence actually supports it, and who inside or outside the business could verify that evidence if challenged?

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A business's marketing team writes an ad claiming a specific product "reduces energy costs by 30%," based on an internal estimate rather than any rigorous, verifiable testing. [ASSUMPTION] If a regulator or a competitor challenges this claim and the business can't substantiate the specific 30% figure with credible evidence, it could face legal consequences for false or misleading advertising, connecting directly to the PURPLE track's broader misrepresentation principles — regardless of whether the marketing team believed the claim was roughly accurate or intended no harm.
