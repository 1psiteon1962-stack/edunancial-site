## Core Content

This curriculum's BLUE track covered break-even analysis using fixed costs, variable costs, and contribution margin. Here's the "why" that same math reveals something counterintuitive about discounting: because a discount reduces the contribution margin on every sale, the volume increase needed just to maintain the same total profit is often much larger than the discount percentage itself suggests.

Here's the mechanism: if a product sells for $100 with $60 in variable cost (a $40 contribution margin), a 20% discount drops the price to $80, cutting contribution margin to $20 — a 50% drop in margin per unit, not 20%. To generate the same total profit as before, the business would need to sell twice as many units, not just 20% more, simply to break even on the discount, let alone actually grow profit.

Before running a discount promotion, what's your actual contribution margin at full price, and how much additional volume would you genuinely need to at least maintain current profit? Is that volume increase realistic given your market?

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A business with a $40 contribution margin per unit offers a 20% discount, dropping margin per unit to $20. [ASSUMPTION] Sales volume increases by 30%, which sounds like a clear win — but because margin per unit was cut in half, that 30% volume increase actually produces less total profit than before the discount, not more, illustrating why the intuitive "discount brings more sales, more sales means more profit" reasoning misses the disproportionate impact of margin erosion.

3. The actual contribution margin at full price and the specific volume increase needed just to maintain current total profit, then an honest assessment of whether that volume increase is realistic.
