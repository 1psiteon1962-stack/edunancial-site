## Core Content

Lesson 3 established that a sales funnel's conversion rates are probabilities, not certainties. A **sales pipeline forecast** applies that same principle at the level of individual deals: rather than simply adding up the total value of every deal currently "in progress," a sound forecast weights each deal by its estimated probability of actually closing, based on its current stage — an early-stage deal might be weighted at 10%, a late-stage deal nearing signature might be weighted at 80%.

Here's the "why" skipping this weighting produces a badly overstated forecast: [ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] if a business has $1 million in total pipeline value across deals at various stages, simply reporting "$1 million in pipeline" implies a level of expected revenue the actual probability-weighted math doesn't support — the true expected value, once each deal is weighted by its realistic likelihood of closing, might be closer to $300,000, a dramatically different, and far more useful, number for actual financial planning.

Before committing to a spending plan based on your pipeline, is the number you're using the raw total value of open deals, or the probability-weighted expected value? Those are very different numbers, and only one of them should drive a financial commitment.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A sales team reports "$1 million in pipeline" to leadership, who use that figure to plan hiring for the next quarter. [ASSUMPTION] The actual probability-weighted expected value of that same pipeline, once each deal's stage and realistic close likelihood are factored in, is closer to $300,000 — a business that planned hiring around the raw $1 million figure has committed to costs based on a number more than three times higher than what's realistically expected, echoing the same probability-based planning error covered in Lesson 3.

2. It produces a badly overstated forecast, since it treats every open deal as if it were certain to close, regardless of its actual stage or likelihood.
