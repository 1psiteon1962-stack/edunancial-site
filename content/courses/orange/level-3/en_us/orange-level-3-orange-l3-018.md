## Core Content

This curriculum's BLUE track introduced the cash conversion cycle — the time between spending cash on inventory or operations and actually collecting cash from customers. Here's the "why" marketing spend needs to be planned with this same cycle in mind: marketing dollars are spent immediately, but the resulting revenue — from leads converting to sales, and then from those sales actually being collected as cash — often arrives weeks or months later, adding its own lag on top of whatever cash conversion cycle the underlying business already has.

This is why a marketing budget planned in isolation from cash flow planning can create a genuine crisis even when the marketing itself is working exactly as intended: a business that ramps up marketing spend aggressively, expecting the resulting sales to arrive and be collected quickly, can find itself short on cash in the gap between paying for the marketing and actually collecting on the sales it generates — a timing mismatch, not a failure of the marketing itself.

Before committing to a significant marketing spend increase, how long does it typically take from ad spend to closed sale to actual cash collection in your business? Does your current cash position comfortably bridge that full gap?

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A business significantly increases marketing spend, expecting the resulting sales growth to fund the next quarter's operations. [ASSUMPTION] The marketing genuinely works — leads convert to sales at the expected rate — but the actual cash from those sales, given the business's typical 60-day collection cycle from the BLUE track's discussion, doesn't arrive until well after the marketing bills came due, creating a cash shortage despite the marketing campaign being, by its own metrics, a clear success.

## Key Takeaways
- Marketing spend is immediate, but the resulting cash collection often lags, adding to the business's existing cash conversion cycle.
- Effective marketing can still create a cash flow crisis if the spending isn't planned alongside this timing reality.
- Marketing budgets should be planned together with cash flow planning, not treated as a separate, independent decision.
