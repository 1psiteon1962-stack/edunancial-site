## Core Content

A sales commission structure is meant to motivate performance, but here's the "why" its specific design matters as much as its existence: salespeople, like anyone responding to incentives, will generally optimize for exactly what the commission structure actually rewards — not necessarily what leadership intends the structure to encourage. A plan rewarding pure sales volume, without regard to customer retention or profitability, can create exactly the kind of behavior that undermines the business's actual long-term goals, even while technically "working" as designed.

This connects to a broader theme relevant across this curriculum: any incentive structure — a commission plan, an executive bonus, a loan officer's compensation — shapes behavior according to its specific mechanics, not according to good intentions layered on top of it. A commission structure rewarding new customer acquisition without regard to the lifetime value or churn covered earlier in this track can inadvertently encourage salespeople to close deals with customers unlikely to stay, since the commission is earned regardless of what happens after the sale.

If your current commission structure rewards X, what behavior is it actually incentivizing, and does that behavior align with what the business genuinely needs — growth that lasts, not just growth that closes?

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A business pays commission purely on new sales closed, with no consideration of customer retention. [ASSUMPTION] Salespeople, rationally responding to this structure, prioritize closing as many deals as possible, sometimes with customers who are a poor fit and likely to churn quickly — the commission is earned either way, so the structure has inadvertently encouraged exactly the kind of low-retention growth that undermines the LTV-dependent business health discussed in Lesson 6, even though no individual salesperson did anything against the letter of their compensation plan.
