## Core Content

A **CRM (Customer Relationship Management)** system is generally used to track sales interactions, pipeline stages, and customer information. Here's the "why" this tool matters well beyond the sales team itself: the data inside a CRM — pipeline stage, deal size, expected close date — is precisely the raw material behind the probability-weighted revenue forecasting covered in Lesson 15, which in turn feeds cash flow planning, hiring decisions, and investor reporting.

This is why CRM data discipline — salespeople accurately and consistently updating deal stages, realistic close dates, and deal values — isn't merely an internal sales management concern. Sloppy or overly optimistic CRM data (deals left at "likely to close" long after they've gone cold, unrealistic close-date estimates) directly corrupts the financial forecasts the rest of the business depends on, since finance and leadership generally can't distinguish accurate CRM data from wishful thinking without independently verifying every entry.

What incentive does your sales team currently have to keep CRM data accurate and current, versus optimistic? If updating a deal's stage down doesn't change how that salesperson is evaluated, what's actually motivating them to keep the data honest?

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A sales team consistently keeps deals marked as "likely to close soon" in the CRM well after those deals have actually gone cold, since there's no specific consequence for optimistic labeling and some upside to appearing productive. [ASSUMPTION] The finance team, using this CRM data to build the probability-weighted forecast discussed in Lesson 15, produces a revenue projection that's systematically too optimistic — not because the forecasting math is wrong, but because the underlying data feeding it was unreliable, illustrating why CRM data quality is a financial planning issue, not just a sales management one.
