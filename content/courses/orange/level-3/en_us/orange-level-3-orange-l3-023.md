## Core Content

A refund policy is often treated as a customer service or brand-tone decision — friendly and generous, or strict and protective of margin. Here's the "why" it's also, simultaneously, a legally binding statement of terms: [VERIFIED FACT] in many jurisdictions, a business's stated refund policy becomes part of the terms of sale, and consumer protection laws may impose additional minimum requirements regardless of what a business's own policy states, echoing the PURPLE track's discussion of mandatory statutory protections that can't simply be contracted around.

This is why a refund policy needs the same careful drafting attention as any other legally significant document — vague language creates ambiguity that leads to disputes, while an overly restrictive policy, even if technically legal, can create the reputational damage discussed in Lesson 4 when customers feel treated unfairly, sharing that experience publicly regardless of whether any law was actually violated.

Before finalizing a refund policy, does it clearly address the specific scenarios customers are most likely to actually encounter, and does it comply with any minimum consumer protection requirements applicable in the jurisdictions where you sell?

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A business drafts a refund policy with vague language about "reasonable circumstances" for a refund, without clearly specifying what that actually means in practice. [ASSUMPTION] When a customer requests a refund under circumstances the business considers unreasonable but the customer considers entirely reasonable, this ambiguity creates a dispute that a more clearly drafted policy would have avoided — and depending on the applicable jurisdiction's consumer protection requirements, the business's restrictive interpretation might not even be legally enforceable, regardless of what its own policy states.
