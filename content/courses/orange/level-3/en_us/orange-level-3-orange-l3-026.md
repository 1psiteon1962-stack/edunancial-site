## Core Content

This curriculum's BLUE track distinguished between TAM (the total possible market) and SOM (what a business can realistically capture). Here's the "why" a deliberate **niche strategy** — focusing on a smaller, specific customer segment rather than the broadest possible audience — can be a genuinely sound choice, not a limitation to eventually outgrow: a business serving a specific niche exceptionally well can build a defensible position — deep expertise, tailored messaging, and strong customer loyalty within that segment — that a broader, more generalized competitor would struggle to match without diluting its own broader appeal.

This directly echoes the BLUE track's caution against chasing an impressive TAM figure without a credible, realistic path to actually capturing a meaningful share of it: a smaller SOM, well-defended within a specific niche, can be a stronger, more sustainable business than a larger but weakly defended position across a broad, undifferentiated market.

Is your current strategy trying to appeal broadly to everyone, or deliberately serving a specific segment exceptionally well? What would you have to give up, and what would you gain, by narrowing your focus?

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A business initially tries to appeal broadly to the widest possible customer base, competing against many larger, better-resourced competitors on their own broad terms. [ASSUMPTION] Shifting to a deliberate niche strategy — focusing specifically on a smaller customer segment with distinctive needs the broader competitors serve poorly — the business builds deep expertise and loyalty within that segment, creating a defensible position a much larger, generalized competitor would find difficult to replicate without diluting its own broad-market positioning, echoing the BLUE track's emphasis on a credible, realistic SOM over an impressive but unrealistic TAM.
