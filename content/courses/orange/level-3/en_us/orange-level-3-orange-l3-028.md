## Core Content

Lesson 19 covered how a commission structure shapes salesperson behavior. Here's a related, legal "why" a commission plan's design matters beyond its motivational impact: this curriculum's PURPLE track established that a worker's actual classification — employee versus independent contractor — depends on the substance of the working relationship, not just a label, and that misclassification carries real financial and legal risk (Lesson 36 in that track). A sales commission structure that closely controls how, when, and where a salesperson works, while labeling them a contractor to avoid payroll obligations, is exactly the kind of substantive classification question that PURPLE track lesson warned about.

Separately, for salespeople correctly classified as employees, [VERIFIED FACT] many jurisdictions have specific wage and hour rules governing how and when commissions must be calculated and paid, including rules about minimum wage guarantees during slower periods or specific timing requirements for commission payments — rules that exist independently of whatever the commission plan document itself states.

Before finalizing a sales incentive plan, has it been reviewed against both the classification substance test from the PURPLE track and any applicable wage-and-hour requirements for commission-based pay in the relevant jurisdiction?

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A business structures its sales team as independent contractors specifically to avoid payroll tax and benefit obligations, while closely controlling their schedules, sales scripts, and reporting requirements in a manner resembling employment. [ASSUMPTION] Applying the substantive classification test from the PURPLE track, this arrangement likely doesn't hold up as genuine independent contractor status, exposing the business to the same misclassification risk covered in that track's Lesson 36 — a risk created by the sales incentive structure's design, not by anything specific to the sales function itself.
