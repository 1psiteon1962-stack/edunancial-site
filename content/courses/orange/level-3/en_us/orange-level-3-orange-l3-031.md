## Core Content

A **lagging indicator** reflects a result that has already happened — total revenue for the quarter, for example. A **leading indicator** signals a likely future change before that change fully shows up in the lagging numbers. Here's the "why" **churn** (the rate at which customers stop doing business with a company) functions as a leading indicator worth watching closely, rather than something to notice only after revenue has already declined: a rising churn rate directly erodes the retention that Lesson 6 established as central to lifetime value — but because existing customers often continue generating some revenue for a period even as churn is climbing, the effect on total revenue can lag behind the underlying churn trend by weeks or months.

This is why waiting for overall revenue to visibly decline before investigating a churn problem means acting well after the underlying issue actually began — a business that monitors churn as a leading indicator can identify and address whatever's driving customers away before that problem fully shows up in the lagging revenue figure everyone eventually notices.

What would a genuinely early warning system for a retention problem look like in your business — what would you monitor to catch a churn increase before it fully shows up in quarterly revenue?

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A business's churn rate begins rising in a given month, but because remaining and newly acquired customers are still generating revenue, the company's total revenue figure doesn't show a clear decline until several months later. [ASSUMPTION] A team monitoring only the lagging revenue figure doesn't notice a problem until that later decline appears, by which point the underlying issue driving customers away has had months to worsen. A team monitoring churn directly as a leading indicator would have identified the same problem months earlier, with far more time to investigate and respond before it fully materialized in revenue.
