## Core Content

**Sales cycle length** measures the typical time between first contact with a prospective customer and an actual closed sale. Here's the "why" this timing measure connects directly to the working capital concept covered in this curriculum's BLUE track: a business spending money on sales and marketing efforts today won't see the resulting revenue until a sales cycle's worth of time has passed — the longer that cycle, the longer the business needs to fund its own operations, salaries, and growth spending before the resulting sales actually convert into revenue.

This is why a business considering a growth strategy that involves entering a market with a longer typical sales cycle than its current one needs to specifically plan for a larger working capital cushion, not just a proportionally larger marketing or sales budget — the timing gap between spending and resulting revenue, not just the total spend, is what determines the actual financing need.

If you were entering a new market segment with a sales cycle twice as long as your current one, what would that mean for how much working capital you'd need to fund that expansion, beyond simply doubling your sales team's budget?

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A business with a typically short, one-month sales cycle expands into a new market segment where deals typically take six months to close. [ASSUMPTION] The business budgets for the new segment by simply scaling up its existing sales spending proportionally, without accounting for the much longer gap between that spending and the resulting revenue — creating a working capital shortfall during the extended period before the new segment's sales actually begin converting to revenue, a financing consequence directly tied to sales cycle length that a purely budget-proportional plan overlooked.
