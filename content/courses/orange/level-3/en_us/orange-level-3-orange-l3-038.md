## Core Content

A common marketing ROI calculation compares ad spend against resulting revenue, producing a seemingly clear return figure. Here's the "why" this simple calculation often significantly overstates the true return: it typically omits the cost of the team's time spent managing the campaign, the cost of the product or service actually delivered to the resulting customers (not just the marginal cost, but the true fully-loaded cost), the cost of any tools or software used to run the campaign, and — connecting directly to the opportunity cost concept covered in the GOLD track — the return that same capital and team attention could have generated in an alternative use.

This is why two marketing campaigns can both report a positive, seemingly attractive ROI while one is genuinely a strong use of resources and the other is quietly unprofitable once real, fully-loaded costs are accounted for — the headline ROI figure alone doesn't reveal which is which.

Before reporting a marketing campaign's ROI as a success, does the calculation include the team's time cost, the true cost of delivering to resulting customers, and a fair comparison against what else that same investment could have achieved?

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A campaign reports spending a specific amount on ads and generating several times that amount in resulting revenue, presented internally as a clear ROI success. [ASSUMPTION] A more complete accounting — including the marketing team's substantial time spent managing the campaign, the full cost of actually delivering the product to the resulting customers, and the opportunity cost of that team's attention being directed elsewhere — reveals the campaign's true return is far more modest than the headline figure suggested, illustrating why a simple ad-spend-to-revenue ratio can create a misleadingly rosy picture of actual marketing performance.
