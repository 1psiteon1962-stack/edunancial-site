## Core Content

Lesson 15 established that a sound sales forecast is a probability-weighted estimate, not a certainty. Here's the "why" an error in that forecast doesn't stay contained to the sales function: a sales forecast typically becomes an input to inventory purchasing decisions, hiring plans, marketing budget allocation, and cash flow planning — each of these downstream decisions is built on top of the forecast, meaning an error in the original number doesn't just affect sales reporting, it propagates into decisions across the business that were made assuming that forecast was reasonably accurate.

This is why a forecast that's off by a modest amount can create effects that feel much larger by the time they show up elsewhere: inventory purchased based on an inflated forecast sits unsold, tying up cash; hiring done based on that same inflated forecast creates payroll costs the actual revenue doesn't support; a marketing budget scaled to match expected growth that didn't materialize compounds the cash strain further — each dependent decision adds its own consequence on top of the original forecasting error.

Before treating your sales forecast as a fixed input for major decisions elsewhere in the business, how confident are you in that number, and what would happen to inventory, hiring, and cash plans if it came in meaningfully below expectations?

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A business forecasts a specific revenue figure for the coming quarter, and that number becomes the basis for inventory purchasing, a hiring plan, and a marketing budget increase. [ASSUMPTION] When actual sales come in 20% below that forecast, the effect isn't a contained 20% miss — inventory purchased on the inflated assumption sits unsold, new hires' payroll isn't supported by the actual revenue, and the marketing spend increase adds further cash strain, meaning the original forecasting error has compounded into a considerably larger financial impact once every dependent decision built on top of it is accounted for.

## Key Takeaways
- A sales forecast typically becomes an input to inventory, hiring, marketing, and cash flow decisions across the business.
- A forecasting error compounds as it moves through these dependent decisions, producing effects larger than the original miss.
- Treating a forecast as a probability-weighted estimate, not a certainty, protects downstream decisions from this compounding risk.
