## Core Content

This track has covered the legal exposure from false marketing claims (Lesson 7), the vulnerability of brand and reputation (Lesson 4), and the risk of sales overpromising (Lesson 16) as separate topics. Here's the "why" they converge on a single practical insight: honest, ethical marketing practices generally align closely with sound risk management, since the same practices that avoid legal exposure and reputational damage — substantiated claims, honest promises, transparent partnerships — are also, straightforwardly, the ethical choice.

This overlap is genuinely useful, but it's worth being precise about what it does and doesn't mean: the alignment between ethics and risk management means a business doesn't have to choose between "doing the right thing" and "protecting itself" in most marketing decisions — but it doesn't mean ethics and risk management are the same thing, or that every ethical marketing question reduces neatly to a risk calculation. Some ethical marketing choices matter even in situations where the legal or reputational risk of the alternative is genuinely low.

Where in your own marketing practices does the ethical choice and the risk-minimizing choice clearly point the same direction? Are there situations where you'd make the ethical choice even if you were confident the risk of the alternative was low?

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A business considers whether to substantiate a marketing claim rigorously before publishing it, or to publish a more impressive-sounding but less rigorously verified version. [ASSUMPTION] The rigorously substantiated version is both the more ethical choice and the lower-risk choice, given the legal exposure discussed in Lesson 7 and the reputational vulnerability discussed in Lesson 4 — in this case, ethics and risk management point in the same direction, making the decision straightforward, even though a genuinely ethical marketer might make the same honest choice even in a hypothetical situation where the legal or reputational risk of exaggerating happened to be lower.
