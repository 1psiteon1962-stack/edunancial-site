## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
- Trace how a single hypothetical market shock affects acquisition, retention, pricing, and forecasting simultaneously
- Identify the relevant concepts from earlier in this track without them being named for you
- Explain why resilience, not prediction, is the practical goal of this kind of exercise

## Core Content

[HYPOTHETICAL SCENARIO — ASSUMPTION, NOT VERIFIED FACT] Imagine a sudden shift in customer behavior — perhaps driven by a broader economic downturn, a shift in customer priorities, or a disruptive new competitor — causes conversion rates to drop sharply and existing customers to become notably more price-sensitive, all within a short period. This is a hypothetical stress-test scenario, not a prediction about any specific real event or market.

Before reading the worked example, think through this on your own: what would happen to your CAC-to-LTV ratio if conversion rates dropped? What would happen to your sales forecast, built on historical conversion assumptions (Lesson 3, Lesson 15)? If customers become more price-sensitive, what does that do to the margin math from Lesson 9 if you're tempted to discount in response? And what would this do to your churn rate (Lesson 31), given that existing customers are also affected, not just new prospects?

Try mapping out this cascade before checking whether your answers match the reasoning below.

## Worked Example

[HYPOTHETICAL — ILLUSTRATIVE, NOT A PREDICTION] A business facing this kind of hypothetical shock finds its CAC rising as conversion rates fall (Lesson 1), its sales forecast becoming unreliable since it was built on historical conversion assumptions that no longer hold (Lessons 3 and 15), and pressure to discount to retain price-sensitive customers — a move that, per Lesson 9's margin math, would require a disproportionately larger volume increase just to maintain current profit, a volume increase this same shock makes less likely, not more. Existing customer churn may also rise as customers reassess spending broadly, compounding the pressure on retention-dependent LTV (Lesson 6) at the same time acquisition is getting harder and more expensive.

## Key Takeaways
- A single hypothetical market shock can simultaneously pressure acquisition cost, forecasting reliability, pricing discipline, and retention.
- These pressures compound together rather than acting independently.
- The value of this kind of exercise is building the habit of recognizing these connections yourself, not memorizing a fixed list of what applies.
