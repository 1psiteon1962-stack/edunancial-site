## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
- Explain what consideration generally requires beyond simply being written into a document
- Explain why a one-sided, unreciprocated promise generally isn't enforceable as a contract
- Explain the general concept of nominal consideration and its limits

## Core Content

Lesson 1 introduced consideration as one of the three basic elements of an enforceable contract. Here's the "why" this requirement is about genuine substance, not just formal appearance: [VERIFIED FACT] consideration generally requires each party to give up or provide something of value — this doesn't need to be monetary, and doesn't need to be of equal value between the parties, but it does generally need to reflect an actual, genuine exchange, not simply a one-sided promise with nothing given in return.

Here's why a purely one-sided promise generally fails this requirement: if one party promises to do something for the other with absolutely nothing expected or given in return, this resembles a gift or a gratuitous promise, which many legal systems generally don't treat as an enforceable contract, precisely because the mutual-exchange element consideration is meant to verify is missing.

[VERIFIED FACT] Some agreements include what's sometimes called "nominal consideration" — a token amount, like a specific small sum, included specifically to satisfy the formal consideration requirement. Courts in various jurisdictions have taken different approaches to whether purely nominal consideration, disconnected from any genuine exchange, is sufficient — this is an area where the specific facts and applicable jurisdiction's approach genuinely matter, rather than a settled, universal rule.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] One party promises to give a valuable item to another as a genuine gift, with nothing expected or given in return. Even if this promise is put in writing and both parties sign it, it likely lacks the consideration needed to be an enforceable contract — it resembles an unenforceable gift promise, not a genuine bargained-for exchange. [ASSUMPTION] If instead the two parties agreed that the item would be given in exchange for some specific service or payment, however modest, this mutual exchange would likely satisfy the consideration requirement, illustrating the difference between a one-sided promise and a genuine, even if modest, bargained-for exchange.

## Key Takeaways
- Consideration must reflect a genuine, mutual exchange of value, not simply appear formally in a written document.
- A one-sided promise with nothing given in return generally lacks the consideration needed for an enforceable contract.
- Nominal consideration's sufficiency, when disconnected from a genuine exchange, varies by jurisdiction and specific facts.
