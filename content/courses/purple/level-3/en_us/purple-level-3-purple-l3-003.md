## Core Content

A **breach of contract** occurs when a party fails to fulfill their obligations under an agreement. Here's the "why" not every breach is treated the same way: [VERIFIED FACT] many legal systems generally distinguish between a **material breach** — a significant failure that undermines the core purpose of the contract — and a **minor breach** — a less significant failure that doesn't defeat the contract's overall purpose, even though it's still technically a failure to perform exactly as agreed.

This distinction matters because it generally determines what remedy is available: a material breach may allow the non-breaching party to treat the entire contract as terminated and pursue damages for the full failure. A minor breach generally doesn't excuse the non-breaching party from their own remaining obligations under the contract — they may be entitled to damages for the specific, limited harm caused by the minor breach, but the underlying contract generally remains in effect and both parties are still expected to continue performing their remaining obligations.

This is why "the other party breached the contract" doesn't automatically mean "I'm free of all my own obligations under it" — the specific severity and nature of the breach matters significantly for what actually happens next.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A contract for a significant construction project specifies a completion date. If the contractor misses that date by a single day due to a minor, easily correctable issue, this might be treated as a minor breach — the property owner likely can't simply walk away from the entire contract, but might be entitled to a specific remedy for that limited delay, such as a small negotiated deduction, if the contract or applicable law provides for one. [ASSUMPTION] If instead the contractor completely abandons the project partway through, failing to deliver anything close to what was agreed, this would likely be treated as a material breach — undermining the contract's core purpose and potentially allowing the property owner to terminate the agreement entirely and pursue damages for the full failure.

## Practice Quiz

1. What is the general difference between a material breach and a minor breach?
2. Why does this distinction matter for what remedy is available?
3. Why doesn't every breach automatically excuse the non-breaching party from their own remaining obligations?

## Answer Key

1. A material breach significantly undermines the contract's core purpose; a minor breach is a less significant failure that doesn't defeat the contract's overall purpose.
2. Because a material breach may allow the non-breaching party to terminate the entire contract and pursue full damages, while a minor breach generally only entitles them to damages for the specific, limited harm, with the contract otherwise remaining in effect.
3. Because the law generally requires the breach to be significant enough to undermine the contract's core purpose before excusing the other party's own obligations entirely — a minor failure doesn't meet that threshold.

## Key Takeaways
- Many legal systems distinguish between a material breach (undermining the contract's core purpose) and a minor breach (a lesser failure).
- This distinction determines whether the non-breaching party can terminate the entire contract or is limited to damages for a specific, lesser harm.
- Not every breach frees the non-breaching party from their own remaining obligations — the severity of the breach matters significantly.
