By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
- Define specific performance as a contract remedy

## Core Content

Lesson 4 established that compensatory damages are the default contract remedy. **Specific performance** is a different, less common remedy: a court order requiring the breaching party to actually perform their specific obligations under the contract, rather than simply paying money damages.

Here's the "why" courts generally prefer money damages and treat specific performance as the exception, not the default: [VERIFIED FACT] courts generally consider money damages adequate to fully compensate a non-breaching party whenever a reasonable substitute for what was promised can be obtained elsewhere — if money can put the non-breaching party in essentially the same position, there's no need for the more intrusive remedy of ordering someone to actually perform.

Specific performance generally becomes more available specifically when the subject matter of the contract is considered unique, such that money damages genuinely couldn't provide an adequate substitute — real estate is a commonly cited example, since [VERIFIED FACT] many legal systems generally treat each specific parcel of real estate as unique, given that no two properties are in the exact same location, making a substitute property an inherently imperfect replacement that money alone might not adequately address.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A contract for the sale of mass-produced, readily available goods is breached by the seller. Because the buyer could reasonably purchase equivalent goods elsewhere, money damages covering any price difference or related costs are generally considered an adequate remedy — a court is unlikely to order the specific seller to perform this particular contract. [ASSUMPTION] A contract for the sale of a specific, unique parcel of real estate, by contrast, might be treated differently: because that specific property's location and characteristics can't be replicated by a substitute property, a court may be more willing to order specific performance, requiring the seller to actually complete that specific sale, since money damages alone might not adequately compensate for the loss of that unique property.

## Key Takeaways
- Specific performance is a court order requiring actual performance of a contract, rather than money damages.
- Courts generally prefer money damages, treating them as adequate whenever a reasonable substitute is available elsewhere.
- Specific performance becomes more available when the contract's subject matter is unique, such as real estate, where money alone may not provide an adequate substitute.
