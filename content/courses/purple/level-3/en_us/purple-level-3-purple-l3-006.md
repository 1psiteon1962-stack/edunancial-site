## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
- Define a liquidated damages clause
- Explain why courts generally require this type of clause to reflect a reasonable pre-estimate of actual harm
- Explain what happens if a liquidated damages clause is found to function as a penalty instead

## Core Content

A **liquidated damages clause** specifies, in advance within the contract itself, a specific dollar amount (or a formula for calculating one) that will be owed if a particular breach occurs, rather than requiring the actual harm to be separately calculated and proven later.

Here's the "why" courts don't simply enforce whatever amount the parties wrote down, connecting back to Lesson 4's compensation-not-punishment principle: [VERIFIED FACT] many legal systems generally require a liquidated damages clause to reflect a reasonable estimate of the actual harm likely to result from the specific breach, made at the time the contract was formed — if a court instead finds that the specified amount is wildly disproportionate to any plausible actual harm, functioning more as a punishment for breaching than a genuine pre-estimate of compensation, the clause may be found unenforceable as an improper penalty.

This directly echoes Lesson 4's principle that contract law generally aims to compensate, not punish — a liquidated damages clause is a tool for efficiently pre-calculating fair compensation in circumstances where actual harm might be difficult to prove precisely later, not a tool for building a punishment into the contract in advance.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A contract includes a liquidated damages clause specifying a dollar amount reasonably estimated, at the time of signing, to reflect the likely costs and losses that would result from a specific type of breach — perhaps a delay in completing a project with genuinely difficult-to-calculate downstream costs. This clause is likely enforceable, since it reflects a reasonable pre-estimate of actual, difficult-to-prove-later harm. [ASSUMPTION] If instead the same contract specified an amount vastly exceeding any plausible actual harm from that same breach — clearly intended to scare the other party into performing rather than to reasonably estimate compensation — a court might find this clause functions as an unenforceable penalty rather than a legitimate liquidated damages provision, striking it down.

## Key Takeaways
- A liquidated damages clause specifies contract damages in advance, rather than requiring later proof of actual harm.
- Courts generally require this amount to reflect a reasonable pre-estimate of actual harm, not an arbitrary or punitive figure.
- A clause found to function as a penalty, disproportionate to plausible actual harm, may be struck down as unenforceable.
