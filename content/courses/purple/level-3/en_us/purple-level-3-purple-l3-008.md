## Core Content

A **force majeure clause** generally excuses a party from performing their contractual obligations if a specific, listed category of extraordinary event beyond their control — commonly including things like natural disasters, war, or government action, depending on the specific clause's language — prevents performance.

Here's the "why" this clause is generally interpreted narrowly by courts: [VERIFIED FACT] many legal systems generally apply a principle of interpreting a force majeure clause based on the specific events it actually lists, rather than extending it broadly to cover any unforeseen difficulty a party might argue justifies non-performance — a clause listing specific events like "war" or "natural disaster" generally won't automatically be extended to excuse performance due to a type of disruption not reasonably falling within those specific listed categories, even if that disruption feels similarly extraordinary to the party invoking it.

This is why the specific drafting of a force majeure clause matters significantly: a party wanting protection against a broad range of possible disruptions needs to ensure the clause's language is drafted broadly and specifically enough to actually cover the situations they're concerned about, rather than assuming a generically worded clause will be interpreted expansively to cover whatever unexpected difficulty later arises.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A contract includes a force majeure clause listing specific events like natural disasters and government-ordered shutdowns. If a party's performance is prevented by an event clearly falling within one of these specifically listed categories, the clause likely excuses their performance as intended. [ASSUMPTION] If instead performance is disrupted by a different type of extraordinary event — one not reasonably falling within any of the clause's specifically listed categories — a court may find the clause doesn't apply, even though the disrupting event felt similarly extraordinary and beyond the party's control, illustrating why the specific listed language, not the general spirit of "unforeseen difficulty," determines the clause's actual protective scope.
