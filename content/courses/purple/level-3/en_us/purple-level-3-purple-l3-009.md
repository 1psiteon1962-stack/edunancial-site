## Core Content

A **Non-Disclosure Agreement (NDA)** generally restricts a party from disclosing information designated as confidential. Here's the "why" the specific definition of what actually counts as "confidential information" within the agreement is arguably the single most important part of the entire document: every other protection and restriction in the NDA — what can't be disclosed, for how long, and under what circumstances — only applies to information that actually falls within this definition.

[VERIFIED FACT] An overly narrow definition can leave genuinely sensitive information unprotected if it doesn't technically fit within the specific defined scope. An overly broad definition — sometimes covering essentially everything shared between the parties, regardless of whether it's genuinely sensitive — can create its own problems, potentially making the agreement difficult to comply with in practice and, in some cases, less likely to be strictly enforced by a court if found to be unreasonably broad.

This is why well-drafted NDAs typically include specific, carefully considered exclusions from the confidentiality definition — commonly excluding information that was already publicly known, information the receiving party already independently possessed before disclosure, or information independently developed without reference to the disclosed confidential information — ensuring the agreement's scope is reasonable and genuinely tied to protecting legitimately sensitive information.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] Two businesses negotiate an NDA before sharing sensitive business information. If the definition of "confidential information" is drafted too narrowly, specific important information shared later might not technically qualify for protection, leaving it exposed despite the parties' apparent intent to protect it. If the definition is instead drafted so broadly that it covers virtually anything ever mentioned between the parties, including publicly available information, [ASSUMPTION] a court might view the agreement's enforcement as impractical or overreaching in a specific dispute. A carefully drafted definition, with reasonable, standard exclusions built in, avoids both of these problems, providing meaningful, appropriately scoped protection.

## Key Takeaways
- The definition of "confidential information" is the most consequential part of an NDA, since it determines the scope of every other protection.
- An overly narrow definition can leave sensitive information unprotected; an overly broad one can create enforcement problems.
- Well-drafted NDAs typically include reasonable, standard exclusions to keep the scope appropriately focused.
