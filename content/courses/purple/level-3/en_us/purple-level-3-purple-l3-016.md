## Core Content

A **fiduciary duty** generally requires a person or entity in a position of trust to act in the best interests of another party, rather than in their own self-interest, when making decisions affecting that other party.

Here's the "why" this creates a fundamentally different, stricter legal standard than an ordinary business relationship: [VERIFIED FACT] most ordinary business relationships operate on an arm's-length basis, where each party is generally expected and legally permitted to look out for their own interests, within the bounds of honesty and the specific terms of any agreement between them. A fiduciary relationship is different: the fiduciary is generally required to actively prioritize the other party's interests, often including specific duties like a **duty of loyalty** (avoiding conflicts of interest, covered further in the next lesson) and a **duty of care** (making decisions with appropriate diligence and skill).

Common examples of relationships that typically involve a fiduciary duty include a trustee's relationship to a trust's beneficiaries, a corporate director's or officer's relationship to the corporation and its shareholders, and an investment advisor's relationship to their clients, among others that vary by jurisdiction and specific legal context.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] In an ordinary business negotiation between two companies, each side is generally expected to advocate for its own best interests, within the bounds of honesty — this is a standard arm's-length relationship. A trustee managing assets on behalf of a trust's beneficiaries, by contrast, is held to a fiduciary standard: [ASSUMPTION] the trustee must make decisions genuinely in the beneficiaries' best interests, even if a different decision might have been more convenient or beneficial to the trustee personally — a fundamentally stricter standard than the self-interested advocacy generally permitted in the ordinary business negotiation.
