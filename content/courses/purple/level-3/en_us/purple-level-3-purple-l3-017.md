## Core Content

Lesson 16 introduced the duty of loyalty as a component of fiduciary duty. A **conflict of interest** arises when a fiduciary's personal interests (or interests owed to another party) could potentially compete with the interests of the party they owe a fiduciary duty to.

Here's the "why" a conflict of interest can be a legal problem even without any actual bad intent or resulting harm: [VERIFIED FACT] many legal systems generally treat the mere existence of an undisclosed, unmanaged conflict as a breach of the duty of loyalty in itself, separate from whether the fiduciary actually acted on that conflict to cause harm — the concern is that even a well-intentioned fiduciary's judgment could be, consciously or unconsciously, influenced by a competing interest, making the conflict itself, not just any resulting bad outcome, the legal problem.

This is why disclosure is generally an important, though not always fully sufficient, part of addressing a conflict of interest: proactively disclosing a potential conflict to the party owed the fiduciary duty, and in some cases obtaining that party's informed consent to proceed despite the conflict, is generally treated very differently than concealing the conflict — though some conflicts may be considered too significant to be cured by disclosure and consent alone, depending on the specific circumstances and applicable rules.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A financial advisor owing a fiduciary duty to a client has a personal financial interest in recommending a specific investment product, separate from what might genuinely be the best option for the client. Even if the advisor genuinely believes, in good faith, that the product is a fine choice for the client, [ASSUMPTION] the undisclosed existence of the advisor's own competing financial interest represents a conflict of interest that could constitute a breach of the duty of loyalty, regardless of the advisor's good intentions or whether the client actually suffers any measurable harm from the specific recommendation. Proactively disclosing this competing interest to the client, allowing the client to evaluate the recommendation with full information, addresses the problem far better than concealing it, even if it doesn't automatically resolve every possible concern.

2. Proactively disclosing a conflict, and in some cases obtaining informed consent, is generally treated very differently than concealing it, though some conflicts may be too significant to be cured by disclosure alone.
3. A conflict of interest is a specific example of a potential breach of the duty of loyalty, which Lesson 16 identified as a component of the broader fiduciary duty concept.

## Key Takeaways
- A conflict of interest can create legal liability even without any actual bad intent or resulting harm, since the conflict itself is the concern.
- Proactive disclosure, and sometimes informed consent, is an important part of addressing a conflict, though not always fully sufficient on its own.
- A conflict of interest is a specific application of the broader duty of loyalty introduced in Lesson 16.
