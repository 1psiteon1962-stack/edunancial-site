## Core Content

This curriculum's BLUE track covers intellectual property's contribution to a business's overall value. Here's a foundational legal rule underlying that discussion: [VERIFIED FACT] in many jurisdictions, the general default rule is that the actual **creator** of a work — the person who wrote the code, created the design, or authored the content — is the initial owner of the resulting intellectual property, regardless of who paid for the work to be done, absent a specific agreement transferring that ownership.

Here's the "why" this surprises many business owners: it's intuitive to assume that paying someone to create something automatically means the payer owns what's created, similar to how paying for a physical product means owning that product. Intellectual property generally works differently by default — the creator's authorship generally establishes their ownership unless a specific contract or, in some cases, a specific legal exception (covered in the next lesson) transfers or reassigns that ownership to someone else.

This is why any business hiring freelancers, contractors, or even certain categories of employees to create intellectual property needs a specific, clear written agreement addressing IP ownership — assuming that payment alone automatically transfers ownership can lead to the business not actually owning the very work it paid to have created.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A business hires an independent freelance designer to create a specific logo, paying the agreed fee upon completion, but without any written agreement specifically addressing IP ownership. [ASSUMPTION] Under the general default rule, the freelance designer, as the actual creator, may retain ownership of the underlying intellectual property in the design, despite having been paid for the work — the business may have only a limited license to use the logo, not full ownership, unless the original agreement specifically and clearly transferred that ownership to the business.
