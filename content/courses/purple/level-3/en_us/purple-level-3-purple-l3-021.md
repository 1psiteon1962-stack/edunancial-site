## Core Content

A **patent** generally grants an inventor the exclusive right to make, use, or sell a specific invention for a limited period, in exchange for publicly disclosing exactly how the invention works. Here's the "why" this specific trade-off exists, sometimes called the patent bargain: [VERIFIED FACT] patent systems are generally designed to encourage innovation by rewarding inventors with a temporary, exclusive right, while also ensuring that society ultimately benefits from the knowledge of how the invention works, since that information is publicly disclosed as part of the patent application process, becoming available to the public (and other innovators to build upon) once the patent eventually expires.

This is why a patent generally has a fixed, limited term — [VERIFIED FACT] unlike copyright or trademark's different duration structures, a patent's exclusivity period is generally set for a specific number of years from filing or grant, varying by patent type and jurisdiction, after which the invention generally becomes available for anyone to use, reflecting the bargain's intended balance between rewarding the inventor and eventually benefiting the broader public.

This differs meaningfully from Lesson 20's trademark discussion: a patent's protection generally doesn't depend on the inventor actually using the invention commercially, and doesn't extend or renew based on continued use — it simply expires after its fixed term, regardless of how it was or wasn't used during that period.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An inventor files a patent application, publicly disclosing exactly how a new invention works as part of that process. In exchange for this disclosure, the inventor receives an exclusive right to make, use, or sell the invention for a specific, fixed period. [ASSUMPTION] Once that fixed period expires, the invention generally becomes available for anyone to use, since the underlying knowledge was already publicly disclosed years earlier — society benefits from that disclosed knowledge both during the patent's exclusivity period (through licensing or the inventor's own commercialization) and afterward (through open access), reflecting the fundamental bargain this lesson describes.

## Practice Quiz

1. What is the fundamental bargain underlying patent law?
2. Why does patent protection have a fixed, limited term rather than lasting indefinitely?
3. How does a patent's duration structure differ from the trademark continued-use requirement discussed in Lesson 20?

## Answer Key

1. An inventor receives a temporary, exclusive right to their invention in exchange for publicly disclosing exactly how it works, so society eventually benefits from that knowledge.
2. Because the system is designed to balance rewarding the inventor's temporary exclusivity with eventually making the disclosed knowledge available for broader public use and innovation.
3. A patent's exclusivity is set for a fixed term regardless of the inventor's actual commercial use; trademark protection instead depends on continued, active use and can be lost through abandonment if that use stops.

## Key Takeaways
- Patent law generally trades a temporary, exclusive right for public disclosure of how an invention works.
- This is why patents have a fixed, limited term, after which the invention generally becomes available for public use.
- Unlike trademarks, a patent's protection doesn't depend on continued commercial use during its fixed term.
