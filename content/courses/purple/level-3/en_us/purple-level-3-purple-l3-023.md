## Core Content

A **will** generally directs how a person's assets should be distributed after death, and names an executor to carry out those instructions. Here's the "why" a will doesn't actually cover everything a person owns, a common and important misconception: [VERIFIED FACT] certain categories of assets generally pass according to their own specific rules or designations, regardless of what a will separately states, effectively bypassing the will entirely for those specific assets.

Common examples generally include assets with a designated beneficiary — life insurance policies and many retirement accounts typically transfer directly to whoever is named as beneficiary on the account itself, regardless of conflicting instructions in a will — and assets held in certain joint ownership forms (a topic explored in more depth in Lesson 30), which may pass automatically to a surviving co-owner outside of the will's instructions entirely.

This is why comprehensive estate planning generally requires more than simply writing a will: [VERIFIED FACT] beneficiary designations on relevant accounts need to be reviewed and kept current, and how various assets are actually titled matters significantly — an outdated beneficiary designation from years earlier can override even a much more recently updated will's stated intentions for that same specific asset, creating results the person may not have actually intended.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A person updates their will to leave a larger share of their overall estate to a specific family member, but forgets to also update the beneficiary designation on a life insurance policy from years earlier, which still names a different, previous beneficiary. [ASSUMPTION] Upon that person's death, the life insurance proceeds will generally go directly to whoever is named on the policy's own beneficiary designation, regardless of the updated will's different, more recent intentions for the overall estate — illustrating why reviewing and updating beneficiary designations is a genuinely separate, essential task from simply updating a will.

## Practice Quiz

1. Why doesn't a will actually cover every type of asset a person owns?

## Answer Key

1. Because certain categories of assets pass according to their own specific rules or designations, regardless of what a will separately states.
2. Any two of: assets with a designated beneficiary (life insurance, many retirement accounts), assets held in certain joint ownership forms.
3. Because beneficiary designations and asset titling need to be independently reviewed and kept current, since an outdated designation can override a more recently updated will for that specific asset.

## Key Takeaways
- A will doesn't govern every asset a person owns — certain assets pass according to their own specific designations or ownership rules.
- Common examples include beneficiary-designated accounts (life insurance, retirement accounts) and certain joint ownership forms.
- Comprehensive estate planning requires independently reviewing and keeping beneficiary designations and asset titling current, not just updating a will.
