## Core Content

This curriculum's GREEN track covers charitable trusts specifically in a tax-planning context. Here's a more general legal "why" behind trusts, relevant to estate planning broadly: [VERIFIED FACT] assets properly transferred into a trust during the trust creator's lifetime are generally no longer considered part of that person's individually owned estate for probate purposes — since the trust itself, not the individual, technically owns those assets, the trust's own terms (not the probate process, covered in the next lesson) generally govern how and when those assets are distributed to beneficiaries.

This is the "why" trusts are commonly used specifically to avoid probate: since the assets are already held by the trust rather than by the individual personally at the time of death, there's generally no need for those specific assets to pass through the court-supervised probate process that applies to a person's individually owned assets not otherwise addressed by beneficiary designations or joint ownership (as discussed in Lesson 23).

This connects directly to Lesson 23's point about certain assets bypassing a will: a properly funded trust is another category of asset arrangement that generally operates independently of, and bypasses, the standard probate process that a will's instructions would otherwise be subject to.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A person transfers ownership of a specific property into a trust during their lifetime, with the trust's own terms specifying how that property should eventually be distributed to named beneficiaries. [ASSUMPTION] Upon that person's death, this specific property generally passes according to the trust's own terms, without needing to go through the probate process — a meaningfully different, generally faster and more private path than if that same property had instead remained in the person's individual name, subject to their will and the standard probate process, as covered in the next lesson.
