## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

- Contrast this directly with the trust-based approach covered in Lesson 24

## Core Content

Lesson 24 explained how trust-held assets generally avoid probate. Here's the "why" **probate** itself exists as a distinct, court-supervised process for the assets that don't have that advantage: probate generally exists to formally validate a will (if one exists), identify and inventory the deceased person's assets, ensure valid debts and taxes are paid, and oversee the distribution of remaining assets to the correct beneficiaries or heirs, all under a court's formal supervision.

[VERIFIED FACT] This court supervision serves a protective purpose — providing an official, formal process for resolving potential disputes about a will's validity, identifying and addressing legitimate creditor claims, and ensuring assets are actually distributed correctly and lawfully. This process is also generally a matter of **public record** in most jurisdictions, meaning the general contents of the estate and its distribution can typically be accessed by interested members of the public, unlike a private trust's terms.

This is why probate and trust-based distribution differ meaningfully beyond just the timing and cost considerations often discussed: probate provides formal, public, court-supervised protections and dispute resolution, while a trust generally provides a more private, and often faster, distribution process that operates largely outside of direct court supervision — each with legitimate trade-offs depending on a person's specific goals and concerns, such as privacy versus formal dispute-resolution protection.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A person's estate, consisting primarily of individually owned assets not held in a trust, goes through the probate process after their death. The court formally validates their will, oversees the identification and payment of valid debts, and supervises the distribution of remaining assets to the named beneficiaries — a process that, being a matter of public record, could in principle be reviewed by an interested member of the public. [ASSUMPTION] A different person, whose assets were instead held in a properly funded trust, sees those specific assets distributed according to the trust's own private terms, without the same court supervision or public record exposure — illustrating the meaningful contrast this lesson draws with Lesson 24's discussion.

## Practice Quiz

1. What is the general purpose of the probate process?
2. Why is probate generally court-supervised?
3. Why is probate generally a matter of public record, unlike a trust's terms?

## Answer Key

1. To formally validate a will, identify and inventory assets, ensure valid debts and taxes are paid, and oversee correct, lawful distribution of remaining assets to beneficiaries or heirs.
2. To provide an official, formal process for resolving potential disputes about a will's validity, addressing legitimate creditor claims, and ensuring assets are distributed correctly and lawfully.
3. Because it's a court proceeding, and court proceedings are generally treated as public record in most jurisdictions, unlike the private terms of a trust.

## Answers and Explanations

1. This is the foundational purpose for the lesson.
2. This explains the protective rationale behind court supervision.
3. This reinforces the specific privacy contrast with the trust-based approach from Lesson 24.

## Key Takeaways
- Probate is a court-supervised process for validating a will, addressing debts, and overseeing lawful asset distribution.
- Court supervision provides formal, protective dispute-resolution and creditor-claim processes.
- Probate is generally a matter of public record, unlike a trust's private terms, representing a meaningful trade-off between formal protection and privacy.
