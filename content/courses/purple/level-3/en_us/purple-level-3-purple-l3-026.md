## Core Content

A **revocable trust** (sometimes called a living trust) is a trust that its creator can generally modify, amend, or completely revoke at any time during their own lifetime, so long as they remain mentally competent to do so.

Here's the "why" this flexibility matters, combined with the probate-avoidance benefit discussed in Lesson 24: unlike an irrevocable trust (covered in the next lesson), a revocable trust doesn't lock the creator into permanent, unchangeable terms — [VERIFIED FACT] the creator can generally add or remove assets, change beneficiaries, or dissolve the trust entirely as their circumstances or wishes change over time, all while the assets held in the trust still generally avoid probate upon the creator's eventual death, exactly as Lesson 24 described.

This combination — lifetime flexibility plus probate avoidance at death — is a significant part of why revocable trusts are commonly used as a core estate planning tool for many people, offering a middle ground between the rigidity of certain other planning tools and the specific asset-protection benefits (discussed in the next lesson) that come only with giving up that same flexibility.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A person creates a revocable trust, transferring several assets into it and naming specific beneficiaries. Years later, as their circumstances change, they modify the trust to add a newly acquired asset and update the named beneficiaries to reflect a change in family circumstances. [ASSUMPTION] This ongoing flexibility to adjust the trust's terms throughout their lifetime, combined with the fact that the trust's assets will still generally avoid probate upon their eventual death, illustrates why a revocable trust offers a practical combination of benefits that a will alone, or a more rigid irrevocable structure, wouldn't provide in the same way.
