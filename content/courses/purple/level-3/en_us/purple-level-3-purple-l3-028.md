## Core Content

A **power of attorney (POA)** is a legal document authorizing a named person (the "agent" or "attorney-in-fact") to act on behalf of another person (the "principal") in specified matters. Here's the "why" the document's specific scope and language matters enormously: an agent's actual legal authority under a power of attorney is generally limited to exactly what the document itself specifies — an agent generally cannot act beyond the scope actually granted, regardless of what the principal might have informally intended or the agent might assume is reasonable.

[VERIFIED FACT] A **general power of attorney** generally grants broad authority across most or all of the principal's financial and legal affairs, while a **limited (or special) power of attorney** generally grants authority for a specific, defined purpose or transaction only — the difference in scope is significant, and choosing the appropriate type, with carefully drafted language matching the principal's actual intentions, matters considerably.

This is why a poorly drafted or ambiguously worded power of attorney can create real problems: if the document's language doesn't clearly cover a specific situation that later arises, the agent may lack the actual legal authority to act on the principal's behalf in that specific circumstance, even if it seems like exactly the kind of situation the principal would have wanted addressed.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A person grants a limited power of attorney to a trusted family member, specifically authorizing that person to handle a single, defined real estate transaction on their behalf while they're unavailable. [ASSUMPTION] If a different, unrelated financial matter unexpectedly arises during that same period, the family member generally has no legal authority to act on that unrelated matter under this specific limited power of attorney, since its scope was narrowly defined to cover only the specific real estate transaction — illustrating why the document's precise scope, not the agent's general trustworthiness or the principal's informal intentions, determines what the agent can actually legally do.

## Key Takeaways
- A power of attorney authorizes a named agent to act on behalf of a principal, but only within the document's specified scope.
- A general power of attorney grants broad authority; a limited power of attorney grants authority for a specific, defined purpose only.
