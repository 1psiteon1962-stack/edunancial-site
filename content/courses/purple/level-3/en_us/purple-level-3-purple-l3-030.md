## Core Content

When two or more people co-own property, they generally do so under one of several possible legal structures, with **joint tenancy** and **tenancy in common** among the most common. Here's the "why" the specific structure chosen matters enormously, particularly connecting back to Lesson 23's discussion of assets that pass outside of a will: [VERIFIED FACT] joint tenancy generally includes a **right of survivorship** — when one joint tenant dies, their ownership share generally passes automatically to the surviving joint tenant(s), regardless of what that deceased person's will might separately state. Tenancy in common generally does not include this automatic survivorship feature — each owner's share generally passes according to their own will (or applicable inheritance law if no will exists) upon their death, rather than automatically to the other co-owners.

This is a direct, concrete example of Lesson 23's broader point: joint tenancy is another specific category of asset ownership that generally bypasses a will's instructions entirely for that particular property, exactly the kind of exception that lesson warned needs to be understood and accounted for in comprehensive estate planning.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] Two people co-own a property as joint tenants with right of survivorship. When one of them dies, their ownership share automatically passes to the surviving co-owner, regardless of what the deceased person's will might have separately stated about that property. [ASSUMPTION] If instead the same two people had co-owned the property as tenants in common, the deceased person's ownership share would instead pass according to their own will (or applicable inheritance law), potentially to someone other than the surviving co-owner — illustrating why choosing between these two ownership structures has a direct, significant, and different impact on what actually happens to a shared property upon one owner's death.
