## Core Content

This track has covered contracts extensively as generally negotiable agreements between parties. Here's the "why" a residential lease agreement is a meaningful exception to fully flexible contract negotiation: [VERIFIED FACT] many jurisdictions have specific landlord-tenant statutes that establish mandatory minimum protections and requirements — covering areas like habitability standards, security deposit handling, notice requirements for entry or termination, and specific eviction procedures (covered in the next lesson) — that generally apply regardless of what a specific lease agreement might otherwise state.

Here's the "why" these specific protections generally can't simply be waived or contracted away, unlike many other ordinary contract terms: landlord-tenant statutes are generally designed to protect tenants, who are often considered to have less bargaining power in this specific relationship, from lease terms that might otherwise be significantly one-sided — a lease clause attempting to waive a tenant's statutorily guaranteed right (such as a right to a habitable living space) may simply be unenforceable, regardless of the tenant's apparent agreement to it in writing.

This is why understanding the specific landlord-tenant statute applicable in a given jurisdiction matters as much as, or more than, understanding the specific lease document itself — the statute generally sets a mandatory floor of protections that the lease operates within, rather than the lease alone determining every aspect of the relationship.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A lease agreement includes a clause attempting to waive the tenant's right to a habitable living space, or attempting to bypass legally required notice periods for entry by the landlord. [ASSUMPTION] Even if the tenant signed this lease, a court applying the applicable landlord-tenant statute might find these specific clauses unenforceable, since they attempt to waive protections the statute generally guarantees regardless of what the lease itself states — illustrating why the statute, not just the lease document, ultimately governs significant aspects of this specific type of relationship.
