## Core Content

Lesson 35 established that a worker's true legal classification depends on the substance of the working relationship, not just a contractual label. Here's the "why" getting this classification wrong carries genuine, significant risk for a business: [VERIFIED FACT] if a worker labeled as an independent contractor is later determined to have actually been an employee, the business can generally face liability for unpaid payroll taxes (connecting directly to the payroll tax and trust fund tax discussion covered in this curriculum's GREEN track), unpaid overtime and minimum wage obligations, unpaid benefits the worker should have received, and in some cases, additional penalties specifically for the misclassification itself.

This connects directly to the trust fund tax concept covered elsewhere in this curriculum: payroll taxes that should have been withheld and remitted for a misclassified employee generally still become owed retroactively, potentially along with the same personal liability exposure for responsible business owners discussed in that context, even though the business believed, whether reasonably or not, that it was dealing with an independent contractor with no such withholding obligation at the time.

This is why the misclassification risk discussed in Lesson 35 isn't merely a technical labeling issue — it's a genuine financial and legal exposure that can result in a business owing substantial retroactive amounts, well after the fact, based on how a working relationship is later determined to have actually functioned in substance.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A business has treated a worker as an independent contractor for several years, based on a written agreement using that label, without withholding payroll taxes or providing employee benefits. [ASSUMPTION] If a government agency or court later determines that the actual working relationship met the substantive test for employee status, the business could face liability for years of unpaid payroll taxes, unpaid overtime, and potentially penalties specifically for the misclassification — a substantial, retroactive financial exposure connecting directly to the trust fund tax risk covered elsewhere in this curriculum, arising from a classification decision made years earlier that turned out to be legally incorrect.
