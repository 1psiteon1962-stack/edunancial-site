## Core Content

Lesson 37 identified termination based on a "protected characteristic" as a key exception to at-will employment. Here's the "why" this protection is generally limited to specific, legally defined categories, rather than covering any treatment an employee might subjectively feel is unfair: [VERIFIED FACT] discrimination law generally identifies specific protected categories — commonly including race, color, religion, sex, national origin, age (often above a certain threshold), and disability, among others that vary by jurisdiction — and prohibits adverse employment decisions based specifically on membership in one of these defined categories.

Here's the "why" this list is deliberately specific and fixed, rather than an open-ended prohibition on any unfair treatment: [VERIFIED FACT] employment decisions based on many other factors — job performance, skills, experience, personality fit, or simply an employer's general business judgment — remain generally legal, even if an affected employee feels the decision was unfair or poorly reasoned; discrimination law specifically targets decisions based on the defined protected categories, not a general legal standard of workplace fairness.

This is why a termination or other adverse employment action, even one that feels genuinely unfair to the affected employee, isn't automatically illegal discrimination unless it's specifically connected to one of these defined protected categories — the law draws a meaningful line between illegal discrimination and other decisions that, while potentially unwise or unfair, don't fall within the law's specific, defined protections.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An employee is terminated and genuinely believes the decision was unfair, poorly reasoned, and based on factors that don't reflect their actual job performance. [ASSUMPTION] If this termination was actually based on general business judgment, personality fit, or a supervisor's subjective (even if arguably unfair) assessment, unconnected to any legally protected category, it likely remains legal despite feeling unjust to the affected employee. If instead the termination was actually motivated by the employee's membership in one of the law's specifically defined protected categories — race, religion, age, disability, or similar — it would constitute illegal discrimination, regardless of how the employer might try to characterize or justify the decision.

## Practice Quiz

1. Why does discrimination law generally protect specific, defined categories rather than any unfair treatment?
2. Name three commonly protected categories under employment discrimination law, noting they vary by jurisdiction.
3. Is a termination that feels genuinely unfair to the affected employee automatically illegal discrimination?

## Answer Key

1. Because the law specifically targets adverse decisions based on defined protected categories, while other employment decisions based on performance, skills, or general business judgment remain generally legal even if subjectively felt as unfair.
2. Any three of: race, color, religion, sex, national origin, age, disability (varies by jurisdiction).
3. No — it's illegal discrimination only if the decision is specifically connected to a legally defined protected category; other unfair, but non-discriminatory, reasons generally remain legal.

## Answers and Explanations

## Key Takeaways
- Discrimination law generally protects specific, defined categories, not a general standard of workplace fairness.
- Commonly protected categories include race, religion, sex, national origin, age, and disability, varying by jurisdiction.
- An unfair-feeling employment decision is illegal discrimination only if connected to one of these specifically defined protected categories.
