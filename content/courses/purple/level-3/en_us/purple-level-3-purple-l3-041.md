## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
- Define a statute of limitations
- Explain the general policy reasoning behind imposing a time limit on legal claims
- Explain why an otherwise valid claim can become permanently barred if the deadline passes

## Core Content

A **statute of limitations** generally sets a specific time limit within which a legal claim must be filed, after which the claim is generally barred entirely, regardless of its underlying merit. [VERIFIED FACT] Specific time limits vary significantly by jurisdiction and by the type of claim involved — different categories of legal claims (contract disputes, personal injury claims, and others) often have different specific limitation periods.

Here's the general "why" this time limit exists: [VERIFIED FACT] statutes of limitations are generally intended to encourage claims to be brought while evidence and witness memories are still relatively fresh and reliable, and to provide potential defendants some eventual certainty and repose, rather than facing the possibility of a lawsuit indefinitely, for events that may have occurred many years earlier.

This is why an otherwise entirely valid, meritorious claim can become permanently barred simply because it was filed after the applicable deadline passed — the claim's underlying merit generally becomes legally irrelevant once the statute of limitations has run, meaning understanding and tracking the applicable deadline matters just as much as the strength of the underlying claim itself.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A person has a genuinely valid legal claim arising from a specific event, but doesn't take action to file that claim until well after the applicable statute of limitations for that type of claim has already expired. [ASSUMPTION] Even though the underlying claim might have been entirely meritorious, a court would likely dismiss it as time-barred, since the statute of limitations deadline had already passed — illustrating why the timing of filing a claim can matter just as much as, or even more than, the actual strength of the claim's underlying facts.
