## Core Content

[HYPOTHETICAL SCENARIO — ASSUMPTION, NOT VERIFIED FACT] Imagine a significant, unexpected regional infrastructure disruption prevents many local businesses from operating normally for an extended period. This is a hypothetical stress-test scenario, not a prediction about any specific real event, disaster, or region.

In this hypothetical, the same basic legal question — was performance excused by this disruption — plays out differently across several types of legal relationships covered throughout this track. Under a commercial lease (Lesson 31), the tenant might look to whether the lease's specific force majeure or similar clause covers this exact type of disruption (Lesson 8's narrow-interpretation principle), and separately, whether the disruption affected the property's basic habitability or usability in a way that triggers other statutory protections. Under a business supply contract, each party might look to whether their own force majeure clause specifically lists this type of event, with Lesson 8's principle meaning a clause listing only "natural disasters" might not automatically extend to this specific kind of infrastructure disruption, even if it feels similarly extraordinary. Under an insurance policy (Lesson 45), a business seeking coverage for resulting losses would need to examine the policy's specific definitions and exclusions to determine whether this particular type of event and resulting loss actually falls within covered categories.

## Worked Example

[HYPOTHETICAL — ILLUSTRATIVE, NOT A PREDICTION] A business affected by this hypothetical disruption reviews three separate contracts to understand its position: its commercial lease's force majeure clause, which lists specific events that may or may not include this exact type of disruption; its own supply contracts with customers, each with their own separately negotiated force majeure language; and its business insurance policy, with its own specific definitions and exclusions determining whether resulting losses are covered. In each case, the business finds that the outcome depends entirely on the specific language of that particular document — a favorable outcome under one contract's language doesn't guarantee a similarly favorable outcome under a different contract's separately negotiated terms, even though all three relate to the exact same underlying disruptive event.
