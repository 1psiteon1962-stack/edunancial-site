## Main Lesson

### 1. Hard Money Loans

Hard money loans are short-term loans from private lenders or companies, typically secured by the property itself and underwritten more on the deal's value than the borrower's personal credit. [ESTIMATE] Hard money loans commonly carry higher interest rates and shorter terms than conventional mortgages, though exact terms vary by lender.

### 2. Private Money

Private money refers to loans from individuals — friends, family, or other investors — rather than institutions, typically with terms negotiated directly between the two parties. [VERIFIED FACT] Because terms are privately negotiated, they can vary enormously; a written agreement is broadly recommended.

### 3. Seller Financing

In seller financing, the property seller acts as the lender, allowing the buyer to make payments directly to them instead of (or alongside) a traditional mortgage. [VERIFIED FACT] Seller financing terms, including interest rate and down payment, are negotiated between buyer and seller and vary case by case.

### 4. When Alternative Financing Is Used

These options are often used when a property needs renovation before it can qualify for conventional financing, when speed is critical to winning a deal, or when a borrower doesn't yet meet conventional lending criteria. [OPINION] Many investors treat alternative financing as a bridge to eventually refinancing into a conventional loan, though this is a strategic choice, not a rule.

## Key Definitions

- **Hard Money Loan:** A short-term loan from a private lender, typically secured by the property and underwritten primarily on the deal's value.
- **Private Money:** A loan from an individual (rather than an institution) with privately negotiated terms.
- **Seller Financing:** A financing arrangement where the seller acts as lender, receiving payments directly from the buyer.
- **Bridge Financing:** Short-term financing used to 'bridge' a gap until longer-term or conventional financing can be obtained.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An investor buying a distressed property that doesn't qualify for a conventional loan might use a hard money loan to purchase and renovate it, then refinance into a conventional mortgage once the property qualifies.

## Common Mistakes

- Using hard money for a long-term hold without a clear refinance plan.
- Not getting a private money agreement in writing.
- Ignoring the higher cost of alternative financing when calculating deal profitability.
- Assuming seller financing terms will resemble conventional mortgage terms.

## FAQ

**Q: Is hard money a bad option?**
It's a tool, not inherently bad — it serves specific use cases (speed, flexibility, deals that don't yet qualify conventionally) but typically costs more than conventional financing.

**Q: Do I need a lawyer for private or seller financing?**
Having an attorney review or draft the agreement is broadly recommended given how much these terms can vary and how significant the transaction is.

**Q: Can seller financing be combined with a conventional loan?**
In some structures yes, though this depends on the specific deal and any restrictions from a primary lender; verify with your financing professionals.

## Key Takeaways

- Alternative financing includes hard money, private money, and seller financing, each with different structures.
- These options typically cost more than conventional financing but offer speed or flexibility.
- Written agreements are strongly recommended for private and seller financing.
- Alternative financing is often used as a bridge to eventual conventional refinancing.
