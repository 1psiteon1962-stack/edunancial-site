## Executive Summary

Every property you'll ever look at as a real estate investor gets sorted into one of two worlds the moment you count its units: 1–4 units, or 5+ units. That single number — the unit count — decides which lender you talk to, how the property gets valued, what paperwork you sign, and even what kind of loan you qualify for. This lesson teaches you where that line sits, why it exists, and how to think about a property differently depending on which side of the line it falls on.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
1. State the exact unit-count threshold that separates residential from commercial real estate financing.
2. Explain how the valuation method changes once a property crosses that threshold.
3. Identify which loan type applies to a property based on its unit count.
4. Apply this threshold when evaluating a real property scenario.

## Main Lesson

### Topic 1: The Number That Changes Everything

In real estate, "residential" and "commercial" don't just describe how a property looks or how it's used day to day. They describe two entirely separate financing and valuation systems. The dividing line is **unit count**:

- **1 to 4 units** = Residential (single-family home, duplex, triplex, fourplex)
- **5 or more units** = Commercial (apartment building, commercial multifamily)

**VERIFIED FACT:** This 1–4 vs. 5+ threshold is the standard used by Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, and FHA for classifying residential vs. commercial multifamily lending in the United States.

This isn't a minor technicality. Cross that line from 4 units to 5, and almost nothing about how you finance and value the property works the same way anymore.

### Topic 2: How Valuation Changes at the Threshold

You learned in RED-L1 that residential property (1–4 units) is valued using **comps** — comparable sales of similar properties nearby, adjusted for differences in size, condition, and features.

Once a property hits 5+ units, comps mostly stop being the primary valuation tool. Commercial multifamily is valued based on **income** — specifically, the property's **Net Operating Income (NOI)** and the **capitalization rate (cap rate)** investors expect for that type of property in that market.

**Simplified relationship:** Value ≈ NOI ÷ Cap Rate

**ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE:** A 4-unit fourplex gets valued mostly by what similar fourplexes sold for nearby in the last 6–12 months. A 12-unit apartment building gets valued mostly by how much rental income it produces after expenses, divided by the market's expected rate of return for that asset class. Two buildings could look almost identical from the street and be valued using completely different math, simply because one has 4 units and the other has 12.

This matters because it changes what you're actually shopping for. With a 1–4 unit property, you're partly buying "what the market says similar homes are worth." With a 5+ unit property, you're buying a stream of income, priced like any other income-producing asset.

### Topic 3: How Financing Changes at the Threshold

The unit-count line also determines what kind of loan you can get.

**1–4 units (residential):**
- Conventional mortgages, FHA loans, VA loans (covered in RED-L2-002) are all available
- Underwriting looks heavily at YOUR personal income, credit, and debt-to-income ratio
- Loan terms are commonly 15 or 30 years, fixed-rate options widely available
- Owner-occupants can often put down as little as 3–5%; investors typically need more

**5+ units (commercial):**
- Requires a commercial mortgage or commercial multifamily loan
- Underwriting looks heavily at the PROPERTY's income (NOI, debt service coverage ratio), not just your personal finances
- Loan terms are often shorter (5, 7, or 10 years) with a balloon payment, or amortized over 20–25 years
- Down payments are typically higher, often 20–30%+
- Commercial lenders often require the property (not just the borrower) to demonstrate it can comfortably cover its own debt payments — this is measured by the **Debt Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR)**

**REGION-SPECIFIC RULE:** Specific down payment percentages, loan terms, and DSCR requirements vary by lender, country, and local banking regulation. The figures above are general U.S. market patterns, not universal rules or guarantees from any specific lender.

### Topic 4: Why the Threshold Exists

This isn't an arbitrary line bureaucrats drew for no reason. The logic behind it:

A 1–4 unit property is still small enough that it behaves like a single-family home in the eyes of a lender — it's something an individual owner-occupant might realistically live in, and its value tracks closely with nearby home sales. A 5+ unit property starts to behave like a small business: it generates its own revenue, has its own operating expenses (maintenance, management, vacancy), and its ability to pay back a loan depends on how well that "business" performs, not just on the borrower's personal paycheck.

Government-backed loan programs (FHA, VA, conventional conforming loans) were built around the idea of helping individuals buy a place to live — which is why they cap out at 4 units. Once you're clearly operating an income-producing business (5+ units), you move into commercial lending, which is built around evaluating a property as an investment, not a home.

**Key Definitions**

- **Unit Count:** The number of separate, independently livable dwelling spaces in a property (each with its own kitchen and bathroom, generally).
- **Comps (Comparable Sales):** Valuation method based on recent sale prices of similar nearby properties. Primary method for 1–4 unit residential.
- **Net Operating Income (NOI):** A property's total income minus operating expenses, before mortgage payments and taxes. Core input for commercial valuation.
- **Cap Rate (Capitalization Rate):** The rate of return an investor expects on a commercial property, used to convert NOI into an estimated value.
- **Debt Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR):** A measure of whether a property's income is sufficient to cover its loan payments, commonly required by commercial lenders.
- **Conventional Loan:** A standard residential mortgage not backed by a specific government program (though it may still be sold to Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac).

## Worked Examples

**ESTIMATE (for illustration only, not a real transaction):**

*Property A — Fourplex (4 units):* Similar fourplexes in the neighborhood sold for $480,000–$520,000 in the last year. Based on comps, this property is estimated around $500,000. A buyer could pursue a conventional residential mortgage.

*Property B — 8-Unit Building:* Annual rental income is $144,000. Operating expenses (maintenance, management, insurance, vacancy allowance) total $54,000. NOI = $144,000 − $54,000 = $90,000. If the market cap rate for this type of property is 6%, estimated value = $90,000 ÷ 0.06 = $1,500,000. This property requires commercial financing, and a lender will scrutinize the $90,000 NOI against the proposed loan payment.

## Common Mistakes

- **Assuming a 5-unit property can be financed like a fourplex.** One extra unit triggers an entirely different underwriting process.
- **Valuing a small apartment building using comps only.** Comps can be a useful sanity check, but income-based valuation (NOI ÷ cap rate) is the standard for 5+ units.
- **Ignoring the Debt Service Coverage Ratio.** A buyer with excellent personal credit can still be denied commercial financing if the property's own income doesn't comfortably cover the debt.
- **Confusing "residential" with "small" and "commercial" with "big."** The distinction is unit count, not size or price. A single $3,000,000 mansion is still residential (1 unit).

## FAQ

**Q: Can I combine two fourplexes on adjacent lots and still get residential financing?**
A: No. Each property is evaluated on its own unit count. Owning multiple 1–4 unit properties doesn't combine them into one commercial deal, but each individual property is still financed based on its own unit count.

**Q: Is a 5-unit property always harder to finance than a 4-unit property?**
A: Not necessarily harder — but it is a different process, with different qualification criteria centered on the property's income rather than primarily on your personal finances.

**Q: Does the 1–4 vs. 5+ threshold apply outside the United States?**
A: This lesson describes U.S. lending conventions (REGION-SPECIFIC RULE). Other countries may use different thresholds or classification systems — always confirm local rules where you're investing.

## Key Takeaways

- 1–4 units = residential financing and comps-based valuation. 5+ units = commercial financing and income-based valuation.
- Crossing this threshold changes the loan type, the down payment expectations, the loan term structure, and how the property is valued.
- The threshold exists because 1–4 unit properties behave like homes, while 5+ unit properties behave like income-producing businesses.
- Know which side of the line a property sits on before you start evaluating it — it determines your entire approach.

## Practical Exercise

You're evaluating two potential purchases:
1. A triplex (3 units) renting for a combined $3,900/month.
2. A 6-unit building renting for a combined $9,000/month, with $3,200/month in operating expenses.

For each property, identify: (a) whether it's residential or commercial, (b) which valuation method applies, and (c) what type of financing you'd expect to pursue. Then calculate the estimated value of the 6-unit building assuming a 7% market cap rate.

*(Answer: Property 1 is residential, 3 units, comps-based valuation, conventional financing. Property 2 is commercial, 6 units, income-based valuation, commercial financing. NOI = $9,000 − $3,200 = $5,800/month = $69,600/year. Value = $69,600 ÷ 0.07 = $994,286, approximately $994,000.)*
