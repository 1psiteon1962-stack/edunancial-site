## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
- Explain the difference between strategic debt and consumer debt
- Identify which category a given loan falls into and why it matters
- Explain why the same word — "debt" — can describe two financially opposite situations

## Core Content

Most people are taught one lesson about debt: avoid it. That advice makes sense for one kind of debt and is dangerous advice for another kind.

**Consumer debt** pays for things that lose value the moment you buy them — credit card balances on things you've already consumed, a car loan on a vehicle that depreciates every year, a loan for a vacation. The asset (or non-asset) doesn't generate income. You're paying interest on money spent, with nothing working to pay that interest back for you.

**Strategic debt** — sometimes called productive debt — is different in one specific way: it's backed by an asset that generates income or appreciates, and that income (or the plan to sell the asset) is what pays the loan back. A mortgage on a rental property is the clearest example. The tenant's rent, not your paycheck, is what's supposed to cover the mortgage payment.

Here's the "why" that matters at this level: it's not the size of the debt, or even the interest rate, that makes debt good or bad. It's whether the debt is attached to something that pays for itself. [ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A $300,000 mortgage on a rental property that nets $500/month in cash flow after all expenses is working debt. A $300,000 mortgage on a house you can't afford, that requires you to dip into savings every month to cover it, is a liability wearing an asset's clothes — same size, same rate, completely different risk.

[VERIFIED FACT] Both types of debt legally obligate you to repay, and both affect your credit and borrowing capacity. That part doesn't change. What changes is who — or what — is actually making the payment: you, out of your own income, or the asset, out of income it generates.

This distinction is the foundation for everything else in this track. Every lesson from here forward assumes you can tell these two apart before you look at the numbers.

## Worked Example

Maria is deciding between two loans:

- **Loan A**: $25,000 loan for a new car. Payment: $450/month. The car loses value every year she owns it.
- **Loan B**: $180,000 mortgage on a duplex. Payment: $1,100/month. [ESTIMATE] Combined rent from both units, after vacancy and repairs set aside, is projected at $1,450/month.

Loan A is consumer debt: it's paid entirely from Maria's job income, and the underlying asset is shrinking in value the whole time.

Loan B is strategic debt: the tenants' rent is projected to cover the payment with $350/month left over, and the underlying asset (the duplex) has the potential to appreciate. Maria isn't the one paying this loan — the property is, assuming the [ASSUMPTION] rent projections hold and the units stay reasonably occupied.

## Practice Quiz

1. What is the single factor that determines whether a debt is "strategic" or "consumer," regardless of the loan amount or interest rate?
2. True or False: A car loan can never be considered strategic debt.
3. In the worked example, why is Loan B considered strategic debt even though it's a much larger amount than Loan A?
4. What real-world risk could turn Maria's "strategic" duplex debt into something that behaves like consumer debt?

## Answer Key

1. Whether the debt is backed by an asset that generates income (or reliably appreciates) sufficient to cover the payment.
2. False — if the car is used to generate income (e.g., a delivery vehicle where the income clearly covers the loan), it could function as strategic debt. Context matters more than the category label.
3. Because the debt is intended to be serviced by rental income from the property, not from Maria's personal paycheck.
4. If the units sit vacant, rents fall, or major repairs eat into the cash flow, Maria may have to cover the mortgage from her own income — at that point it behaves like consumer debt even though it's secured by real estate.

## Answers and Explanations

1. The size of the loan and the interest rate matter for the terms of the deal, but they don't determine the *category* of debt. Category is about the relationship between the debt and the income it's tied to.
2. This is a "why" question, not a "what" question — it's testing whether you understand the underlying principle rather than memorizing "cars = bad debt." The principle travels: any debt backed by income-generating use can be strategic.
3. Size is irrelevant to the classification. What matters is the income relationship — Loan B is designed to be self-paying.
4. This is the core risk of real estate leverage, and it's why later lessons in this track cover vacancy reserves and cash flow cushions in detail. Strategic debt can degrade into consumer debt if the income side of the equation breaks down.

## Key Takeaways
- Debt is not inherently good or bad — its classification depends on what's paying it back.
- Strategic debt is backed by income-generating or appreciating assets; consumer debt is backed by nothing but your future paycheck.
- The same size loan, at the same rate, can be radically different in risk depending on this one factor.
- Strategic debt can still fail — the "why" behind the failure is usually a breakdown in the income assumption, not the debt itself.
