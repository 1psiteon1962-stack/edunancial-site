## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
- Explain what fiat currency is and why its value isn't fixed over time
- Explain why a fixed-rate loan can become "cheaper" in real terms as time passes
- Identify the conditions under which this dynamic helps — or doesn't help — a borrower

## Core Content

Fiat currency is money that has value because a government says it does and people agree to use it — not because it's backed by a fixed amount of gold or another physical commodity. [VERIFIED FACT] Most of the world's major currencies today, including the U.S. dollar, are fiat currencies.

Here's why that matters for a mortgage: fiat currency's purchasing power tends to shrink over time. A dollar today generally buys less than a dollar did ten years ago. This is usually called inflation.

Now apply that to a fixed-rate mortgage. If you locked in a $1,500/month payment ten years ago, that $1,500 obligation never changes — but the dollars you use to pay it are worth less each year than the dollars you borrowed originally. Meanwhile, [ASSUMPTION] if wages and rents tend to rise with inflation over time, your rental income used to cover that fixed payment likely grows, while the payment itself stays flat.

This is why real estate investors often describe fixed-rate debt as an asset in itself, not just a liability: you're being allowed to repay a debt, over decades, in currency that is worth less each year, while — in many cases — the income backing that debt rises with inflation.

This only works under specific conditions, and it's important to name them honestly:
- The loan must be **fixed-rate**. A variable-rate loan can rise with inflation too, erasing this advantage.
- Rental income needs to actually keep pace with inflation in your specific market — this isn't guaranteed everywhere or every year.
- You need to be able to hold the property through economic cycles, including periods when this dynamic isn't working in your favor.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] Ten years ago, David took out a $200,000 fixed-rate mortgage at a $1,200/month payment, backed by a rental unit renting for $1,400/month at the time.

Today, that same $1,200/month payment hasn't changed — it's fixed. But [ESTIMATE] if rents in his market have risen with inflation, that unit might now rent for something closer to $1,900/month. David's payment is unchanged; his rental income has grown. The gap between the two — his monthly cash flow — has widened, not because he did anything differently, but because his debt is fixed while his income is not.

Compare this to a hypothetical variable-rate version of the same loan: if the rate had risen along with inflation, David's payment could have climbed toward, or past, his rental income, erasing the advantage entirely.

## Practice Quiz

1. Why does inflation, on its own, not automatically help a borrower?
2. What is the specific structural feature a loan needs to benefit from this dynamic?
3. In David's example, what two things changed and didn't change, and why does that gap matter?
4. Name one condition under which this "fiat advantage" would fail to help a borrower.

## Answer Key

1. Inflation only helps if the loan payment itself is fixed and doesn't rise along with prices; on a variable-rate loan, the payment can rise with inflation too.
2. A fixed interest rate.
3. His mortgage payment stayed fixed; his rental income (assumed to track inflation) rose. The widening gap is his growing cash flow advantage.
4. If the property's rental income doesn't keep pace with inflation in that specific market (e.g., a declining local economy), or if the loan is variable-rate.

## Answers and Explanations

1. This tests whether you understand that inflation alone isn't the mechanism — the fixed nature of the *payment* relative to a rising cost environment is what creates the advantage.
2. Without a fixed rate, the loan can move with the same inflationary pressures that are helping the income side, canceling out the benefit.
3. This is the core mechanism of the lesson: the widening gap between a flat obligation and rising income is where the advantage lives.
4. This question checks that you understand the assumption is conditional, not automatic — market-specific rent stagnation or a variable rate would break it.

## Key Takeaways
- Fiat currency's value can shrink over time; this is usually called inflation.
- A fixed-rate mortgage locks in your payment while your income (rent) may rise — widening cash flow over time.
- This advantage requires a fixed rate, income that rises with inflation, and the ability to hold through cycles.
- This is a real, historically observed pattern in many markets — but it is not guaranteed and does not apply to variable-rate debt.
