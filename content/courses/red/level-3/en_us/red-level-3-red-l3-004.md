- Explain why these ratios protect the lender more than the borrower

## Core Content

When a lender decides whether to approve a loan, they're really asking one question: if something goes wrong, how much do we stand to lose? Two ratios help answer that.

**Debt-to-Income (DTI)** compares your total monthly debt payments to your gross monthly income. [ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] If you earn $6,000/month and owe $2,000/month across all debts, your DTI is 33%. This ratio tells the lender how much of your income is already spoken for — the lower it is, the more room you have to absorb a new payment.

**Loan-to-Value (LTV)** compares the loan amount to the property's appraised value. [ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A $160,000 loan on a $200,000 property is an 80% LTV. This ratio tells the lender how much of a cushion exists if they had to repossess and resell the property — the lower the LTV, the more equity cushion protects the lender if the market drops.

Here's the "why" that connects them: DTI measures your capacity to keep paying; LTV measures the lender's cushion if you stop. A borrower can have a low DTI (plenty of income) but a lender might still hesitate on a high-LTV deal, because a market downturn could leave the loan larger than the property is worth. Conversely, a low-LTV deal with a high-DTI borrower is still risky because that borrower has little room to absorb a surprise expense.

[VERIFIED FACT] Lenders typically set maximum thresholds for both ratios, and requirements can differ for investment properties compared to primary residences — investment loans often carry stricter limits because the lender views them as higher risk.

## Worked Example

Priya wants to buy a $250,000 rental property with a $50,000 down payment (a $200,000 loan). Her gross monthly income is $7,500, and she already pays $1,200/month in other debts. Adding the projected $1,400/month mortgage payment:

- LTV: $200,000 / $250,000 = 80%
- DTI: ($1,200 + $1,400) / $7,500 = 34.7%

[ASSUMPTION] If the lender's investment-property guidelines cap DTI at 45% and LTV at 80%, Priya's deal fits within both limits — but only barely on LTV, meaning a smaller down payment could push the deal outside the lender's comfort zone entirely.

## Answer Key

1. How much the lender stands to lose if the borrower can't keep paying.
2. Because even a financially capable borrower could hand back a property worth less than the loan balance in a downturn, leaving the lender with a loss regardless of the borrower's income.
3. Her LTV would rise to 90% ($225,000 / $250,000), likely pushing the deal outside typical lender limits.
4. Because the borrower isn't living in the property, lenders view investment loans as carrying a higher risk of walk-away default if the deal turns unprofitable.

## Key Takeaways
- DTI measures a borrower's capacity to keep paying; LTV measures the lender's cushion if they can't.
- Both ratios protect the lender first — a strong number on one doesn't offset a weak number on the other.
- Investment property loans are usually held to stricter standards than primary residence loans.
- Down payment size directly drives LTV, which is why bigger down payments often unlock better loan terms.
