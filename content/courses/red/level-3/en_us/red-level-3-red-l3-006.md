## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
- Explain the difference between fixed-rate and adjustable-rate loans
- Explain why rate-type risk grows disproportionately as an investor holds more properties
- Identify why a rate increase affects a portfolio differently than it affects a single loan

## Core Content

A fixed-rate loan locks in one interest rate for the life of the loan. An adjustable-rate loan (often called an ARM) starts at a rate — sometimes lower than a fixed rate — and then resets periodically based on market conditions.

On a single loan, the difference between these two might mean a few hundred dollars a month if rates rise. That's manageable for most people. Here's the "why" behind this lesson: that same risk doesn't stay small as an investor scales up.

If you hold one adjustable-rate mortgage and rates rise, one payment goes up. If you hold ten properties financed with adjustable-rate debt, and rates rise, all ten payments can go up at roughly the same time — often within the same rate cycle, since many ARMs reset on similar schedules tied to the same benchmark rates. What was a manageable inconvenience on one loan becomes a portfolio-wide cash flow event on ten.

[VERIFIED FACT] Interest rates are influenced by broader economic policy and market conditions, and they can rise faster than rental income typically adjusts, since rents are usually constrained by lease terms and local market tolerance.

This is why many experienced investors deliberately trade a slightly higher rate today for the certainty of a fixed rate as their portfolio grows — the value of predictability increases with scale, because the downside of unpredictability multiplies across every loan at once.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An investor holds five rental properties, each financed with an adjustable-rate loan at a $1,000/month payment, for $5,000/month in total mortgage obligations.

If rates rise and each payment increases by $300/month, that's not a $300 problem — it's a $1,500/month portfolio-wide increase, hitting all five properties around the same time. [ASSUMPTION] If none of the five properties' leases allow a rent increase mid-term, the investor absorbs the full $1,500/month increase out of pocket until leases renew — a very different situation than a single homeowner absorbing a $300 increase on one loan.

## Practice Quiz

1. What is the core difference between a fixed-rate and an adjustable-rate loan?
2. Why does rate-type risk grow faster than the number of properties an investor holds?
3. In the worked example, why couldn't the investor immediately offset the rate increase with higher rent?
4. Why might an investor accept a slightly higher fixed rate instead of a lower adjustable rate as their portfolio grows?

## Answer Key

1. A fixed-rate loan keeps the same interest rate for its full term; an adjustable-rate loan resets periodically based on market rates.
2. Because multiple ARMs often reset on similar timelines tied to the same benchmark, so a single rate move can hit every property's payment at once, compounding rather than staying isolated.
3. Because rents are typically fixed for the length of a lease and can't be raised until the lease is up for renewal, creating a lag between rising costs and rising income.
4. Because predictability protects against a portfolio-wide cash flow shock, and the value of that protection grows as more loans — and more simultaneous exposure — are added to the portfolio.

## Key Takeaways
- Fixed-rate loans lock in payments; adjustable-rate loans can rise or fall with market conditions.
- A rate increase that's minor on one loan can become a major cash flow event across a multi-property portfolio.
- Rents typically can't adjust as quickly as rates can rise, creating a lag that hits leveraged portfolios hardest.
- Many investors trade a higher fixed rate for predictability as their portfolio — and their exposure — grows.
