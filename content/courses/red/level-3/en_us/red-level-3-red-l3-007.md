## Core Content

An amortization schedule breaks a loan into its full series of payments, showing how much of each payment goes toward interest versus principal (the actual amount borrowed). On a standard fixed-rate mortgage, this split isn't even across the life of the loan.

Here's the "why": interest is calculated on the remaining loan balance. Early on, the balance is at its highest, so the interest portion of each payment is largest — and the principal portion, what's left over, is smallest. As the balance shrinks year over year, more of each payment goes toward principal, and less toward interest. [VERIFIED FACT] This structure means that in the early years of a typical 30-year fixed mortgage, the majority of each payment is interest, not principal, even though the total payment amount stays the same throughout.

This matters directly for real estate strategy. [ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] If you sell or refinance a property after only 3–4 years, you've barely built equity through principal paydown — most of what you paid went to the lender as interest, not into your ownership stake. If your plan depends on building equity quickly through payments alone (separate from appreciation), a short hold period works against you.

This is why hold-period planning and amortization have to be considered together — a loan that looks fine on paper can quietly undermine a short-term strategy if you don't account for how little principal gets paid down early on.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] On a $200,000, 30-year fixed mortgage at a given rate, the first year's payments might apply roughly 80% toward interest and only 20% toward principal. By year 20, that ratio could be closer to 30% interest and 70% principal.

[ASSUMPTION] An investor planning to hold a property for only 3 years, expecting payments to meaningfully build their equity stake, may be surprised to find they've paid down only a small fraction of the original loan balance — most of their payments went to interest, and their equity growth came mainly from any appreciation, not from the payments themselves.

## Practice Quiz

1. Why is more of an early mortgage payment interest rather than principal?
2. What happens to the interest/principal split as the loan matures?
3. Why would a short hold period reduce the equity benefit of "paying down the loan"?
4. What should an investor planning a short hold period rely on instead of principal paydown for equity growth?

## Answer Key

1. Because interest is calculated on the remaining balance, which is highest at the start of the loan.
2. As the balance shrinks, the interest portion decreases and the principal portion increases with each payment.
3. Because in the early years, most of each payment goes to interest, so very little of the loan balance is actually reduced — equity built through payments alone is minimal in a short window.
4. Appreciation, or another source of equity growth (such as value-add improvements), since principal paydown alone won't contribute much equity in a short hold period.

## Answers and Explanations

1. This is the mechanical foundation of amortization — balance-based interest calculation.
2. This confirms understanding of the shifting ratio over the loan's life.
3. This connects the mechanic directly to a strategic consequence, which is the point of the lesson.
4. This checks that the investor understands which other lever they'd need to rely on if principal paydown isn't doing much work yet.

## Key Takeaways
- Amortization schedules show how each payment splits between interest and principal over time.
- Early payments are mostly interest; later payments are mostly principal.
- A short hold period means minimal equity gain from payments alone — appreciation or added value has to carry more of the weight.
- Hold-period planning and amortization should be considered together before choosing a strategy.
