## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
- Explain the difference between a portfolio lender and a lender who sells loans
- Explain why this difference changes what a lender is willing to approve
- Identify why portfolio lenders can be more flexible for unconventional deals

## Core Content

Most conventional mortgages don't stay with the bank that issued them — they get sold on the secondary market to investors, often bundled into mortgage-backed securities. Because these loans will be sold, the originating lender has to follow strict, standardized guidelines so the loan qualifies for that resale.

A **portfolio lender** does something different: it keeps the loan on its own books instead of selling it. Here's the "why" that matters: because the lender is holding the risk itself, it can set its own underwriting rules instead of following secondary-market standards. This often means more flexibility — evaluating a deal based on the property's income potential, an investor's overall portfolio, or unconventional property types that wouldn't qualify under standardized guidelines.

[VERIFIED FACT] This flexibility is not automatic generosity — portfolio lenders take on more direct risk by keeping the loan, so they often charge higher rates or require larger down payments to compensate.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An investor wants to finance a mixed-use property (retail on the ground floor, apartments above) — a property type that doesn't fit neatly into standard conventional guidelines. A retail bank following secondary-market rules may decline the loan outright. A portfolio lender, evaluating the deal on the property's overall income and the investor's track record, may approve it — at a higher rate than a conventional loan would have carried, reflecting the added risk it's taking on directly.

## Answer Key

1. They are typically sold on the secondary market, often bundled into mortgage-backed securities.
2. Because it keeps the loan on its own books and bears the risk directly, it isn't bound by the standardized rules required for loans intended for resale.
3. Higher interest rates or larger down payment requirements, since the lender is taking on more direct risk.

## Answers and Explanations

1. This establishes the baseline (secondary market) that portfolio lending contrasts with.
2. This is the central mechanism of the lesson — who holds the risk determines who sets the rules.
3. This reinforces that flexibility isn't free; it's priced into the deal.

## Key Takeaways
- Most conventional loans are sold to the secondary market and must meet standardized guidelines.
- Portfolio lenders keep loans on their own books and can set their own underwriting rules.
- This flexibility often comes with higher rates or stricter down payment requirements.
