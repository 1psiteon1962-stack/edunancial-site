## Core Content

Normally, selling an investment property triggers capital gains tax on the profit. A **1031 exchange** (named for the relevant tax code section) allows an investor to sell a property and reinvest the proceeds into a new "like-kind" property while deferring that tax bill, rather than paying it immediately.

Here's why this matters for leverage: without a 1031 exchange, selling a property means losing a chunk of your proceeds to taxes before you can reinvest them — shrinking the down payment available for the next, larger deal. By deferring that tax, an investor can roll the full equity amount into a bigger property, supporting a bigger loan and a bigger portfolio, faster than paying taxes at each step would allow.

[VERIFIED FACT] A 1031 exchange comes with strict timing rules — including deadlines to identify and close on a replacement property — and the replacement property must meet specific requirements. Missing these deadlines can disqualify the exchange and trigger the deferred tax immediately.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An investor sells a property with $150,000 in equity, including $60,000 in taxable gain. [ASSUMPTION] Without a 1031 exchange, a portion of that $60,000 gain might go to capital gains tax, leaving less than $150,000 to reinvest. With a properly executed 1031 exchange, the full $150,000 can be rolled into a down payment on a larger replacement property, deferring the tax and supporting a bigger loan than the after-tax proceeds alone would have allowed.

## Key Takeaways
- A 1031 exchange defers capital gains tax when proceeds are reinvested into a like-kind property.
- Preserving more equity through deferral supports bigger down payments and bigger loans on the next property.
- Strict timing and property-type rules apply, and missing them can trigger the deferred tax immediately.
