## Core Content

Every lesson so far has shown leverage as a tool that can work in an investor's favor. This lesson covers the other side deliberately: leverage doesn't ask whether a property is "good" — it just multiplies whatever happens, good or bad, as covered in Lesson 3.

**Over-leveraging** happens when the amount of debt stacked onto a property (or a portfolio) leaves little to no margin for the normal bumps every property experiences — vacancy, repairs, rate changes. The property itself might be a genuinely sound asset in a good location with real rental demand. It's the size of the debt relative to the property's ability to service it that turns a good asset into a fragile investment.

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] Stacking a first mortgage, a HELOC against the same property, and a personal loan used for the down payment can all be individually reasonable-looking pieces of debt that, combined, leave the investor with almost no cushion if even one thing goes wrong. This is why the earlier lessons on DTI, LTV, vacancy reserves, and cash flow all matter together — over-leveraging is what happens when those guardrails are ignored or stretched too far, one at a time.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An investor buys a property with a 90% LTV first mortgage, then takes out a personal loan to cover renovation costs instead of paying cash, and skips setting aside vacancy reserves to save on the down payment. [ASSUMPTION] If a single tenant leaves unexpectedly, the property now has to cover the first mortgage, the personal loan payment, and lost rental income simultaneously — with no reserve built in. A property that would have handled a normal vacancy comfortably at a lower debt load now threatens the investor's entire cash position.

## Answer Key

1. No — the property itself can be sound; over-leveraging is about the debt structure stacked on top of it leaving no margin for normal setbacks.
2. Over-leveraging is what happens when those individual guardrails (DTI limits, LTV limits, reserves) are each stretched or skipped, removing the cushion they're each meant to provide.
3. A high-LTV first mortgage, an added personal loan for renovations, and no vacancy reserve — each reasonable-looking alone, but combined they removed all margin for error.

## Key Takeaways
- Over-leveraging is a debt-structure problem, not necessarily an asset-quality problem.
- Stacking multiple loans on the same property can each look reasonable individually while combining into real fragility.
- The guardrails from earlier lessons (DTI, LTV, reserves) exist specifically to prevent this outcome.
