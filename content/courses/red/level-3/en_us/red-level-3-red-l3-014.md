## Core Content

[HYPOTHETICAL SCENARIO — ASSUMPTION, NOT VERIFIED FACT] Imagine a central bank, responding to rising inflation, raises benchmark interest rates sharply over a short period. This kind of scenario is a useful stress test, not a prediction about any specific real event.

In this hypothetical, new mortgage rates for investment properties rise significantly within a matter of months. Adjustable-rate loans already in place begin resetting higher at their next adjustment date. Refinancing becomes more expensive, which slows the "Repeat" step of BRRRR-style strategies covered in Lesson 9. Property values in some markets soften, as higher rates reduce what buyers can afford to pay — which affects LTV calculations on any planned refinance.

This single hypothetical event touches nearly everything covered in this track at once: DTI calculations shift as new debt costs more relative to income; cash flow margins shrink on any adjustable-rate property; reserve funds get tested as tenants facing their own rising costs may be more likely to fall behind; and exit strategies planned around refinancing may need to shift toward holding longer or selling instead.

The "why" behind this lesson: a rate shock isn't really a single risk — it's a stress test that reveals whether every earlier assumption in a deal (income growth, appreciation, refinance timing, reserve size) was realistic or optimistic. Deals with real cash flow margin and reasonable reserves tend to survive this kind of scenario. Deals that were only working because of favorable rates and rising prices are the ones most exposed.

## Worked Example

[HYPOTHETICAL — ILLUSTRATIVE, NOT A PREDICTION] An investor holding three properties financed with adjustable-rate loans planned to refinance each one within two years to pull out cash for further purchases. In this scenario, rates rise sharply before any refinance happens. All three adjustable payments increase at their next reset. The planned cash-out refinances no longer make sense at the new, higher rates, and appraised values have softened slightly, reducing the amount of equity available to pull out even if the investor proceeded anyway. The investor's original plan — refinance, extract capital, repeat — is now on hold, and the properties' cash flow margins (if they existed) are what determines whether the portfolio can simply hold steady until conditions change.

## Practice Quiz

1. Why is this scenario described as hypothetical rather than a specific historical event?
2. Name three separate concepts from earlier in this track that a rate shock scenario touches simultaneously.
3. What distinguishes a deal that "survives" this kind of scenario from one that doesn't?
4. Why should an investor stress-test a deal against a rate shock before closing, rather than after?

## Answer Key

1. Because it's designed as a general stress-test exercise applicable to reasoning about risk, not a claim about any particular real-world event or date.
2. Any three of: DTI, LTV/appraised value, adjustable-rate resets, refinance timing (BRRRR), cash flow margin, vacancy/reserve funds, exit strategy.
3. Cash flow margin and reserve size — deals with real margin can absorb the shock; deals relying on favorable rates and rising prices are most exposed.
4. Because once a deal is closed and leveraged, the investor has far less flexibility to change course — testing assumptions upfront reveals hidden fragility while there's still a choice to walk away or restructure the deal.

## Key Takeaways
- A single hypothetical economic event can touch DTI, LTV, refinance timing, cash flow, and reserves all at once.
- This is why this track treats these concepts as connected, not separate topics.
- Deals with real cash flow margin and adequate reserves are more likely to survive a rate shock than deals relying on favorable conditions continuing indefinitely.
- Stress-testing a deal against a rate-shock scenario before closing reveals fragility while there's still time to act on it.
