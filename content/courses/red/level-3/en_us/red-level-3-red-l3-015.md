## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
- Explain why an exit strategy affects financing choices made at purchase
- Compare at least two different exit strategies and their financing implications
- Explain the risk of buying a property without a clear exit plan

## Core Content

Every lesson in this track — amortization, refinancing, rate type, 1031 exchanges — depends on one thing that's easy to skip: knowing, roughly, how long you plan to hold the property and how you plan to eventually exit the investment. Without that plan, it's hard to choose the right debt structure at all.

Consider a few common exit strategies: hold indefinitely for cash flow and eventual estate transfer; hold for a set number of years then sell for a lump sum; or hold short-term with a planned refinance to redeploy capital (the BRRRR approach). Each of these points toward a different financing choice. A long-term hold favors a fixed-rate loan, since amortization from Lesson 7 shows equity building slowly but steadily over decades. A short hold planned around a refinance needs to account for the appraisal risk covered in Lesson 9. A planned sale needs to account for prepayment penalties (a later lesson) and the tax treatment of the sale.

The "why" here is sequencing: exit strategy isn't a decision to make when you're ready to sell — the earlier lessons in this track show that financing choices made at purchase either support or undermine a given exit plan. Choosing the exit strategy first, even loosely, lets you choose financing that's actually built for it, rather than discovering a mismatch years later.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] Two investors buy similar duplexes. One plans to hold for 20+ years as a retirement income property and chooses a 30-year fixed-rate loan, prioritizing payment stability over rate. The other plans to renovate and refinance within 18 months to redeploy capital into another deal, so she chooses a shorter-term loan structure designed to be refinanced without a large prepayment penalty, even though it carries a slightly higher rate. Each choice fits its own exit plan — but if the two investors swapped strategies without swapping financing, each would likely run into friction: the long-term holder facing early refinance costs unnecessarily, and the short-term investor facing decades of a loan structure she never intended to keep.

## Practice Quiz

1. Why does exit strategy affect the financing decision made at purchase, rather than only mattering at the time of sale?
2. Which financing feature would matter most to an investor planning a long-term hold, based on earlier lessons in this track?
3. Which financing feature would matter most to an investor planning a short hold with a planned refinance?
4. What risk does an investor take on by buying a property with no exit strategy in mind at all?

## Answer Key

1. Because different exit strategies rely on different loan structures to work well — choosing financing first, without an exit plan, risks a mismatch discovered too late.
2. Payment stability from a fixed rate, since a long hold benefits from predictable amortization over time (Lessons 6 and 7).
3. Low or no prepayment penalties and terms that support an early refinance (Lessons 8 and 9).
4. They risk ending up with financing that doesn't match how they'll eventually want to use the property, creating unnecessary cost or friction whenever they do decide to sell, refinance, or hold.

## Key Takeaways
- Deciding roughly how you plan to exit a property should happen before choosing how to finance it, not after.
- Different exit strategies favor different loan structures: long-term holds favor fixed-rate stability; short-term plans favor flexible refinance terms.
- Financing chosen without an exit plan risks a costly mismatch discovered only when it's time to sell, refinance, or hold.
- This lesson closes the foundational arc of RED Level 3: every earlier concept assumes a deliberate ownership timeline.
