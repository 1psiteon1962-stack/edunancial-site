## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
- Explain the general size threshold separating residential and commercial lending
- Explain why commercial loans are evaluated primarily on property income
- Identify why commercial loan terms often look different from residential ones

## Core Content

Residential lending rules, including most of what's covered earlier in this track, generally apply to properties with 1–4 units. [VERIFIED FACT] Properties with 5 or more residential units, along with most non-residential property types, typically fall under commercial lending rules, which follow a different framework.

Here's the "why": residential loans are usually evaluated heavily on the borrower's personal income and credit (DTI, as covered in Lesson 4). Commercial loans shift the emphasis toward the property's own income-generating ability — similar in spirit to DSCR loans covered in a later lesson, but with additional commercial-specific underwriting. Because commercial loans are evaluated more on the asset itself, terms often differ structurally: shorter loan terms (sometimes 5–10 years) with payments calculated as if the loan were spread over a longer period, ending in a balloon payment (covered in a later lesson) rather than a full 30-year payoff.

This shift in evaluation approach exists because commercial properties and their income streams vary far more widely than standardized single-family homes, so lenders build flexibility — and risk protection — into how they structure the loan itself, not just how they qualify the borrower.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An investor buying a fourplex (4 units) goes through standard residential underwriting, similar to a single-family purchase, with DTI as a central factor. The same investor buying a 12-unit apartment building instead goes through commercial underwriting, where the lender focuses heavily on the building's net operating income and debt service coverage, and offers a 10-year loan term with payments based on a 25-year amortization schedule — meaning a balloon payment is due at the end of year 10 for the remaining balance.

## Practice Quiz

1. What size threshold generally separates residential from commercial lending?
2. What does commercial underwriting emphasize instead of the borrower's personal DTI?
3. Why do commercial loans often include a balloon payment structure?

## Answer Key

1. Generally 5 or more residential units (or most non-residential commercial property types).
2. The property's own income-generating ability and debt service coverage.
3. Because commercial loan terms are often shorter than the amortization period used to calculate payments, leaving a remaining balance due at the end of the term.

## Answers and Explanations

1. This is a factual threshold worth knowing before comparing loan types.
2. This is the central shift in underwriting philosophy the lesson explains.
3. This connects the balloon-payment structure (detailed further in a later lesson) to the shorter-term nature of commercial loans.

## Key Takeaways
- Residential lending generally applies to 1–4 unit properties; 5+ units and most commercial property types fall under commercial lending.
- Commercial underwriting emphasizes the property's income, not just the borrower's personal finances.
- Commercial loans often carry shorter terms with balloon payments, reflecting the lender's different risk approach to larger, more variable properties.
