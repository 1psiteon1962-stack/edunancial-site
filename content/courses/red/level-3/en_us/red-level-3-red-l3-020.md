## Core Content

**Debt Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR)** compares a property's net operating income to its total debt payments. [ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A property generating $2,400/month in net income against a $2,000/month mortgage payment has a DSCR of 1.2 — meaning it generates 20% more income than it needs to cover the debt.

A **DSCR loan** qualifies a borrower based primarily on this ratio, rather than the personal DTI calculation covered in Lesson 4. Here's the "why" this matters: many real estate investors, especially those who are self-employed or hold multiple properties, may show relatively low personal taxable income on paper (often due to legitimate depreciation deductions) even while their properties perform well. A conventional DTI-based loan can undervalue these investors' true financial position; a DSCR loan instead asks a more direct question — does this specific property generate enough income to cover its own debt?

[VERIFIED FACT] DSCR loans typically require a DSCR at or above a lender-specified minimum (often around 1.0 to 1.25) and often carry somewhat higher rates than conventional owner-occupied loans, reflecting their investor-focused, less personal-income-dependent underwriting.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] Two investors, Sam and Lena, both want to buy a rental property with a projected $1,800/month net operating income and a projected $1,600/month mortgage payment (DSCR of 1.125). Sam has strong W-2 income and easily qualifies under conventional DTI rules. Lena is self-employed with several properties and shows lower taxable income due to legitimate depreciation write-offs, making conventional DTI qualification harder despite her properties performing well. A DSCR loan, evaluating the property itself rather than Lena's personal tax return, may allow her to qualify for the same deal that a DTI-based loan would make difficult.

## Practice Quiz

1. What does DSCR measure?
2. Why might a DSCR loan help an investor who shows low taxable income despite owning profitable properties?
3. What is a typical trade-off for using a DSCR loan instead of conventional financing?

## Answer Key

1. A property's net operating income relative to its total debt payments.
2. Because it qualifies the loan based on the property's own income performance rather than the borrower's personal tax return, which can understate true financial strength due to legitimate deductions like depreciation.
3. DSCR loans often carry somewhat higher interest rates than conventional loans, reflecting the different underwriting approach.

## Answers and Explanations

1. This is the foundational definition needed for the rest of the lesson.
2. This is the central "why" — DSCR loans solve a specific mismatch between taxable income and actual investment performance.

## Key Takeaways
- DSCR compares a property's net income to its debt payments, rather than relying on personal income.
- DSCR loans can help investors whose tax returns understate their true financial position due to legitimate deductions.
- This flexibility typically comes with somewhat higher interest rates than conventional owner-occupied loans.
