## Core Content

A **balloon payment** is a large lump-sum payment due at the end of a loan term, covering the remaining loan balance that wasn't paid off through regular monthly payments. As introduced in Lesson 16, this structure is common in commercial lending: a loan might be amortized as if it were a 25- or 30-year loan (keeping monthly payments manageable) but actually mature — meaning the full remaining balance comes due — after only 5 or 10 years.

Here's the "why" this creates a hidden deadline: because monthly payments are calculated on a longer amortization schedule than the loan's actual term, only a small portion of the principal gets paid down by the maturity date, as shown in Lesson 7's amortization discussion. The investor is left owing a large remaining balance all at once, which almost always needs to be handled through a refinance or a sale — not paid out of pocket.

This is why balloon-payment loans require exit-strategy planning (Lesson 15) years in advance: if market conditions, the property's value, or the investor's qualification change unfavorably as the balloon date approaches, refinancing may become difficult or expensive right when it's most needed.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An investor takes a commercial loan on an 8-unit building: payments are calculated on a 25-year amortization schedule, but the loan matures in 7 years, at which point the remaining balance is due in full. [ASSUMPTION] If, as that 7-year mark approaches, interest rates have risen and the property's income has softened, refinancing into a new loan to cover the balloon payment could be harder to secure — or come with less favorable terms — than if the investor had planned ahead and built reserves or an earlier refinance option into their strategy.

## Practice Quiz

1. What is a balloon payment?
2. Why does a balloon structure leave a large remaining balance at maturity?
3. What risk does waiting until the balloon date approaches to plan for it create?

## Answer Key

1. A large lump-sum payment due at the end of a loan's term, covering the remaining balance not paid off through regular payments.
2. Because payments are calculated using a longer amortization schedule than the loan's actual term, so relatively little principal is paid down by the time the loan matures.
3. If market conditions or the investor's financial position have shifted unfavorably by the balloon date, refinancing to cover the payment may be difficult or costly right when it's needed most.

## Key Takeaways
- A balloon payment is a lump sum due at loan maturity, common in commercial lending structures.
- Because payments are calculated on a longer schedule than the loan's actual term, a large balance remains at maturity.
- Balloon deadlines should be planned for years in advance, since market or personal financial shifts near the deadline can make refinancing harder exactly when it's needed.
