## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
- Define capitalization rate (cap rate)
- Explain why comparing cap rate to mortgage rate predicts whether leverage helps or hurts
- Identify the term for when this comparison favors the borrower

## Core Content

**Cap rate** is a property's net operating income divided by its purchase price (or current value), expressed as a percentage — a way of measuring a property's return as if it were bought entirely in cash, with no financing involved.

Here's the "why" this connects to leverage: comparing the cap rate to the mortgage's interest rate tells you, in general terms, whether borrowing money to buy the property is likely to help or hurt your cash-on-cash return (the return on the actual cash you invested, as introduced in Lesson 3). If the cap rate is higher than the mortgage rate, this is generally called **positive leverage** — the property's return outpaces the cost of the debt, and borrowing tends to boost your cash-on-cash return above the property's own cap rate. If the mortgage rate is higher than the cap rate, this is **negative leverage** — the cost of debt outpaces the property's own return, and borrowing tends to reduce your cash-on-cash return below the cap rate (a topic explored further in the next lesson).

[VERIFIED FACT] This comparison is a useful screening tool, not a complete underwriting process — actual cash-on-cash return also depends on the specific loan terms, down payment size, and the property's real expenses, all covered elsewhere in this track.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A property has a 7% cap rate. If mortgage rates for similar investment properties are around 6%, this is a positive-leverage scenario — borrowing at a rate below the property's unleveraged return tends to amplify the investor's cash-on-cash return above 7%. If mortgage rates were instead around 8%, this would be a negative-leverage scenario — the cost of the debt exceeds the property's own return, and cash-on-cash return would tend to fall below 7%, even though the property itself hasn't changed at all.

## Practice Quiz

1. What does cap rate measure, and what financing assumption does it make?
2. What does it mean when a property is in a "positive leverage" position?
3. Why can the same property be a "positive leverage" deal at one point in time and a "negative leverage" deal at another?

## Answer Key

1. Net operating income divided by the property's price or value, assuming a full cash purchase with no financing.
2. The property's cap rate is higher than the mortgage interest rate, meaning borrowing tends to increase the investor's cash-on-cash return above the property's own cap rate.
3. Because mortgage rates change over time (and cap rates can shift with market conditions too) — the same property's leverage position depends on prevailing interest rates at the time of financing, not just the property itself.

## Key Takeaways
- Cap rate measures a property's unleveraged return based on net operating income and price.
- Comparing cap rate to mortgage rate reveals whether leverage will help ("positive leverage") or hurt ("negative leverage") your cash-on-cash return.
- This comparison shifts with interest rate cycles — the same property can move between positive and negative leverage as rates change.
