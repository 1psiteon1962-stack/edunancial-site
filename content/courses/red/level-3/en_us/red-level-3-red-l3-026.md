## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
- Explain what a rate lock does and why timing matters between application and closing
- Explain how a rate buydown (points) works
- Explain why a buydown is a trade-off, not simply a discount

## Core Content

Interest rates can move between the day you apply for a loan and the day you close. A **rate lock** freezes the interest rate you were quoted for a set period, protecting you from rate increases during that window — but also meaning you won't benefit if rates happen to drop before closing, unless the lock includes a specific float-down option.

A **buydown** (paying "points") is different: it's an upfront fee paid at closing in exchange for a permanently lower interest rate on the loan. [VERIFIED FACT] One point typically costs 1% of the loan amount and commonly reduces the rate by a modest amount, though the exact relationship varies by lender and market conditions.

Here's the "why" that matters: a buydown isn't a discount — it's a trade of cash today for a lower payment over time. Whether it's worth doing depends on how long you plan to hold the loan, connecting directly back to Lesson 15's exit strategy lesson. If you plan to refinance or sell before the monthly savings add up to more than the upfront cost, the buydown doesn't pay for itself.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An investor is quoted a rate and can pay $3,000 upfront to buy the rate down, saving [ASSUMPTION] roughly $60/month on the payment. That's a 50-month breakeven point (roughly 4 years) before the buydown "pays for itself." If she plans to refinance within 2 years as part of a BRRRR strategy (Lesson 9), the buydown likely isn't worth it. If she plans to hold the loan for 15+ years, it likely is.

## Practice Quiz

1. What does a rate lock protect against, and what does it not protect against (without a float-down option)?
2. How does a buydown work?
3. Why does an investor's exit strategy directly affect whether a buydown makes sense?

## Answer Key

1. It protects against rate increases before closing; without a float-down option, it doesn't let you benefit if rates drop instead.
2. Paying an upfront fee ("points") at closing in exchange for a permanently lower interest rate on the loan.
3. Because the upfront cost only "pays for itself" after enough months of lower payments accumulate — a short expected hold period may not allow enough time to recoup that cost.

## Answers and Explanations

1. This tests the specific limitation of a standard rate lock, not just its benefit.
2. This is the mechanical foundation for the rest of the lesson.
3. This directly ties the decision back to Lesson 15's principle that exit strategy should shape financing choices.

## Key Takeaways
- A rate lock protects against rate increases before closing but typically doesn't let you benefit from a rate drop unless it includes a float-down feature.
- A buydown trades upfront cash for a lower rate over the life of the loan — it's a trade-off, not a discount.
- Whether a buydown makes sense depends heavily on how long you plan to hold the loan.
