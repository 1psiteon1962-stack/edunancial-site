## Core Content

If a loan defaults and the lender takes back the property, is that the end of the borrower's liability, or can the lender pursue the borrower's other assets for any remaining shortfall? The answer depends on whether the loan is recourse or non-recourse.

With a **recourse loan**, if the property's value at foreclosure doesn't cover the remaining loan balance, the lender can pursue the borrower personally for the difference — other assets, wages, or savings could potentially be at risk. With a **non-recourse loan**, the lender's recovery is limited to the property itself; if it doesn't cover the debt, the lender generally can't pursue the borrower's other assets for the shortfall.

Here's the "why" this matters directly: this distinction changes the actual worst-case scenario in every leverage decision covered throughout this track. A recourse loan means a failed deal can threaten assets well beyond the property itself. [VERIFIED FACT] Non-recourse loans are more commonly associated with certain commercial and larger multifamily financing, while many conventional residential and smaller investment property loans are recourse loans — though exact terms vary by lender, loan type, and jurisdiction, and should always be confirmed in the specific loan documents.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] Two investors each default on a loan after a property's value drops significantly. Investor A has a recourse loan; after foreclosure, the lender can pursue her for the remaining shortfall beyond what the property sale covered. Investor B has a non-recourse loan on a similar-sized deal; after foreclosure, the lender's recovery is limited to the property itself, and he isn't personally pursued for any remaining gap. The underlying deal outcome was similar, but the personal financial exposure was very different.

## Key Takeaways
- Recourse loans allow a lender to pursue a borrower's other assets for any shortfall after foreclosure; non-recourse loans generally limit recovery to the property itself.
- This distinction defines the real worst-case exposure of a leveraged deal, beyond just losing the property.
- Loan documents should always be checked directly to confirm which type applies to a specific loan.
