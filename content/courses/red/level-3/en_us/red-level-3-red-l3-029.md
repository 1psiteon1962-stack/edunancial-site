## Core Content

A **loan covenant** is an ongoing condition, written into the loan agreement, that the borrower must maintain for the life of the loan — separate from simply making payments on time. Covenants are more common in commercial lending (Lesson 16) than in typical conventional residential mortgages.

A common example is a minimum DSCR covenant (connecting to Lesson 20): the loan agreement might require the property to maintain a DSCR above a certain level throughout the loan term, not just at the time of approval. Other covenants might restrict additional borrowing against the property, require the lender's approval before major renovations, or require regular financial reporting on the property's performance.

Here's the "why" this matters: violating a covenant is called being in "default" even if every payment has been made on time. [VERIFIED FACT] Depending on the loan agreement, a covenant violation can give the lender rights to call the loan due immediately, adjust terms, or take other specified action — the exact consequences depend entirely on the specific language in that loan's agreement.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A commercial loan on an apartment building includes a covenant requiring the property to maintain a DSCR of at least 1.15 throughout the loan term. The investor has made every payment on time, but a temporary rise in vacancy causes the property's actual DSCR to drop to 1.05 for a reporting period. [ASSUMPTION] Even though no payment was missed, this could trigger a covenant default under the loan agreement, potentially giving the lender rights specified in that agreement — a consequence entirely separate from the investor's payment history.

## Practice Quiz

1. What is a loan covenant?
2. Give one example of a common real estate loan covenant.
3. Why can a covenant violation be considered a default even if payments are current?

## Answer Key

1. An ongoing condition written into a loan agreement that the borrower must maintain for the life of the loan, separate from making payments.
2. A minimum DSCR requirement is one common example (others include restrictions on additional borrowing or requirements for lender approval before major changes to the property).
3. Because the loan agreement defines default based on maintaining specific conditions, not solely on payment history — violating any defined covenant can trigger default regardless of payment status.

## Key Takeaways
- Loan covenants are ongoing conditions a borrower must maintain throughout a loan's term, common in commercial financing.
- A common example is a minimum DSCR requirement that must be maintained, not just met at closing.
- Violating a covenant can trigger default even if every payment has been made on time, with consequences defined by the specific loan agreement.
