## Core Content

A property's "capital stack" describes the layers of money used to fund it, ranked by repayment priority if something goes wrong. A first mortgage typically sits at the top of that priority order — paid first from any proceeds if the property is sold or foreclosed. Equity (the investor's own cash) typically sits at the bottom — paid last, if anything is left over.

**Mezzanine debt** sits in between: it's a secondary layer of financing, subordinate to the first mortgage but senior to the equity investors. Here's the "why" behind its cost: because mezzanine lenders are repaid only after the first mortgage is satisfied, they take on more risk than a first-mortgage lender — and price that risk with a meaningfully higher interest rate, often closer to what an equity investor might expect than what a mortgage lender would charge.

Mezzanine debt is typically used on larger commercial deals where the first mortgage alone doesn't cover the full amount needed, and the sponsor wants to fill the gap with debt rather than bringing in more equity investors and diluting ownership further.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A commercial property acquisition needs $10 million in total capital. A first mortgage covers $6.5 million at a relatively low rate reflecting its senior repayment position. Rather than raising the remaining $3.5 million entirely from equity investors, the sponsor uses $1.5 million in mezzanine debt — carrying a materially higher rate, reflecting its subordinate position — and raises the remaining $2 million from equity. This layered structure fills the funding gap while giving up less ownership than raising the full remaining amount as equity would have required.

## Practice Quiz

1. Where does mezzanine debt sit in a property's capital stack, relative to a first mortgage and equity?
2. Why do mezzanine lenders charge higher rates than first-mortgage lenders?
3. Why might a sponsor use mezzanine debt instead of raising more equity?

## Answer Key

1. Between the first mortgage (senior) and equity (junior) — subordinate to the mortgage but senior to equity investors.
2. Because they're repaid only after the first mortgage is satisfied, making their position riskier, which is priced into a higher rate.
3. To fill a funding gap without diluting ownership further among equity investors, since debt doesn't require giving up a share of ownership.

## Key Takeaways
- Mezzanine debt sits between a first mortgage and equity in a property's capital stack.
- Its subordinate repayment position means higher risk for the lender, priced as a higher interest rate.
- It's commonly used on larger deals to fill a funding gap without diluting ownership as much as raising more equity would.
