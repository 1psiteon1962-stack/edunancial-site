## Core Content

**Depreciation** is a tax deduction that lets property owners deduct a portion of a property's value each year as a "cost" of ownership, based on the idea that buildings wear out over time — even though, unlike a maintenance expense, no actual cash leaves your pocket when you claim it.

Here's the "why" this creates an important gap: a property can show a **taxable loss** on paper (rental income minus expenses minus depreciation) while still generating **positive cash flow** in reality (rental income minus actual cash expenses, including the mortgage payment, but not including the non-cash depreciation deduction). Depreciation reduces the taxable income the IRS sees without reducing the actual money the investor collects.

This connects directly back to Lesson 20's DSCR discussion: it's exactly this gap — a real estate investor whose tax returns show relatively low or even negative taxable income, despite properties that perform well in cash flow terms — that led to DSCR loans qualifying on the property's income rather than the borrower's personal tax return.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A property collects $24,000/year in rent, has $10,000/year in actual cash expenses (mortgage, taxes, insurance, maintenance), and qualifies for $9,000/year in depreciation. Actual cash flow: $24,000 − $10,000 = $14,000 positive. Taxable income: $24,000 − $10,000 − $9,000 = $5,000 — still positive here, but in many real scenarios, particularly with larger properties or accelerated depreciation methods, this taxable figure can turn negative even while cash flow remains strongly positive.

## Practice Quiz

1. Why doesn't depreciation reduce actual cash flow, even though it reduces taxable income?
2. What can happen to the relationship between taxable income and cash flow as depreciation increases?
3. How does this concept connect back to the DSCR loan discussion in Lesson 20?

## Answer Key

1. Because depreciation is a non-cash deduction — it reflects an assumed decline in value, not an actual expense paid out during the year.
2. Taxable income can fall well below actual cash flow, and in some cases turn negative, even while the property generates strong real cash flow.
3. DSCR loans exist partly to solve the mismatch this lesson describes — qualifying based on the property's actual income performance rather than a personal tax return that depreciation may have made look weaker than the true financial picture.

## Key Takeaways
- Depreciation reduces taxable income without reducing actual cash flow, since it's a non-cash deduction.
- This can create a meaningful gap between what a tax return shows and what a property actually generates in cash.
- This gap is a key reason DSCR loans exist — to qualify investors based on real property performance, not a tax return that depreciation may make look weaker.
