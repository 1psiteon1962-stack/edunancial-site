## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

## Core Content

Earlier lessons in this track have referenced "mortgage payment" somewhat loosely. In reality, many loans require an **escrow (or impound) account**: alongside principal and interest, the lender collects an additional monthly amount, holds it, and pays your property taxes and insurance on your behalf when those bills come due.

Here's the "why" this matters for cash flow accuracy: if an investor calculates cash flow using only principal and interest, and separately budgets for taxes and insurance loosely or inconsistently, the numbers can look better than reality. The escrowed amount is a real, required monthly outflow, collected by the lender as part of the total payment — it needs to be included in cash flow math from Lesson 5, not treated as a separate, optional line item.

[VERIFIED FACT] Escrow requirements and amounts vary by lender, loan type, and jurisdiction, and the escrowed amount can be adjusted periodically (often annually) if actual tax or insurance costs change, which can shift the total monthly payment even though the loan's principal and interest amount hasn't changed.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An investor's loan has a principal-and-interest payment of $1,200/month. With escrow for taxes and insurance added, the total required monthly payment is actually $1,450/month. If she calculated her cash flow projection using only the $1,200 figure, she'd overstate her monthly cash flow by $250 — a gap that could turn a projected positive cash flow deal into a break-even or negative one once the full, real payment is used.

## Practice Quiz

1. What is an escrow (impound) account used for?
2. Why can using only the principal-and-interest amount distort a cash flow projection?
3. Why might a mortgage payment change over time even if the principal and interest amount stays fixed?

## Answer Key

1. To collect additional funds monthly, alongside principal and interest, that the lender uses to pay property taxes and insurance on the borrower's behalf.
2. Because it leaves out a real, required monthly outflow (the escrowed taxes and insurance), making cash flow appear better than it actually is.
3. Because the escrowed amount can be periodically adjusted if actual tax or insurance costs change, even though the fixed-rate principal and interest portion stays the same.

## Answers and Explanations

1. This establishes the mechanism the lesson is built around.
2. This is the central, practical caution — a common source of overly optimistic cash flow projections.

## Key Takeaways
- An escrow (impound) account collects extra money monthly to cover property taxes and insurance on the borrower's behalf.
- Cash flow calculations should use the full payment, including escrow, not just principal and interest.
- Escrowed amounts can be periodically adjusted, changing the total monthly payment even on an otherwise fixed-rate loan.
