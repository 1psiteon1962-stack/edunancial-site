## Core Content

A **lien** is a legal claim against a property, usually securing a debt — a mortgage is one common example, but liens can also arise from unpaid contractor work, unpaid taxes, or other legal claims. When more than one lien exists on the same property, **lien priority** determines the order in which each is paid if the property is sold or foreclosed.

Here's the general "why" behind priority: [VERIFIED FACT] lien priority is often, though not always, determined by the order in which liens were recorded — a "first" mortgage typically has priority over a "second" mortgage or HELOC (Lesson 21) recorded later, meaning the first lienholder gets paid in full before anything is available for lower-priority liens. Certain liens, such as property tax liens, may take priority under specific rules regardless of when they were recorded — the exact rules vary by jurisdiction and lien type.

This matters directly for real-world risk: if a property is sold for less than the total debt against it, junior lienholders may receive little or nothing, even though their debt is entirely legitimate. This is also why cross-collateralization (Lesson 19) can be especially risky — a lender with a claim against multiple properties may have varying priority positions on each one, adding complexity to an already interconnected risk.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A property has a first mortgage of $150,000 and a HELOC (second lien) of $30,000. If the property is foreclosed and sold for $160,000 after costs, the first mortgage lender is paid its full $150,000 first, leaving only $10,000 available for the HELOC lender — who is owed $30,000 but recovers only a fraction of that amount due to their junior lien position.

3. Because the first mortgage has priority and is paid in full first; only the remaining sale proceeds are available for the junior HELOC lien, which is less than the full amount owed.

## Key Takeaways
- A lien is a legal claim against a property, often securing a debt like a mortgage.
- Lien priority, generally based on recording order (with some exceptions), determines who gets paid first if a property is sold or foreclosed.
- Junior lienholders can recover little or nothing if sale proceeds don't cover all debts, which adds real risk to any secondary financing.
