## Core Content

Lesson 20 introduced DSCR as a measure of a property's income relative to its debt payments, calculated under expected, "everything goes as planned" conditions. **Stress-testing** DSCR means recalculating that same ratio under a deliberately worse scenario — higher vacancy, lower rent, or higher expenses — to see whether the property still covers its debt if conditions turn less favorable than projected.

Here's the "why" this matters: a deal that looks solid under optimistic assumptions can look very different once realistic downside scenarios (Lesson 10's vacancy and reserves, Lesson 14's rate shock scenario) are applied. Stress-testing before committing to a deal — not after a downturn hits — is what turns the abstract risk-management lessons throughout this track into a concrete, repeatable underwriting habit.

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A simple stress test might recalculate DSCR assuming rent 10–15% lower than projected, or vacancy at double the typical rate for that market, and check whether the resulting DSCR still stays above a comfortable threshold (echoing the DSCR minimums discussed in Lesson 20).

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A property is projected to generate $2,000/month in net operating income against a $1,700/month debt payment — a DSCR of about 1.18. Stress-testing with rent reduced by 12% drops net operating income to roughly $1,760/month, for a stressed DSCR of about 1.04. [ASSUMPTION] If the investor's own minimum acceptable stressed DSCR is 1.10, this deal fails the stress test even though it looks reasonable under the original, optimistic projection — a signal to either negotiate a lower purchase price, put more down to reduce the debt payment, or walk away.

## Practice Quiz

1. What does it mean to stress-test a property's DSCR?
2. Why is stress-testing done before committing to a deal rather than after a downturn occurs?
3. In the worked example, what specific action might the failed stress test prompt the investor to consider?

## Answer Key

1. Recalculating the DSCR under a deliberately worse scenario — such as higher vacancy or lower rent — to see whether the property would still cover its debt payments.
2. Because it reveals hidden fragility while there's still a choice to walk away, negotiate, or restructure the deal, rather than discovering the fragility only after conditions have already worsened.
3. Negotiating a lower purchase price, increasing the down payment to reduce the debt payment, or walking away from the deal entirely.

## Key Takeaways
- Stress-testing DSCR means recalculating it under a deliberately worse scenario than the optimistic projection.
- This practice turns risk-management concepts from earlier lessons into a concrete, repeatable underwriting habit.
- A failed stress test is a signal to renegotiate terms, increase the down payment, or walk away — before committing, not after a downturn.
