## Learning Objectives

## Core Content

Every loan discussed so far in this track — following the amortization pattern from Lesson 7 — has each payment covering both interest and some principal, gradually paying down the balance. An **interest-only loan** is structured differently, at least for a set initial period: payments only cover the interest owed, with none of the payment reducing the principal balance.

Here's the "why" this creates a lower payment: since none of the payment goes toward principal during the interest-only period, the monthly payment is lower than an equivalent amortizing loan would require — [ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] potentially meaningfully lower in the early years. This can be attractive for cash flow purposes, or as a bridge (similar in spirit to Lesson 31) until a planned refinance or sale.

[VERIFIED FACT] After the interest-only period ends, the loan typically converts to a fully amortizing structure for the remaining term, which means the payment can increase noticeably — since the same remaining balance now has to be paid off (with interest) over a shorter remaining period than the original full term. This is a timing bet: it only works well if the investor has a specific plan for what happens once the interest-only period ends, not an open-ended assumption that the low payment will continue indefinitely.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An investor takes a 10-year interest-only loan on a 30-year term, with payments lower than a fully amortizing loan would require for the first 10 years. [ASSUMPTION] If she plans to refinance or sell before that 10-year mark — similar to the exit-strategy planning from Lesson 15 — the interest-only structure supports her strategy. If she has no such plan and simply continues holding the loan, the payment could increase substantially once the loan converts to amortizing over the remaining, shorter term, since none of the principal was paid down during those first 10 years.

## Practice Quiz

1. How does an interest-only loan's payment structure differ from a standard amortizing loan?
2. Why is the payment on an interest-only loan lower during the interest-only period?
3. What happens once the interest-only period ends, and why can the payment increase noticeably?

## Answer Key

1. Payments during the interest-only period cover only interest, with none going toward reducing the principal balance.
2. Because none of the payment is allocated to principal reduction, so the payment covers only the interest cost, which is lower than a payment that also includes principal.
3. The loan typically converts to a fully amortizing structure, and because no principal was paid down earlier, the same balance now must be repaid over a shorter remaining term — increasing the monthly payment.

## Answers and Explanations

2. This explains the mechanical reason for the lower payment.
3. This is the central risk and reinforces the need for a specific exit plan, connecting to Lesson 15.

## Key Takeaways
- Interest-only loans have payments covering only interest for a set period, with no principal reduction during that time.
- This creates a temporarily lower payment, useful for cash flow or as a bridge to a planned refinance or sale.
- Once the interest-only period ends, the payment typically increases as the loan converts to a fully amortizing structure — this only works well with a specific plan in place.
