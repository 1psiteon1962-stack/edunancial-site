## Core Content

Lesson 6 introduced the general risk of adjustable-rate loans as a portfolio scales. This lesson focuses on a specific protective feature built into many ARMs: **rate caps**. These typically limit how much the rate can increase at each adjustment period, and how much it can increase over the life of the loan overall.

Here's the "why" caps matter more than the initial rate: an ARM's starting rate — sometimes called a teaser rate — is often lower than a comparable fixed rate specifically to attract borrowers, but it reveals nothing about the loan's worst-case scenario. The caps do. [VERIFIED FACT] Cap structures vary by loan, but often include a per-adjustment cap (limiting how much the rate can rise at any single reset) and a lifetime cap (limiting the total possible increase over the loan's full term) — understanding both is necessary to know the true maximum payment the loan could ever require.

An investor evaluating an ARM should underwrite the deal against the lifetime cap's worst-case payment, not the attractive initial rate — echoing the stress-testing principle from Lesson 40, applied specifically to rate risk.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An ARM starts at a rate noticeably below current fixed rates, with a structure allowing up to a 2% increase per adjustment and a 5% lifetime cap above the starting rate. An investor evaluating only the attractive starting rate might underestimate the loan's real risk. [ASSUMPTION] Properly underwriting the deal means calculating the payment at the full 5% lifetime cap and confirming the property's projected income could still service that worst-case payment — not just the appealing initial one.

## Key Takeaways
- Rate caps limit how much an ARM's rate can rise per adjustment and over its lifetime.
- The initial teaser rate is often set low to attract borrowers and doesn't reflect the loan's true worst-case risk.
- A deal should be underwritten against the lifetime-cap worst-case payment, not the appealing starting rate.
