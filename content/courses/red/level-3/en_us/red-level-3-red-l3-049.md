## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
- Explain why nearly every financing concept in this track ultimately connects back to exit strategy
- Apply this synthesis by matching several financing tools from this track to the exit strategies they best support
- Explain why treating financing choice and exit planning as separate decisions creates risk

## Core Content

Lesson 15 established a foundational principle early in this track: exit strategy should be decided before financing is chosen, not after. This lesson revisits that principle now that many more tools have been introduced, showing how consistently it applies.

A long-term hold benefits from a fixed-rate loan (Lesson 6) for payment stability, benefits less from an interest-only structure (Lesson 41) since equity-building matters more over decades, and depends on stress-tested reserves (Lesson 40) to survive multiple economic cycles (Lesson 43) along the way.

A short-term hold planned around a refinance (BRRRR, Lesson 9) needs financing with low or no prepayment penalties (Lesson 23), may reasonably use a bridge or hard money loan (Lessons 17 and 31) as a starting point, and depends heavily on the appraisal risk discussed in Lesson 9 actually resolving favorably.

A planned sale within a few years needs to account for the amortization timeline (Lesson 7) — how much real equity will actually exist by the sale date — and may benefit from 1031 exchange planning (Lesson 12) if proceeds will be reinvested into another property.

The "why" tying all of this together: none of the individual financing tools covered in this track are inherently "good" or "bad" — each is well-suited to a specific exit plan and poorly suited to others. Choosing a tool without first deciding the plan it needs to support is choosing somewhat randomly, even if the tool itself is sound.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An investor picks an interest-only loan (Lesson 41) because the lower initial payment looks appealing, without having decided whether she plans to hold long-term or sell soon. If she later decides to hold long-term, she's delayed equity-building for years with nothing to show for it once the interest-only period ends. If she'd decided on her exit plan first — sell within 5 years, for instance — the interest-only structure might have been a reasonable, deliberate choice rather than one made without a clear plan behind it.

## Answer Key

1. That exit strategy should be decided before financing is chosen, not after.
2. Long-term hold: a fixed-rate loan (Lesson 6). Short-term/refinance-focused: a bridge or hard money loan with low prepayment penalties (Lessons 17, 23, 31).
3. Because each tool is well-suited to specific circumstances and poorly suited to others — without a plan, the choice is essentially made at random, and a mismatch may not become apparent until it's costly to correct.

## Key Takeaways
- Nearly every financing tool covered in this track is well-suited to a specific exit strategy and poorly suited to others.
- Long-term holds favor stability (fixed rates, reserves); short-term, refinance-focused strategies favor flexibility (low prepayment penalties, bridge financing).
- Deciding an exit plan before choosing financing prevents a costly mismatch discovered too late.
