# Introduction to Financial Markets

## Executive Summary
WHITE-L1-001-MAIN

Financial markets are the systems that let people and institutions buy and sell financial assets — stocks, bonds, currencies, and more. This lesson introduces what financial markets are, why they exist, and the basic categories a new investor needs to recognize before going further into the WHITE track.

## Learning Objectives
- Define a financial market in plain terms.
- Identify the main categories of financial markets (equity, bond, money, and derivatives markets).
- Explain the basic function markets serve for both companies and investors.
- Recognize the difference between a primary market and a secondary market.

## Prerequisites
None. This is the entry point of the WHITE track.

## Estimated Completion Time
25 minutes

## Difficulty Level
Beginner (Level 1)

## Main Lesson
WHITE-L1-001-MAIN

### Topic 1: What Is a Financial Market?
[VERIFIED FACT] A financial market is any marketplace where buyers and sellers trade financial assets — such as stocks, bonds, currencies, and derivatives — at prices determined by supply and demand.

Financial markets exist because they connect two groups with opposite needs: people or businesses who need money now (issuers, borrowers) and people or businesses who have money to invest (savers, investors). The market is the mechanism that brings these two groups together.

### Topic 2: Primary vs. Secondary Markets
[VERIFIED FACT] In a primary market, a financial asset is created and sold for the first time — for example, a company issuing new shares in an Initial Public Offering (IPO). In a secondary market, previously issued assets are traded between investors — for example, buying shares of that same company on a stock exchange the following year.

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] When a company sells stock directly to investors for the first time, that sale happens in the primary market. Every trade after that, between one investor and another, happens in the secondary market — the company itself is not a party to those later trades.

### Topic 3: The Four Major Market Categories
- **Equity (Stock) Markets** — where shares of ownership in companies are bought and sold.
- **Bond (Fixed Income) Markets** — where debt instruments issued by governments and companies are traded.
- **Money Markets** — where short-term, highly liquid instruments (such as treasury bills) are traded, typically maturing in under a year.
- **Derivatives Markets** — where contracts whose value is based on an underlying asset (such as options or futures) are traded.

[REGION-SPECIFIC RULE] The specific exchanges, regulators, and trading hours for each of these markets vary by country. Learners should confirm the relevant regulatory body and exchange structure for their own jurisdiction before trading.

### Topic 4: Why Markets Matter
Financial markets allow companies to raise capital to grow, allow governments to fund public projects, and allow individual investors to grow personal wealth over time by participating in that growth. Later WHITE lessons build directly on this foundation — stocks (WHITE-L1-002), bonds (WHITE-L1-003), and diversification across both (WHITE-L1-005).

## Socratic Teaching Guide
WHITE-L1-001-SOCRATIC

1. Before we define it — what do you picture when you hear the phrase "the stock market"? Where does that picture come from?
2. If a company already has money from selling its product, why would it also need to raise money by issuing stock?
3. When you buy a share of stock from another investor on an exchange, does the company that issued that stock receive any of your money? Why might that surprise people?
4. What's the difference between "the market went up today" and "a specific stock went up today"? Are these always the same thing?
5. If money markets deal in short-term instruments and bond markets deal in longer-term debt, what tradeoff might an investor be making by choosing one over the other?
6. Why might a government want to issue debt (bonds) instead of just raising taxes?
7. What real-world outcome depends on financial markets functioning smoothly — for a small business owner? For a retiree?
8. If markets didn't exist and every investment had to be negotiated privately, one deal at a time, what would change about how easy it is to invest?
9. What's one assumption you're currently making about "how the stock market works" that this lesson either confirmed or challenged?
10. What question do you still have after this lesson that you'd want answered before you'd feel comfortable investing your own money?

# Understanding Stocks

## Executive Summary
WHITE-L1-002-MAIN

A share of stock is a unit of ownership in a company. This lesson explains what owning stock actually means, how investors make or lose money on it, and the difference between common and preferred stock.

## Learning Objectives
- Define what a share of stock represents.
- Explain the two main ways investors can earn a return from stock ownership.
- Distinguish between common stock and preferred stock.
- Identify basic risks associated with stock ownership.

## Prerequisites
WHITE-L1-001 (Introduction to Financial Markets)

## Estimated Completion Time
25 minutes

## Difficulty Level
Beginner (Level 1)

## Main Lesson
WHITE-L1-002-MAIN

### Topic 1: What Owning a Share Means
[VERIFIED FACT] A share of stock represents a fractional ownership stake in a company. If a company has 1,000,000 shares outstanding and you own 1,000 shares, you own 0.1% of that company.

Ownership generally comes with certain rights, such as voting on major corporate matters and a claim on a proportional share of the company's assets and earnings — though the specific rights depend on the class of stock and jurisdiction.

### Topic 2: How Investors Earn a Return
- **Capital Appreciation** — selling the stock for more than you paid for it.
- **Dividends** — a portion of company profits paid directly to shareholders, when and if the company chooses to pay them.

[ASSUMPTION] Not all companies pay dividends. Younger or growth-focused companies often reinvest profits into the business instead of distributing them to shareholders.

### Topic 3: Common Stock vs. Preferred Stock
[VERIFIED FACT] Common stock typically carries voting rights and variable dividends (if any). Preferred stock typically does not carry voting rights but usually receives a fixed dividend and has a higher claim than common stock on company assets if the company is liquidated.

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] If a company goes bankrupt and its remaining assets are distributed, preferred shareholders are generally paid before common shareholders — but both are paid after the company's creditors (lenders, bondholders) are paid first.

### Topic 4: Basic Risks of Stock Ownership
[VERIFIED FACT] Stock prices can rise or fall based on company performance, broader economic conditions, investor sentiment, and other factors. Unlike a savings account, stock investments are not guaranteed and can lose value, including the possibility of losing the entire investment if a company fails.

[OPINION] Some investors believe short-term price swings matter less than long-term company fundamentals; others actively trade based on short-term price movement. This lesson does not take a position on which approach is superior — later lessons (WHITE-L1-007, WHITE-L1-010) explore portfolio-building and common mistakes in more depth.

## Socratic Teaching Guide
WHITE-L1-002-SOCRATIC

1. If you own 0.1% of a company, what does that actually entitle you to — and what doesn't it entitle you to?
2. Why might a company choose to reinvest its profits instead of paying a dividend?
3. If preferred stock gets paid before common stock in a liquidation but doesn't usually vote — what's the tradeoff being made by each type of shareholder?
4. What's the difference between a stock "losing value" and a stock "losing all its value"? What would cause each?
5. Would you rather own stock in a company with a high dividend and slow growth, or no dividend and fast growth? Why?
6. If a friend told you "stocks always go up over time," how would you respond using what you've learned in this lesson?
7. What information would you want to know about a company before buying its stock?
8. How does owning a share of stock differ from lending a company money (as in WHITE-L1-003, bonds)?
9. If voting rights come with common stock, do you think the average retail investor actually uses that right? Why or why not?
10. What's one thing about stock ownership that this lesson clarified that you weren't sure about before?

## Assessment
WHITE-L1-002-ASSESSMENT

1. (Multiple Choice) A share of stock represents:
   a) A loan made to a company
   b) A fractional ownership stake in a company
   c) A guaranteed fixed payment
   d) A government-issued security

2. (Multiple Choice) Which type of stock typically does NOT carry voting rights?
   a) Common stock
   b) Preferred stock
   c) Both carry voting rights equally
   d) Neither carries voting rights

3. (True/False) All companies are required to pay dividends to shareholders.

4. (Short Answer) Name the two main ways an investor can earn a return from owning stock.

5. (Short Answer) In a company liquidation, who is generally paid first: creditors, preferred shareholders, or common shareholders? Explain the order.

## Answer Key
WHITE-L1-002-ANSWER-KEY

1. b) A fractional ownership stake in a company
2. b) Preferred stock
3. False — dividends are discretionary; not all companies pay them.
4. Capital appreciation and dividends.
5. Creditors are paid first, then preferred shareholders, then common shareholders.

# Introduction to Bonds and Fixed Income

## Executive Summary
WHITE-L1-003-MAIN

A bond is essentially a loan made by an investor to a borrower — typically a government or a company. This lesson explains how bonds work, why they're called "fixed income," and how they differ from stocks in risk and return.

## Learning Objectives
- Define a bond and identify the key parties involved.
- Explain the terms "face value," "coupon rate," and "maturity."
- Describe how bond prices relate to interest rates.
- Compare the general risk and return profile of bonds to stocks.

## Prerequisites
WHITE-L1-001, WHITE-L1-002

## Estimated Completion Time
25 minutes

## Difficulty Level
Beginner (Level 1)

## Main Lesson
WHITE-L1-003-MAIN

### Topic 1: What Is a Bond?
[VERIFIED FACT] A bond is a debt instrument in which an investor lends money to an issuer (a government, municipality, or corporation) in exchange for periodic interest payments and the return of the loan's principal at a set future date.

### Topic 2: Key Bond Terminology
- **Face Value (Par Value)** — the amount the bondholder is repaid at maturity.
- **Coupon Rate** — the fixed interest rate the bond pays, usually expressed annually.
- **Maturity Date** — the date on which the issuer repays the face value to the bondholder.

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A $1,000 face value bond with a 5% coupon rate pays $50 per year in interest until maturity, at which point the investor receives the original $1,000 back (assuming the issuer does not default).

### Topic 3: Bond Prices and Interest Rates
[VERIFIED FACT] Bond prices and interest rates generally move in opposite directions. When market interest rates rise, existing bonds with lower fixed coupon rates become less attractive, so their price tends to fall. When market interest rates fall, existing bonds with higher fixed coupon rates become more attractive, so their price tends to rise.

[ASSUMPTION] This inverse relationship assumes the bond is held and traded before maturity. An investor who holds a bond to maturity and the issuer does not default will still receive the face value regardless of interim price fluctuations.

### Topic 4: Bonds vs. Stocks — General Risk and Return
[OPINION] Bonds are often considered lower-risk than stocks because bondholders have a contractual right to interest and principal repayment and are paid before stockholders in a liquidation. However, "lower risk" does not mean "no risk" — bond issuers can still default, and bond prices can still fluctuate.

[REGION-SPECIFIC RULE] Government bond risk varies significantly by country. Bonds issued by financially stable governments are generally considered safer than bonds issued by governments with a history of default; learners should not assume all government bonds carry identical risk.

## Socratic Teaching Guide
WHITE-L1-003-SOCRATIC

1. If a bond is essentially a loan, who is the lender and who is the borrower in that relationship?
2. Why would an investor accept a fixed interest payment instead of the potentially higher (but less certain) returns of stock ownership?
3. If interest rates rise after you buy a bond, why does the value of your existing bond go down?
4. Would you rather hold a bond to maturity or sell it early? What would influence that decision?
5. What does it mean for a bond issuer to "default," and who bears that risk?
6. Why might a government bond from one country carry different risk than a government bond from another country?
7. If bonds are generally paid before stockholders in a liquidation, does that mean bonds are risk-free? Why or why not?
8. How is a coupon rate different from the actual return an investor might earn if they buy or sell a bond before maturity?
9. What role might bonds play in a portfolio alongside stocks? (We'll explore this further in WHITE-L1-005 and WHITE-L1-007.)
10. What's one question you still have about how bonds actually work in practice?

WHITE-L1-003-ASSESSMENT

   c) Market rate
   d) Dividend

4. (Short Answer) Define "coupon rate" in your own words.

5. (Short Answer) Explain why bonds are generally considered lower-risk than stocks, and note one reason this doesn't mean bonds are risk-free.

## Answer Key
WHITE-L1-003-ANSWER-KEY

1. b) Face value
2. b) Falls
3. False — government bond risk varies significantly by the financial stability of the issuing country.
4. Sample answer: The coupon rate is the fixed interest rate a bond pays, usually stated as an annual percentage of its face value.
5. Sample answer: Bonds are generally lower-risk because bondholders have a contractual right to interest and principal and are paid before stockholders in a liquidation. However, issuers can still default and bond prices can still fluctuate, so bonds are not risk-free.

# Mutual Funds and Exchange-Traded Funds (ETFs)

## Executive Summary
WHITE-L1-004-MAIN

Rather than buying individual stocks or bonds one at a time, many investors pool their money into funds that hold many securities at once. This lesson introduces mutual funds and ETFs, how they work, and the key differences between them.

## Learning Objectives
- Define a mutual fund and an ETF.
- Explain the concept of pooled investing.
- Identify key differences between mutual funds and ETFs (trading, pricing, fees).
- Recognize the general purpose of an expense ratio.

## Prerequisites
WHITE-L1-001, WHITE-L1-002, WHITE-L1-003

## Estimated Completion Time
25 minutes

## Difficulty Level
Beginner (Level 1)

## Main Lesson
WHITE-L1-004-MAIN

### Topic 1: What Is Pooled Investing?
[VERIFIED FACT] A mutual fund or ETF pools money from many investors to purchase a diversified collection of assets — such as stocks, bonds, or a mix of both — rather than requiring each investor to buy every individual security themselves.

### Topic 2: Mutual Funds
[VERIFIED FACT] A mutual fund is priced once per day, after markets close, based on its Net Asset Value (NAV). Orders to buy or sell shares of a mutual fund are executed at that day's closing NAV, not throughout the trading day.

### Topic 3: Exchange-Traded Funds (ETFs)
[VERIFIED FACT] An ETF trades on a stock exchange throughout the trading day, similarly to an individual stock, with a price that fluctuates continuously based on supply and demand — unlike a mutual fund, which prices only once daily.

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] If you place an order to buy a mutual fund at 10 a.m., your order will typically be executed at that day's closing NAV price, calculated after the market closes. If you place an order to buy an ETF at 10 a.m., it typically executes near the market price at that moment.

### Topic 4: Fees — The Expense Ratio
[VERIFIED FACT] Both mutual funds and ETFs typically charge an expense ratio — an annual fee, expressed as a percentage of assets invested, that covers the fund's management and operating costs. This fee is deducted automatically from the fund's returns.

[ESTIMATE] Expense ratios vary widely by fund type and provider; actively managed funds have historically tended to charge higher expense ratios than passively managed index funds, though exact figures should be checked for any specific fund before investing.

## Socratic Teaching Guide
WHITE-L1-004-SOCRATIC

1. Why might an investor prefer to buy one fund holding hundreds of stocks instead of buying each stock individually?
2. What practical difference does it make to you as an investor whether a fund prices once a day versus continuously throughout the day?
3. If two funds hold nearly identical assets but have different expense ratios, what effect might that difference have over 20 or 30 years?
4. Would you expect an actively managed fund or a passively managed index fund to typically have higher fees? Why might that be?
5. What's one scenario where the "trade anytime during the day" feature of an ETF might actually matter to an investor?
6. If a fund's expense ratio is 1% per year, what is that actually costing you in dollar terms on a $10,000 investment?
7. How does pooled investing relate to the diversification concept we'll cover in WHITE-L1-005?
8. What questions would you want answered before choosing between two similar funds?
9. Does a lower expense ratio automatically mean a better investment? What else might matter?
10. What's one thing about mutual funds or ETFs that surprised you in this lesson?

## Assessment
WHITE-L1-004-ASSESSMENT

   a) Continuously throughout the trading day

   c) Once per week
   d) Only at the start of the trading day

2. (Multiple Choice) An ETF differs from a mutual fund primarily because it:
   a) Cannot hold stocks
   b) Trades on an exchange throughout the day like a stock
   c) Is always more expensive
   d) Has no expense ratio

3. (True/False) An expense ratio is a one-time fee charged only when you first invest in a fund.

4. (Short Answer) Define "pooled investing" in your own words.

5. (Short Answer) Explain the main pricing/trading difference between a mutual fund and an ETF.

## Answer Key
WHITE-L1-004-ANSWER-KEY

1. b) Once per day, after markets close
2. b) Trades on an exchange throughout the day like a stock
3. False — an expense ratio is an ongoing annual fee, not a one-time charge.
4. Sample answer: Pooled investing means combining money from many investors to buy a diversified collection of assets together, rather than each investor buying every security individually.
5. Sample answer: A mutual fund is priced once daily after market close and trades execute at that closing price; an ETF trades continuously during the day at fluctuating market prices, similar to an individual stock.

# The Basics of Diversification

## Executive Summary
WHITE-L1-005-MAIN

Diversification is the practice of spreading investments across different assets to reduce risk. This lesson explains why diversification works, what it can and cannot protect against, and how it connects to the asset types introduced earlier in this level.

## Learning Objectives
- Define diversification and explain its core purpose.
- Distinguish between diversifiable and non-diversifiable risk.

## Estimated Completion Time

## Difficulty Level
Beginner (Level 1)

## Main Lesson
WHITE-L1-005-MAIN

### Topic 1: What Is Diversification?
[VERIFIED FACT] Diversification is an investment strategy of holding a variety of different assets so that poor performance in any single investment has a limited effect on the overall portfolio.

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An investor holding only one company's stock is fully exposed if that company performs poorly. An investor holding shares across 50 different companies in different industries is far less affected if any single company underperforms.

### Topic 2: Diversifiable vs. Non-Diversifiable Risk
[VERIFIED FACT] Diversifiable risk (also called unsystematic risk) is risk specific to a single company or industry, and can be reduced by holding a variety of investments. Non-diversifiable risk (also called systematic risk) affects the broader market or economy as a whole and cannot be eliminated through diversification alone — examples include recessions, interest rate changes, or global economic events.

### Topic 3: Ways to Diversify
- **Across Asset Classes** — combining stocks, bonds, and other asset types (as introduced in WHITE-L1-002 through WHITE-L1-004).
- **Across Sectors/Industries** — avoiding overconcentration in a single industry (e.g., technology alone).
- **Across Geography** — holding investments across multiple countries or regions, not just one domestic market.

[ASSUMPTION] The right diversification mix depends on an individual's goals, timeline, and risk tolerance; this lesson does not prescribe a specific allocation, which is explored further in WHITE-L1-007.

### Topic 4: The Limits of Diversification
[VERIFIED FACT] Diversification reduces risk but does not eliminate it. A well-diversified portfolio can still lose value during broad market downturns, since non-diversifiable risk affects nearly all assets to some degree.

[OPINION] Some investors treat diversification as a guarantee against loss. This is a misunderstanding this lesson explicitly corrects — diversification manages risk, it does not remove it.

# Understanding Credit, Interest, and Debt

## Executive Summary
WHITE-L1-006-MAIN

Before building an investment portfolio, it helps to understand the mechanics of credit and debt — since interest works both for you (as a bond investor) and against you (as a borrower). This lesson introduces credit scores, interest rates, and the difference between good and bad debt.

## Learning Objectives
- Define credit, interest, and debt in plain terms.
- Explain how compound interest works, using a simple example.
- Describe what a credit score generally measures.
- Distinguish between generally productive and generally unproductive uses of debt.

## Prerequisites
WHITE-L1-001 through WHITE-L1-005

## Estimated Completion Time
25 minutes

## Difficulty Level
Beginner (Level 1)

## Main Lesson
WHITE-L1-006-MAIN

### Topic 1: What Are Credit and Debt?
[VERIFIED FACT] Credit is the ability to borrow money or access goods/services with the agreement to pay later. Debt is the amount owed as a result of borrowing. Interest is the cost charged by a lender for the use of borrowed money, usually expressed as an annual percentage rate.

### Topic 2: How Compound Interest Works
[VERIFIED FACT] Compound interest is interest calculated on both the original principal and on interest that has already accumulated. This means debt (or savings) can grow faster over time than with simple interest, which is calculated only on the original principal.

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] $1,000 borrowed at 10% simple annual interest owes $100 in interest each year. The same $1,000 at 10% compound annual interest owes $100 in year one, but in year two, interest is calculated on $1,100 (principal plus prior interest) — resulting in $110 of interest for that year, and the gap widens each year after.

### Topic 3: What Is a Credit Score?
[VERIFIED FACT] A credit score is a numerical representation, generated by a credit reporting system, intended to reflect a borrower's creditworthiness based on factors such as repayment history, amounts owed, length of credit history, and other factors.

[REGION-SPECIFIC RULE] Credit scoring systems, the specific factors weighted, and the scoring ranges used differ significantly by country. Learners should research the credit reporting system used in their own jurisdiction rather than assuming a single global standard.

### Topic 4: Productive vs. Unproductive Debt
[OPINION] Some educators categorize debt as "good" or "bad" based on whether it is used to acquire an asset likely to appreciate or generate income (such as a mortgage on rental property, addressed in the RED track) versus debt used for depreciating assets or consumption with no lasting value. This lesson uses the terms "generally productive" and "generally unproductive" to describe this distinction, while acknowledging that individual circumstances vary and this is a general framework, not a strict rule.

[ASSUMPTION] Whether any specific use of debt is "productive" depends heavily on the interest rate, the borrower's ability to repay, and the actual outcome of how the borrowed funds are used — this lesson provides a general framework, not individualized financial advice.

## Main Lesson
WHITE-L1-007-MAIN

### Topic 1: What Is Asset Allocation?
[VERIFIED FACT] Asset allocation is the process of dividing an investment portfolio among different asset categories, such as stocks, bonds, and cash, based on an investor's goals, time horizon, and risk tolerance.

### Topic 2: Time Horizon
[VERIFIED FACT] Time horizon refers to the length of time an investor expects to hold an investment before needing the money. Generally, a longer time horizon allows an investor more time to recover from short-term market downturns, which is one reason younger investors saving for distant goals (such as retirement) are often able to consider a higher allocation to stocks relative to bonds.

[ASSUMPTION] This is a general tendency, not a rule that applies to every individual. Personal circumstances, income stability, and risk tolerance also matter and can outweigh time horizon alone.

### Topic 3: Risk Tolerance
[VERIFIED FACT] Risk tolerance is an investor's psychological and financial capacity to withstand investment losses without abandoning their investment strategy. It is influenced by both financial factors (income, savings, obligations) and personal factors (comfort with uncertainty).

[OPINION] There is no single "correct" risk tolerance — the right allocation is one an investor can realistically stick with during a market downturn, since abandoning a strategy during a decline (as discussed further in WHITE-L1-010) often causes more harm than the decline itself.

### Topic 4: A Conceptual Beginner Framework
[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A commonly referenced (though not universally prescribed) starting framework combines a mix of stocks and bonds based on age or time horizon, with the stock percentage decreasing and bond percentage increasing as an investor's time horizon shortens. For example, an investor decades from a financial goal might weight more heavily toward stocks and diversified equity funds (WHITE-L1-002, WHITE-L1-004), while an investor close to needing the funds might weight more heavily toward bonds (WHITE-L1-003).

[ASSUMPTION] This is presented as one illustrative framework among several possible approaches, not as individualized financial advice. Actual allocation decisions should account for an individual's complete financial picture.

# Introduction to Options Trading

## Executive Summary
WHITE-L1-009-MAIN

Options are contracts that give the holder a right — but not an obligation — to buy or sell an asset at a set price within a specific time frame. This lesson introduces basic options concepts at a beginner level and flags the elevated risk involved compared to the asset types covered earlier in this level.

## Learning Objectives
- Define an option and distinguish it from owning the underlying asset directly.
- Explain the difference between a call option and a put option.
- Identify the key components of an option contract (strike price, expiration date, premium).
- Recognize why options are generally considered higher-risk than the instruments covered in WHITE-L1-002 through WHITE-L1-004.

## Prerequisites
WHITE-L1-001 through WHITE-L1-008

## Estimated Completion Time
30 minutes

## Difficulty Level
Beginner-Intermediate (Level 1, advanced topic)

## Main Lesson
WHITE-L1-009-MAIN

### Topic 1: What Is an Option?
[VERIFIED FACT] An option is a derivatives contract (referenced in WHITE-L1-001, Topic 3) that gives the buyer the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell an underlying asset at a predetermined price (the strike price) on or before a specific date (the expiration date), in exchange for an upfront payment (the premium).

### Topic 2: Call Options vs. Put Options
[VERIFIED FACT] A call option gives the holder the right to buy the underlying asset at the strike price. A put option gives the holder the right to sell the underlying asset at the strike price.

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] If an investor buys a call option on a stock with a strike price of $50 and the stock later rises to $70, the option holder has the right to buy at $50 — potentially profitable. If the stock instead falls to $40, the option holder is not obligated to buy at $50 and may simply let the option expire, losing only the premium paid.

### Topic 3: Key Contract Components
- **Strike Price** — the price at which the underlying asset can be bought (call) or sold (put).
- **Expiration Date** — the date by which the option must be exercised or it becomes worthless.
- **Premium** — the price paid upfront to purchase the option contract, which is the maximum an option buyer can lose.

### Topic 4: Why Options Carry Elevated Risk
[VERIFIED FACT] Unlike buying a stock directly (WHITE-L1-002), an option has a built-in expiration date, meaning it can lose all of its value if the underlying asset does not move favorably before that date — even if the underlying asset eventually would have moved favorably later.

[OPINION] Options are generally considered more complex and higher-risk than the stock, bond, and fund instruments covered earlier in WHITE Level 1, and are often more appropriate for investors with a solid grounding in the fundamentals (WHITE-L1-002 through WHITE-L1-007) before use. This lesson provides only a conceptual introduction, not a trading guide.

[ASSUMPTION] Some options strategies (such as selling, rather than buying, options) carry different — and in some cases greater or theoretically unlimited — risk profiles not covered in this introductory lesson.

# Common Investing Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

## Executive Summary
WHITE-L1-010-MAIN

This capstone lesson reviews the most common behavioral and structural mistakes new investors make, drawing on every concept introduced earlier in WHITE Level 1, and serves as a checkpoint before advancing further in the WHITE track.

## Learning Objectives

- Identify common structural mistakes, including poor diversification and ignoring fees.
- Connect each mistake back to the specific WHITE Level 1 lesson that addresses it.
- Apply this review to evaluate a realistic investing scenario.

## Prerequisites
WHITE-L1-001 through WHITE-L1-009 (completion of WHITE Level 1)

## Estimated Completion Time
30 minutes

## Difficulty Level
Beginner (Level 1, capstone review)

## Main Lesson
WHITE-L1-010-MAIN

### Topic 1: Emotional/Behavioral Mistakes
[VERIFIED FACT] Panic selling — selling investments during a market downturn out of fear rather than a change in underlying strategy or circumstances — is a commonly cited behavioral mistake, as it can lock in losses that might have otherwise recovered over time.

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An investor with a 30-year time horizon (WHITE-L1-007) who sells all their stock holdings during a temporary market decline converts a temporary paper loss into a permanent realized loss, and may miss the subsequent recovery.

[OPINION] This does not mean an investor should never sell — but the decision should generally be based on a change in goals, time horizon, or strategy, not solely on short-term price movement or fear.

### Topic 2: Poor Diversification
[VERIFIED FACT] Concentrating investments in a single stock, sector, or asset type (contrary to the principles in WHITE-L1-005) exposes an investor to unnecessary diversifiable risk that could otherwise be reduced.

### Topic 3: Ignoring Fees
[VERIFIED FACT] Small differences in expense ratios (WHITE-L1-004) can compound into significant differences in long-term returns, since fees are deducted from returns every year, compounding the drag over time in the same way that compound interest amplifies growth (WHITE-L1-006).

### Topic 4: Chasing Performance and Ignoring Risk Tolerance
[VERIFIED FACT] Chasing recent high performers without regard to overall allocation strategy (WHITE-L1-007) can lead to buying assets after they have already risen significantly and selling after they have already fallen — the opposite of a disciplined buy-low, sell-high approach.

[ASSUMPTION] Past performance of any individual investment is not a guarantee of future results; this is a foundational assumption underlying this entire topic.

### Topic 5: Misunderstanding Risk (Including Higher-Risk Instruments)
[VERIFIED FACT] Using higher-risk instruments such as options (WHITE-L1-009) without a solid foundation in the underlying fundamentals (WHITE-L1-002 through WHITE-L1-007) is a commonly cited mistake among new investors.
