# WHITE-L1-006: What Is a Brokerage Account?

## Learning Objectives
1. Define a brokerage account.
2. Distinguish a brokerage account from a bank account.
3. Identify basic account types (individual, joint, retirement).

## Core Content
A brokerage account is an account that allows an investor to buy and sell securities like shares, bonds, mutual funds, and ETFs, opened through a licensed brokerage firm. *VERIFIED FACT:* Unlike a bank account, money in a brokerage account is typically invested, not simply held as cash, and its value can rise or fall with market conditions.

Common account types include individual accounts (one owner), joint accounts (multiple owners), and tax-advantaged retirement accounts (like a 401(k) or IRA, covered in later lessons), each with different tax treatment and rules.

## Worked Example
*ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE:* Aisha opens an individual brokerage account and deposits $1,000. Unlike a savings account where that $1,000 would sit as cash earning modest interest, she uses it to buy shares of a share. Her balance now fluctuates daily with that share's price — she could see $1,100 or $900 depending on market movement, a fundamentally different risk profile than a bank account.

## Practice Quiz
1. What is a brokerage account used for?
2. How does a brokerage account differ from a typical bank account?
3. Name two common account ownership types.
4. True/False: Money in a brokerage account always earns a fixed, predictable return like a savings account.

## Answer Key
1. Buying and selling securities   2. Brokerage funds are typically invested and can fluctuate; bank funds are typically held as stable cash   3. Individual, joint (among others)   4. False

## Answers and Explanations
1. This is the core function of a brokerage account.
2. Investment risk and fluctuation distinguish brokerage accounts from bank deposits.
3. These are standard account ownership structures.
4. Brokerage account value depends on the underlying investments, not a fixed rate.

## Key Takeaways
- A brokerage account enables buying and selling of securities.
- Unlike bank accounts, brokerage funds are typically invested and can fluctuate in value.
- Account types (individual, joint, retirement) carry different rules and tax treatment.
