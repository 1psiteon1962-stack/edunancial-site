## Core Content

Almost everyone has heard the advice "buy low, sell high." It's true — and it's nearly useless on its own, because it assumes you can identify "low" and "high" while you're living through them, not just when looking back at a chart.

Here's the "why" behind the difficulty: a price drop that looks like "low" in hindsight usually doesn't feel like an opportunity while it's happening — it feels like uncertainty, bad news, and real financial pain. [VERIFIED FACT] Markets historically decline sharply during periods of significant economic stress or uncertainty, which is exactly when buying can feel most difficult, even though those periods have often preceded eventual recoveries. Similarly, a price that looks like "high" in hindsight often feels, at the time, like unstoppable momentum — everyone else seems to be making money, and selling feels like leaving gains on the table.

This is why "buy low, sell high" is better understood as a description of a desired outcome than as actionable advice on its own. The lessons that follow in this track dig into specific tools, concepts, and behavioral patterns that make it more possible to act rationally when your instincts are pulling the other way.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] During a sharp market downturn, an investor sees her portfolio drop significantly in value. In hindsight, this period turns out to have been a strong long-term buying opportunity. But living through it, she was reading discouraging headlines, watching her account balance fall daily, and feeling genuine anxiety about further losses — the exact opposite of a comfortable "this is clearly low" feeling. [ASSUMPTION] Investors who bought during that discomfort, rather than waiting for confirmation that the bottom had passed, historically captured more of the eventual recovery — but doing so required acting against how the moment actually felt.

## Practice Quiz

1. Why is "buy low, sell high" easier to say than to actually do?
2. Why might a genuine buying opportunity feel uncomfortable rather than obvious while it's happening?
3. Why might a period of strong momentum feel like a poor time to sell, even if it later turns out to have been a high point?

## Answer Key

1. Because "low" and "high" are only clearly identifiable in hindsight — in the moment, low prices are usually accompanied by uncertainty and bad news, and high prices by apparent momentum.
2. Because it typically coincides with negative headlines, falling account values, and real uncertainty about whether prices will fall further.
3. Because strong momentum can create a feeling that further gains are likely, making it emotionally difficult to sell into apparent strength.

## Answers and Explanations

1. This is the central paradox the lesson introduces.
2. This explains the specific emotional experience that makes "low" hard to recognize in real time.
3. This mirrors the previous answer from the opposite direction, on the "high" side of the advice.

## Key Takeaways
- "Buy low, sell high" describes a desired outcome, not an actionable strategy on its own.
- Genuine low points often feel uncertain and uncomfortable while they're happening, not obviously like opportunities.
- Genuine high points often feel like strong momentum, making it hard to sell.
- The rest of this track introduces specific tools and concepts that make acting on this advice more achievable.
