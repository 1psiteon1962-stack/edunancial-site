Title: Why Diversification Reduces Risk Without Reducing Expected Return (as Much as You'd Think)

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
- Explain why diversification reduces risk without proportionally reducing expected return
- Distinguish between risk specific to one company and risk affecting the whole market
- Explain why diversification has diminishing benefits past a certain point

## Core Content

It might seem like spreading your money across many investments instead of concentrating it in one would also spread out — and reduce — your expected return. That's not quite how it works, and understanding why is central to this lesson.

Risk in an individual stock comes from two sources: risk specific to that one company (a bad earnings report, a product recall, a lawsuit) and risk that affects the whole market (a recession, broad interest rate changes). Diversification — holding many different stocks instead of one — cancels out much of the company-specific risk, because bad news at one company is unlikely to happen to all your holdings at once. It does very little, however, to cancel out market-wide risk, since that affects nearly everything together.

Here's the "why" this doesn't proportionally reduce expected return: you're not diluting your best ideas with weaker ones in a way that necessarily lowers your average expected outcome — you're removing the specific, uncompensated risk of any single company's bad luck, while keeping your expected exposure to the market's overall return. [VERIFIED FACT] This benefit has diminishing returns — the risk-reduction benefit of adding more holdings drops off substantially after a portfolio reaches a moderate number of different companies, since most remaining risk at that point is the market-wide risk diversification can't remove.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An investor holding only one company's stock is exposed to that specific company's risk — a scandal, a failed product, or a lawsuit could sharply hurt her portfolio even if the broader market is fine. An investor holding shares across 30 different companies in different industries is far less exposed to any single company's bad news; a problem at one holding is a small drag on the total portfolio rather than a major loss. [ASSUMPTION] Going from 30 holdings to 300 typically adds much less additional risk reduction than going from 1 holding to 30 did — most of the achievable, company-specific risk reduction has already been captured.

## Practice Quiz

1. What are the two sources of risk in an individual stock, as described in this lesson?
2. Why doesn't diversification proportionally reduce expected return the way it reduces risk?
3. Why does adding more and more holdings eventually produce diminishing risk-reduction benefits?

## Answer Key

1. Company-specific risk (unique to one business) and market-wide risk (affecting nearly everything together).
2. Because diversification primarily removes uncompensated, company-specific risk rather than diluting overall expected exposure to market returns.
3. Because most company-specific risk is already reduced after a moderate number of holdings, and the remaining risk is largely market-wide risk, which diversification across individual stocks can't remove.

## Answers and Explanations

1. This distinction is the foundation the entire lesson depends on.
2. This is the central, often-misunderstood "why" of the lesson.
3. This explains the diminishing-returns pattern and previews why diversifying across asset classes (a later lesson) matters for the market-wide risk that individual-stock diversification can't touch.

## Key Takeaways
- Diversification primarily reduces company-specific risk, not market-wide risk.
- This is why it can lower risk without proportionally lowering expected return — it removes largely uncompensated risk, not upside.
- The risk-reduction benefit of adding more holdings diminishes substantially after a moderate number of different companies.
