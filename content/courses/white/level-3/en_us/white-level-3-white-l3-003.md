## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
- Define correlation in the context of investment returns
- Explain why owning many holdings that move together isn't real diversification
- Explain why low or negative correlation between assets provides stronger diversification benefits

## Core Content

Lesson 2 established that diversification reduces company-specific risk. This lesson adds an important refinement: simply owning many different holdings isn't enough — what matters is how those holdings move in relation to each other, a concept called **correlation**.

Correlation measures the degree to which two investments tend to move together. Highly correlated assets tend to rise and fall at similar times, for similar reasons. Low or negatively correlated assets tend to respond differently to the same events — one might hold steady or even rise while the other falls.

Here's the "why" this matters more than raw holding count: [ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] an investor holding 50 different technology stocks has numerically diversified across many companies, but if all 50 are highly correlated — likely to fall together during a broad tech-sector downturn — the real diversification benefit is much smaller than the number "50" suggests. True risk reduction comes from combining assets that don't all respond to the same events in the same way.

[VERIFIED FACT] Correlation between assets isn't fixed forever — it can shift over time and, notably, tends to increase during periods of severe market stress, when assets that normally move somewhat independently can start falling together, reducing the protective benefit of diversification exactly when it's most wanted.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An investor builds a portfolio of 40 stocks, all within the same industry and geographic region. Despite the large number of holdings, they tend to respond similarly to interest rate changes, regulatory news, and broader economic shifts affecting that industry — meaning the portfolio behaves more like a concentrated bet than a diversified one. A second investor holds just 15 stocks, but spread across different industries, geographies, and asset types with historically lower correlation to each other. [ASSUMPTION] Despite having fewer total holdings, the second investor's portfolio may experience smoother, more genuinely diversified performance during a sector-specific downturn than the first investor's larger but highly correlated portfolio.

## Practice Quiz

1. What does correlation measure between two investments?
2. Why can a portfolio with many holdings still lack real diversification?
3. Why might diversification benefits weaken exactly during periods of severe market stress?

## Answer Key

1. The degree to which two investments' returns tend to move together.
2. If those holdings are highly correlated — likely to respond similarly to the same events — the portfolio behaves more like a concentrated bet than a truly diversified one, regardless of the number of holdings.
3. Because correlation between assets tends to increase during severe market stress, meaning previously less-correlated assets can start moving together, reducing the protective benefit right when it's most needed.

## Key Takeaways
- Real diversification depends on correlation between holdings, not just the total number of holdings.
- Many holdings that are highly correlated provide much less risk reduction than the count alone suggests.
- Correlation can shift over time and often rises during periods of severe market stress, weakening diversification's protective effect exactly when it matters most.
