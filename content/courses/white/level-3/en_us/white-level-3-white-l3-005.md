## Core Content

An **option** is a contract giving the buyer the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell an underlying asset (like a stock) at a specific price (the strike price) before or on a specific date (the expiration date). A **call option** gives the right to buy at the strike price; a **put option** gives the right to sell at the strike price.

Here's the "why" this differs from simply owning the stock: because you're only obligated to act if it benefits you, the option buyer's downside is limited to what they paid for the contract — called the **premium** — while the potential upside can be substantial if the underlying asset moves favorably. This asymmetry (limited downside, larger potential upside) is exactly what the buyer is paying for.

[VERIFIED FACT] The seller (or "writer") of an option is on the other side of this trade, taking on the obligation to fulfill the contract if the buyer chooses to exercise their right — this is why option selling carries a fundamentally different risk profile, covered in a later lesson. The premium isn't arbitrary; it reflects the current price of the underlying asset, the strike price, time remaining until expiration, and the asset's expected volatility.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An investor buys a call option on a stock currently trading at $50, with a strike price of $55, paying a premium of $2 per share. If the stock rises to $65 before expiration, she can exercise her right to buy at $55 — well below the market price — for a meaningful profit, minus the $2 premium paid. If the stock stays below $55 or falls, she simply lets the option expire, and her total loss is limited to the $2 premium she paid — she was never obligated to buy at $55 if it didn't make sense to.

## Practice Quiz

1. What is the key difference between owning an option and being obligated to act on it?
2. What does a call option give the buyer the right to do?
3. Why does the option buyer's downside stay limited to the premium paid, even if the underlying asset moves sharply against them?

## Answer Key

1. Owning (buying) an option gives a right, not an obligation — the buyer can choose whether to exercise it, unlike owning the stock outright, which doesn't involve a choice about whether to "activate" the position.
2. The right to buy the underlying asset at the strike price before or on the expiration date.
3. Because the buyer isn't obligated to exercise the option — if it's unfavorable, they simply let it expire, losing only the premium they already paid, not more.

## Answers and Explanations

1. This is the foundational distinction underlying every other lesson on options in this track.
2. This is the specific definition of a call option, contrasted with a put option.
3. This explains the mechanical reason for the limited-downside asymmetry described in the Core Content.

## Key Takeaways
- An option gives the buyer a right, not an obligation, to buy (call) or sell (put) an asset at a set price before expiration.
- Because exercising is optional, the buyer's downside is limited to the premium paid, while potential upside can be larger.
- The option seller takes on the obligation side of the contract, which carries a different risk profile covered in a later lesson.
