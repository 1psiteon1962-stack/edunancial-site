## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

## Core Content

Lesson 5 introduced calls and puts as directional bets: a call profits if the underlying asset rises, a put profits if it falls. But what if you're confident something big is about to happen — earnings, a major announcement — but genuinely uncertain which direction the price will move?

A **straddle** combines buying both a call and a put on the same underlying asset, at the same strike price and expiration. Here's the "why" this shifts the bet: instead of betting on direction, a straddle bets on **magnitude of movement** — it profits if the underlying asset moves significantly in either direction, enough to cover the combined cost of both premiums, and loses value if the price stays relatively flat, since both options would expire with little value.

[VERIFIED FACT] Because a straddle involves buying two options instead of one, it costs more upfront than a single call or put — meaning the underlying asset needs to move further, in either direction, just to break even on the combined position.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An investor expects a company's upcoming earnings announcement to cause a big price swing but has no strong view on whether it will be positive or negative news. Instead of buying just a call (betting up) or just a put (betting down), she buys a straddle — a call and a put at the same strike price. [ASSUMPTION] If the stock jumps sharply after earnings, the call gains enough value to more than offset the put's loss and the total premium paid, resulting in a profit. If the stock instead drops sharply, the put performs the same function in reverse. But if the stock barely moves either way, both options may expire worth little, and she loses most or all of the combined premium paid.

## Practice Quiz

1. What does a straddle combine, structurally?
2. How does betting on "movement" differ from betting on "direction"?
3. Why does a straddle need a bigger price move to become profitable than a single call or put would?

## Answer Key

1. Buying both a call and a put on the same underlying asset, at the same strike price and expiration.
2. Betting on direction means profiting only if the price moves a specific way (up for a call, down for a put); betting on movement means profiting from a large move in either direction, regardless of which way it goes.
3. Because the combined cost of buying two options (the call and the put) is higher than buying just one, so the price needs to move further to cover that larger total premium before the position turns profitable.

## Answers and Explanations

1. This is the structural definition needed to understand the rest of the lesson.
2. This is the central conceptual distinction the lesson is built around.
3. This reinforces the cost trade-off of combining two positions instead of one.

## Key Takeaways
- A straddle combines a call and a put at the same strike and expiration, betting on movement rather than direction.
- It profits from a large price move in either direction and loses value if the price stays relatively flat.
- Because it costs more than a single option, it requires a bigger move to become profitable.
