## Core Content

**Margin** lets an investor borrow money from a brokerage to buy more securities than their own cash alone would allow, using the securities in the account as collateral for the loan. This concept mirrors the real estate leverage discussed elsewhere in this curriculum's RED track: borrowed money lets you control a larger position with a smaller amount of your own capital.

Here's the "why" the same multiplying effect applies: your percentage return is calculated relative to your own invested cash, not the full position size — so if the position gains value, that gain is measured against a smaller base, amplifying your percentage return. If the position loses value, the same math amplifies your percentage loss in exactly the same way, and you still owe the full borrowed amount regardless of how the investment performs.

[VERIFIED FACT] Margin loans typically charge interest, adding an ongoing cost to the position, and brokerages require maintaining a minimum level of equity in the account — if the account's value falls too much, this can trigger a margin call, covered in the next lesson.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An investor has $10,000 in cash and uses margin to buy $20,000 worth of stock (borrowing the other $10,000). If the stock rises 10%, the position gains $2,000 — a 20% return on the investor's original $10,000, not 10%. If the stock instead falls 10%, the position loses $2,000 — a 20% loss on the original $10,000, and the investor still owes the full $10,000 borrowed regardless of the stock's new, lower value.

## Practice Quiz

1. What does buying on margin allow an investor to do?
2. Why does margin amplify percentage gains and losses compared to an all-cash purchase?
3. What ongoing cost does margin typically add, beyond the amplified risk?

## Answer Key

1. Borrow money from a brokerage to buy more securities than their own cash alone would allow, using the account's holdings as collateral.
2. Because returns are measured against the investor's own smaller invested amount rather than the full position size, the same dollar gain or loss becomes a larger percentage change.
3. Interest charged on the margin loan, which adds an ongoing cost regardless of how the investment performs.

## Answers and Explanations

1. This is the definitional foundation for the lesson.
2. This is the same multiplier mechanism as real estate leverage, applied here to margin investing.
3. This adds a cost dimension specific to margin that doesn't have as direct a parallel in the real estate leverage discussion.

## Key Takeaways
- Margin allows borrowing to buy more securities than cash alone would allow, using the account as collateral.
- This amplifies both gains and losses proportionally, mirroring the leverage multiplier effect seen in real estate.
- Margin loans charge ongoing interest and require maintaining minimum account equity, risking a margin call if the account value falls too far.
