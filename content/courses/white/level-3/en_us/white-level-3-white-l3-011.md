## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

## Core Content

**Short selling** involves borrowing shares of a stock (typically through a brokerage), selling them immediately at the current price, and later buying them back (hopefully at a lower price) to return to the lender — profiting from the difference if the price falls.

Here's the "why" short selling has a fundamentally different risk shape than simply owning a stock: a stock's price can only fall to zero, so the maximum possible gain for a short seller is capped (the full value of the stock, if it goes to zero). But a stock's price has no theoretical upper limit — it can keep rising indefinitely, which means the short seller's potential loss, needing to eventually buy back the borrowed shares at whatever the price has risen to, is theoretically unlimited.

[VERIFIED FACT] This risk can accelerate through a **short squeeze** — if a heavily shorted stock's price starts rising, short sellers facing mounting losses may be forced (sometimes through mechanisms similar to a margin call, Lesson 10) to buy back shares to limit their losses, and that buying pressure itself can push the price up further, triggering more short sellers to do the same in a self-reinforcing cycle.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An investor borrows and sells short 100 shares of a stock trading at $50, expecting the price to fall. If the price does fall to $30, he can buy back the shares for $3,000 and return them, pocketing the $2,000 difference (before costs) — a solid, but capped, gain, since the stock could only fall to zero at most. If instead the stock unexpectedly rises to $150, he'd need to spend $15,000 to buy back the shares he sold for $5,000 — a $10,000 loss, and if the price kept climbing further, that loss would keep growing with no upper limit.

## Practice Quiz

1. How does short selling work, mechanically?
2. Why is a short seller's maximum possible gain capped, while potential loss is theoretically unlimited?
3. What is a short squeeze, and how can it accelerate a short seller's losses?

## Answer Key

1. Borrowing shares, selling them at the current price, and later buying them back to return to the lender, profiting if the buy-back price is lower than the sale price.
2. A stock's price can only fall to zero (capping the gain), but has no theoretical upper limit on how high it can rise (leaving loss potential unlimited).
3. A self-reinforcing cycle where rising prices force short sellers to buy back shares to limit losses, and that buying pressure pushes prices up further, triggering more forced buying.

## Answers and Explanations

1. This is the mechanical foundation for the entire lesson.
2. This is the central asymmetric risk shape that distinguishes short selling from ordinary stock ownership.
3. This explains a real, historically observed acceleration mechanism tied to the unlimited-loss risk described above.

## Key Takeaways
- Short selling profits from a falling stock price by borrowing, selling, and later buying back shares at (hopefully) a lower price.
- Maximum gain is capped since a stock can only fall to zero, but potential loss is theoretically unlimited since price has no upper bound.
- A short squeeze can accelerate losses through a self-reinforcing cycle of forced buying as prices rise.
