## Core Content

**Dollar-cost averaging (DCA)** means investing a fixed amount of money at regular intervals — monthly, for example — regardless of the price at each interval, rather than investing a larger sum all at once.

Here's the "why" this specifically addresses timing risk, connecting back to Lesson 1: since you're buying at many different price points over time, you avoid the risk of investing your entire amount right before a significant, poorly timed downturn. Some purchases happen at higher prices, some at lower prices, and the average cost per share smooths out over the investing period.

[VERIFIED FACT] DCA does not reduce the underlying investment's actual risk or volatility — the stock, fund, or asset you're buying still carries the same fundamental risk characteristics regardless of how you time your purchases into it. What DCA changes is your exposure to a single bad entry point, not the investment's inherent behavior once you own it.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An investor has $12,000 to invest. Investing it all in a single lump sum right before a sharp market decline would mean the entire amount is immediately exposed to that drop. Investing $1,000 per month over 12 months instead means only the earliest contributions are exposed to the full decline if it happens early in that period — later contributions buy in at the now-lower prices, averaging the investor's overall cost basis. [ASSUMPTION] If the market instead rises steadily throughout the 12 months, DCA would have resulted in a higher average cost than investing the full lump sum immediately — DCA protects against a specific bad-timing scenario, but doesn't guarantee a better outcome than lump-sum investing in every scenario (a comparison covered directly in the next lesson).

## Key Takeaways
- DCA involves investing a fixed amount at regular intervals rather than all at once.
- It specifically reduces timing risk — the risk of a single, poorly timed large investment.
- DCA does not change the underlying risk or volatility of the investment itself, only your exposure to a bad entry point.
