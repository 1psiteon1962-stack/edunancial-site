## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
- Explain why lump-sum investing tends to outperform DCA in a typically rising market
- Explain why many investors still choose DCA despite this statistical tendency
- Explain why this is a legitimate trade-off, not simply a math error by DCA investors

## Core Content

Lesson 12 showed that DCA protects against a specific bad-timing scenario. Here's the "why" this lesson pushes further: because markets have historically risen over most extended time periods more often than they've fallen, investing a lump sum immediately, rather than spreading it out, has more often than not resulted in a better outcome — since more of your money is invested and exposed to potential gains for a longer period, rather than sitting partly uninvested while it's gradually deployed.

[VERIFIED FACT] This is a statistical tendency based on historical patterns, not a guarantee for any specific future period — there have been periods where DCA would have outperformed a lump sum, particularly ones beginning shortly before a significant decline.

So why do many investors still choose DCA, even understanding this tendency? The reason connects back to Lesson 1's behavioral theme: DCA reduces the emotional risk of investing a large sum right before a downturn and then, driven by regret or fear, abandoning the strategy entirely. An investor who chooses DCA and sticks with their plan, even if it produces a statistically lower expected outcome, may end up better off than one who chooses lump-sum investing but panics and sells during a subsequent downturn.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An investor receives a large inheritance and has to decide between investing it all immediately or spreading it out over a year through DCA. [ASSUMPTION] Historically, in a randomly chosen year, investing the lump sum immediately would have outperformed DCA more often than not, since markets have risen in most historical periods. But this investor knows from past experience that a sudden large investment followed by a market dip would likely cause her significant anxiety and possibly lead her to sell at a loss. Choosing DCA, even with a statistically lower expected return, may be the better decision for her specifically, if it means she's more likely to stick with her investment plan through market fluctuations.

## Practice Quiz

1. Why does lump-sum investing tend to statistically outperform DCA over most historical periods?
2. Is this tendency a guarantee for any specific future period? Why or why not?
3. Why might an investor rationally choose DCA anyway, despite this statistical tendency?

## Answer Key

1. Because markets have historically risen more often than they've fallen over extended periods, so having more money invested for longer typically captures more of that upward trend than gradually phasing money in.
2. No — it's based on historical patterns, and there have been specific periods, particularly ones beginning before a significant decline, where DCA would have performed better.
3. Because DCA can reduce the emotional risk of poor timing and subsequent panic-selling, potentially leading to a better real-world outcome than a statistically superior strategy the investor might abandon during a downturn.

## Answers and Explanations

1. This is the central statistical reasoning behind the lesson's title.
2. This is an important, appropriately hedged caveat against overconfidence in historical patterns.
3. This connects the decision back to investor psychology, reinforcing that the "best" choice on paper isn't always the best choice for a specific individual's behavior.

## Key Takeaways
- Lump-sum investing has historically outperformed DCA more often than not, since markets have tended to rise over most extended periods.
- This is a statistical tendency, not a guarantee for any specific period.
- Many investors rationally choose DCA anyway, because it can reduce the emotional risk of poor timing and improve the odds of sticking with an investment plan long-term.
