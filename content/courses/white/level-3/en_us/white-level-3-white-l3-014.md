## Core Content

Lesson 1 opened this track with the difficulty of actually executing "buy low, sell high." **Rebalancing** offers a partial, mechanical solution: it means periodically adjusting a portfolio back to its original target allocation (for example, 60% stocks and 40% bonds) after market movements have shifted those proportions away from the target.

Here's the "why" this builds in the desired behavior automatically: if stocks have performed well and now make up 70% of the portfolio instead of the target 60%, rebalancing means selling some of the stocks (which have gone up in relative value) and buying more bonds (which have lagged) to restore the original target — mechanically selling some of what's gone up and buying some of what hasn't, without needing to predict the market's next move.

[VERIFIED FACT] Rebalancing can be done on a set schedule (e.g., annually) or triggered when allocations drift beyond a certain threshold — either approach removes emotional decision-making from the process, replacing it with a predetermined rule.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An investor starts with a 60% stocks / 40% bonds portfolio. After a strong year for stocks, the portfolio has drifted to 72% stocks / 28% bonds. Rebalancing means selling enough stock and buying enough bonds to return to the 60/40 target — mechanically taking some profit from the winning asset class and adding to the lagging one, following a predetermined rule rather than a prediction about what will happen next. [ASSUMPTION] If stocks continue rising afterward, this rebalancing "cost" the investor some additional upside; if stocks instead pull back, the rebalancing looks prescient in hindsight — but either way, the process was followed consistently rather than reactively.

## Practice Quiz

1. What does portfolio rebalancing mean?
2. Why does rebalancing mechanically result in selling relatively higher-performing assets and buying relatively lower-performing ones?
3. Why does this remove emotional decision-making from the process?

## Answer Key

1. Periodically adjusting a portfolio back to its original target asset allocation after market movements have shifted the actual proportions away from that target.
2. Because an asset class that has performed well grows to represent a larger share of the portfolio than intended, so returning to the target allocation requires selling some of it; the reverse applies to an underperforming asset class needing to be added to.
3. Because the decision to sell or buy is based on a predetermined rule (the target allocation and a rebalancing schedule or threshold) rather than a real-time judgment call about where the market is headed next.

## Key Takeaways
- Rebalancing periodically restores a portfolio to its original target asset allocation.
- This mechanically results in selling assets that have performed relatively well and buying ones that have lagged, without needing to predict future performance.
- Following a predetermined schedule or threshold removes emotional decision-making from the process.
