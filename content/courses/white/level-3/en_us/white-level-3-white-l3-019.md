## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
- Explain why bond prices fall when interest rates rise

## Core Content

Bonds pay a fixed interest rate (the coupon) set when they're issued. Interest rates in the broader market, however, change over time. Here's the "why" this creates a predictable, inverse relationship between existing bond prices and current interest rates: an existing bond's fixed payments become more or less attractive depending on what new bonds are currently offering.

If interest rates rise after a bond is issued, new bonds come out offering higher fixed payments than the older bond's fixed rate — making the older, lower-paying bond less attractive by comparison. For the older bond to remain competitive to a potential buyer, its price has to fall, effectively raising its yield (the return relative to its price) to match current market conditions. The reverse happens if interest rates fall: the older bond's relatively higher fixed payments look more attractive than what's newly available, so its price rises.

[VERIFIED FACT] This relationship applies to a bond's price if sold before maturity — an investor holding a bond to its full maturity still receives the original fixed payments and the return of principal as promised (assuming no default), regardless of what happens to interest rates or the bond's price in the meantime.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An investor holds a bond paying a 3% fixed rate. Interest rates in the broader market subsequently rise, and new, similar bonds now offer 5%. If she wanted to sell her 3% bond before maturity, she'd need to offer it at a lower price than she paid, since a buyer could otherwise simply buy a new 5% bond instead — the price adjustment is what makes her older, lower-paying bond competitive again. If she instead holds the bond to maturity, she continues receiving the original 3% payments and her principal back as promised, unaffected by what happened to prevailing rates in the meantime.

## Practice Quiz

1. Why does a bond's price fall when interest rates rise?
2. Why does a bond's price rise when interest rates fall?
3. Does this price relationship affect an investor who holds a bond to maturity? Why or why not?

## Answer Key

1. Because new bonds start offering higher fixed payments, making the older, lower-paying bond less attractive unless its price falls to make its effective yield competitive.
2. Because the older bond's relatively higher fixed payments become more attractive than newly available lower rates, increasing demand for it and pushing its price up.
3. No, in terms of the promised payments — an investor holding to maturity still receives the original fixed payments and principal as promised (assuming no default), regardless of price fluctuations along the way.

## Answers and Explanations

1. This is the central mechanism explaining the inverse relationship.
2. This confirms understanding of the relationship in the opposite direction.
3. This is an important clarification distinguishing market price risk from the bond's contractual payment terms.

## Key Takeaways
- Bond prices and interest rates move in opposite directions: rates up, existing bond prices down, and vice versa.
- This happens because existing bonds' fixed payments become more or less attractive compared to newly available rates.
- This price relationship affects selling a bond before maturity; holding to maturity still delivers the original promised payments, regardless of rate changes along the way.
