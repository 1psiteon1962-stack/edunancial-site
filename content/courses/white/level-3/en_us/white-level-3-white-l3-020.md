## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
- Distinguish between credit risk and interest rate risk in bonds
- Explain why these two risks can affect a bond's price independently of each other
- Explain why a bond rating primarily reflects credit risk, not interest rate risk

## Core Content

Lesson 19 explained how bond prices move inversely with broader interest rates — that's **interest rate risk**, and it applies to essentially all bonds, even the safest ones. There's a second, separate risk that also affects bond prices: **credit risk** — the risk that the bond's issuer might be unable to make its promised payments at all, due to financial trouble or default.

Here's the "why" it matters to keep these separate: a bond's price can fall due to broader interest rates rising, with no change at all in the issuer's financial health — this is interest rate risk, affecting even bonds from the most financially sound issuers. A bond's price can also fall because the market has become worried about that specific issuer's ability to pay, even if broader interest rates haven't moved — this is credit risk, and it's specific to that issuer.

[VERIFIED FACT] Bond credit ratings, assigned by independent rating agencies, are designed to reflect an issuer's credit risk — the likelihood of default — not their bond's sensitivity to interest rate changes; a very high credit rating (an indication of low credit risk) can still be attached to a bond with meaningful interest rate risk if it has a long duration (Lesson 18).

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] Two bonds both experience a price decline over the same period. Bond A's issuer remains financially strong and unchanged, but the decline happened because broader interest rates rose — a pure interest rate risk event. Bond B's issuer, meanwhile, released troubling financial news raising real doubts about its ability to make future payments — a credit risk event, causing its price to fall independent of what interest rates did. [ASSUMPTION] An investor confusing these two causes might mistakenly assume Bond B's price decline is temporary and rate-driven, like Bond A's, when it actually reflects a more serious, issuer-specific problem.

## Practice Quiz

1. What is the difference between credit risk and interest rate risk in bonds?
2. Why can a bond's price fall due to interest rate risk even if the issuer's financial health hasn't changed?
3. What does a bond's credit rating primarily reflect, according to this lesson?

## Answer Key

1. Interest rate risk is the risk of price changes due to broader interest rate movements, affecting essentially all bonds; credit risk is the risk that a specific issuer might be unable to make its promised payments, independent of broader rates.
2. Because interest rate risk reflects how a bond's fixed payments compare to newly available market rates, a factor entirely separate from the issuer's own financial condition.
3. The issuer's credit risk — the likelihood of default — not the bond's sensitivity to interest rate changes.

## Key Takeaways
- Interest rate risk affects nearly all bonds and is driven by broader rate movements, regardless of the issuer's financial health.
- Credit risk is specific to an issuer and reflects the chance they might fail to make promised payments.
- Bond credit ratings reflect credit risk specifically, not a bond's sensitivity to interest rate changes.
