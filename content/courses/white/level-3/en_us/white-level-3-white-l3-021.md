## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
- Explain how ETFs and mutual funds differ in how they're traded

- Explain why cost structures can differ between the two

## Core Content

Both **ETFs (exchange-traded funds)** and **mutual funds** pool investor money into a diversified basket of securities, and both can track similar strategies or indexes — but they differ in some practical ways worth understanding.

Here's the "why" trading differs: ETFs trade throughout the day on an exchange, like individual stocks, at prices that fluctuate continuously based on market activity. Mutual funds are typically priced and traded only once per day, after the market closes, at that day's calculated net asset value — you don't know the exact execution price when you place a mutual fund order during the trading day.

[VERIFIED FACT] ETFs are also often more tax-efficient than comparable mutual funds, largely due to a structural mechanism in how ETF shares are created and redeemed, which tends to generate fewer taxable capital gains distributions to shareholders than the process many mutual funds use — though this varies by specific fund and isn't automatic for every ETF.

Cost structures can also differ: many ETFs, especially those tracking broad indexes, tend to have lower expense ratios than actively managed mutual funds, though low-cost index mutual funds exist as well — cost should be compared fund by fund rather than assumed based solely on structure type.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An investor wants to buy shares of an S&P 500 index fund. Choosing the ETF version, she can place an order during market hours and see the executed price immediately, similar to buying a stock. Choosing the mutual fund version instead, her order executes at the fund's net asset value calculated after market close that day — she won't know the exact price until after the trading day ends. [ASSUMPTION] Over multiple years, if the ETF version happens to generate fewer taxable capital gains distributions due to its structural mechanism, the ETF investor may end up with a somewhat better after-tax return, even if the funds' underlying holdings and pre-tax performance are very similar.

## Practice Quiz

1. ETFs trade throughout the day at continuously fluctuating prices, like stocks; mutual funds are typically priced and traded once per day, after market close, at that day's net asset value.
2. Largely due to a structural mechanism in how ETF shares are created and redeemed, which tends to generate fewer taxable capital gains distributions than many mutual funds' processes.
3. No — while ETFs and index funds broadly tend to have lower costs than actively managed funds, low-cost index mutual funds also exist, so cost should be compared fund by fund rather than assumed by structure type alone.

2. This is the central tax-efficiency mechanism, appropriately caveated as not automatic for every ETF.

## Key Takeaways
- ETFs trade throughout the day like stocks; mutual funds trade once per day at a calculated net asset value.
- ETFs are often, though not always, more tax-efficient due to their share creation/redemption structure.
- Cost should be compared fund by fund, since low-cost options exist in both ETF and mutual fund structures.
