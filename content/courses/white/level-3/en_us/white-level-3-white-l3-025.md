## Core Content

It's intuitive to assume a fund that has performed well recently will likely continue performing well — after all, it must be doing something right. Here's the "why" this intuition often doesn't hold up: [VERIFIED FACT] extensive research comparing funds' historical performance rankings across different time periods has generally found weak persistence — meaning top-performing funds in one period are not reliably the top performers in the following period, and this pattern has been noted by regulators, which is part of why standardized disclosures include language noting that past performance doesn't guarantee future results.

Several factors contribute to this weak persistence: strong recent performance can reflect a temporary favorable environment for that fund's particular style or sector rather than a repeatable skill advantage; strategies or sectors that perform well often see conditions shift or become more crowded, reducing future advantage; and manager changes, asset growth, and other factors can all affect a fund's future results independent of its past record.

This is why chasing recent top performers — moving money into whatever fund has recently done the best — is often described as **performance chasing**, and can lead investors to consistently buy after strong runs (potentially near a peak) and sell after disappointing periods (potentially near a trough), which connects to a fund-level version of the "buy high, sell low" mistake discussed at the start of this track.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An investor notices a fund has posted the best returns in its category over the past three years and decides to invest heavily based on that record. [ASSUMPTION] If that fund's strong performance was driven largely by a temporarily favorable environment for its specific investment style, and that environment shifts in the following years, the fund may underperform going forward — leaving the investor with a disappointing experience despite having chosen based on an impressive-looking track record.

## Practice Quiz

1. What has research generally found about the persistence of top fund performance across different time periods?
2. What is "performance chasing," and why can it be problematic?
3. Why might strong recent performance reflect a temporary environment rather than repeatable manager skill?

## Answer Key

1. Generally weak persistence — top performers in one period are not reliably the top performers in the following period.
2. Moving money into recently top-performing funds; it can lead to buying after a strong run has already occurred and selling after a weak period, potentially near a peak and trough respectively.
3. Because a fund's specific investment style or sector focus may have simply been favored by conditions during that period, rather than the manager demonstrating a repeatable skill advantage likely to continue.

## Key Takeaways
- Past fund performance has generally shown weak predictive value for future performance, according to available research.
- Performance chasing — buying recently top-performing funds — can lead to a fund-level version of buying high and selling low.
- Strong recent performance may reflect temporary favorable conditions rather than a repeatable manager skill advantage.
