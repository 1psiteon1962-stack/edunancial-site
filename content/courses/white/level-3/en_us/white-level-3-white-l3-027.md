## Core Content

**Loss aversion** describes a well-documented behavioral pattern: for most people, the emotional pain of losing a given amount of money tends to feel stronger than the emotional pleasure of gaining that same amount. This asymmetry, while a normal part of human psychology, can distort investment decisions in a specific, predictable way.

Here's the "why" this leads to a common mistake called the **disposition effect**: investors influenced by loss aversion tend to sell winning investments too early, locking in a modest gain to "feel good" and avoid the risk of that gain disappearing — while holding onto losing investments far longer than the fundamentals would justify, hoping to avoid "realizing" the loss by simply not selling, even when the investment's outlook has genuinely deteriorated.

[VERIFIED FACT] This pattern has been documented across large sets of real investor trading data, showing a general tendency to sell winners at a higher rate than losers, relative to how each position is actually performing — a pattern that, if consistently followed, can systematically hurt long-term returns by cutting off winning positions early and letting losing positions run.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An investor holds two positions: one has gained 15%, the other has lost 15%. Influenced by loss aversion, she sells the winning position to "lock in" the gain and feel a sense of accomplishment, while holding the losing position, telling herself she'll sell once it "gets back to even" so she doesn't have to accept the loss. [ASSUMPTION] If the winning position continues to perform well after she sells it, and the losing position continues to decline while she holds it waiting for a rebound that may not come, this pattern — driven by the emotional asymmetry of loss aversion rather than each investment's actual outlook — has cost her more than a decision based purely on each position's fundamentals would have.

## Practice Quiz

1. What does loss aversion describe?
2. What is the "disposition effect," and how does it result from loss aversion?
3. Why can consistently following this pattern hurt long-term returns?

## Answer Key

1. The tendency for the emotional pain of a loss to feel stronger than the emotional pleasure of an equivalent gain.
2. The tendency to sell winning investments too early (to lock in a good feeling) and hold losing investments too long (to avoid the discomfort of realizing a loss) — a direct behavioral consequence of loss aversion.
3. Because it systematically cuts off winning positions before they've run their course while allowing losing positions to continue declining, rather than basing sell decisions on each investment's actual outlook.

## Key Takeaways
- Loss aversion describes the tendency for losses to feel more painful than equivalent gains feel pleasurable.
- This can lead to the disposition effect: selling winners too early and holding losers too long.
- This pattern, well-documented in real trading data, can systematically hurt long-term returns if followed consistently.
