## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
- Explain the difference between nominal return and real (inflation-adjusted) return
- Explain why a "safe," low-return portfolio can still lose purchasing power over time
- Connect this concept to the fiat currency discussion covered elsewhere in this curriculum

## Core Content

An investment's **nominal return** is its stated percentage gain, without adjusting for inflation. Its **real return** subtracts inflation from that nominal figure, showing the actual change in purchasing power. This distinction connects directly to the fiat currency discussion elsewhere in this curriculum: if fiat currency's purchasing power tends to shrink over time, an investment that doesn't outpace that shrinkage is losing real value, even if its nominal balance is technically growing or holding steady.

Here's the "why" a "safe" portfolio can still be risky in real terms: very low-return, highly stable assets — cash held in a low-interest account, for example — might avoid market volatility entirely, but if their return doesn't keep pace with inflation, the purchasing power of that money is shrinking every year, even though the nominal dollar amount isn't falling at all. [VERIFIED FACT] This is sometimes called inflation risk, and it's easy to overlook because it doesn't show up as a visible loss on an account statement the way a market decline does — the number itself doesn't go down, but what it can buy does.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An investor keeps $50,000 in cash, earning a very low interest rate, specifically to avoid any market risk. [ASSUMPTION] If inflation runs meaningfully higher than that interest rate over several years, the $50,000 balance itself doesn't decrease — it might even grow slightly from the interest earned — but the actual goods and services that $50,000 can purchase have quietly declined, since prices have risen faster than the account's growth. The account statement never shows a "loss," making this erosion easy to miss compared to a visible market decline.

## Practice Quiz

1. What is the difference between nominal return and real return?
2. Why can a "safe," low-return portfolio still lose real value over time?
3. Why is inflation risk easy to overlook compared to market risk?

## Answer Key

1. Nominal return is the stated percentage gain without adjusting for inflation; real return subtracts inflation to show the actual change in purchasing power.
2. Because if the return doesn't keep pace with inflation, the money's purchasing power shrinks even though its nominal dollar value may be stable or growing.
3. Because the account balance itself doesn't visibly decrease the way a market decline would — the erosion happens in what the money can buy, not in the number displayed on a statement.

## Answers and Explanations

1. This is the foundational distinction the lesson is built around.
2. This is the central, easy-to-overlook risk the lesson highlights.
3. This explains why this particular risk is psychologically easy to miss compared to a market loss.

## Key Takeaways
- Real return, not nominal return, reflects an investment's actual change in purchasing power.
- A "safe," low-return portfolio can still lose real value over time if its return doesn't keep pace with inflation.
- Inflation risk is easy to overlook because it doesn't show up as a visible loss on an account statement.
