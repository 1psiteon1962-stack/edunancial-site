## Core Content

When an investor buys an international investment — a foreign stock or bond, for example — that investment is typically priced in its local currency. Converting the eventual returns back into the investor's home currency introduces a separate factor: **currency risk**, the risk that exchange rate movements between the two currencies affect the investor's actual return, independent of how the underlying investment itself performed.

Here's the "why" this creates two separate factors in the total return: the investment could perform well in its local currency, but if that local currency weakens significantly against the investor's home currency before conversion, some or all of that gain could be offset. Conversely, a modest local-currency return combined with a currency that strengthens against the investor's home currency could result in a better total return than the local performance alone would suggest.

[VERIFIED FACT] Currency movements can be difficult to predict and are influenced by many factors, including interest rate differences between countries, economic conditions, and broader market sentiment — some international funds offer currency-hedged versions specifically designed to reduce this particular risk, isolating the investor's return closer to the underlying investment's local performance alone, though hedging itself has its own costs and trade-offs.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An investor buys shares in a foreign company that rises 10% in its local currency over a year. If that local currency weakens by 8% against the investor's home currency over the same period, her actual return, once converted back, is much closer to roughly 2% — most of the local gain was offset by the unfavorable currency movement. [ASSUMPTION] If instead that local currency had strengthened by 8% against her home currency, her converted return could have been closer to 18% — currency movement working in her favor on top of the underlying 10% gain.

## Practice Quiz

1. What is currency risk?
2. Why does an international investment's total return depend on two separate factors?
3. What is a currency-hedged fund designed to do?

## Answer Key

1. The risk that exchange rate movements between the investor's home currency and the investment's local currency affect the investor's actual return, independent of the investment's own performance.
2. Because the return depends both on how the underlying investment performs in its local currency and on how that local currency's value changes relative to the investor's home currency before conversion.
3. Reduce currency risk by isolating the investor's return closer to the underlying investment's local-currency performance, though this comes with its own costs and trade-offs.

## Key Takeaways
- Currency risk is the risk that exchange rate movements affect an international investment's return, separate from the investment's own performance.
- Total return on an international investment depends on both the local-currency performance and the currency's movement relative to the investor's home currency.
- Currency-hedged funds are designed to reduce this specific risk, though hedging carries its own costs and trade-offs.
