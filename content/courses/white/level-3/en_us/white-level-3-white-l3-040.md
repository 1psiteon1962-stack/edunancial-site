## Core Content

**Cost basis** generally refers to the original value used to determine a taxable gain or loss when an investment is eventually sold — in its simplest form, the purchase price. Here's the "why" it's often more complex than that simple description: cost basis can be adjusted by several factors over the holding period, including reinvested dividends (Lesson 17) that were used to purchase additional shares, since each reinvestment typically establishes its own cost basis at that purchase's price.

[VERIFIED FACT] Accurately tracking cost basis matters directly because it determines the calculated taxable gain (sale price minus cost basis) when an investment is eventually sold — understating cost basis overstates the taxable gain, potentially resulting in paying more tax than necessary, while overstating cost basis understates the gain, which can create compliance issues.

Brokerages in many jurisdictions are required to track and report cost basis information for many types of accounts, which has made this less of a manual burden for investors than it once was — but understanding the underlying concept remains useful, particularly for situations involving multiple purchases over time, reinvested dividends, or accounts and situations where cost basis tracking may be less automated.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An investor buys shares of a fund over several years, adding to her position at different times and also having dividends automatically reinvested into additional shares along the way. Each of these purchases — the original buys and each dividend reinvestment — establishes its own cost basis at that specific transaction's price. [ASSUMPTION] When she eventually sells a portion of her total position, the calculated taxable gain depends on which specific shares (and their associated cost basis) are considered sold, based on the accounting method used — illustrating why simply using the very first purchase price as "the" cost basis for the whole position would likely be inaccurate.

## Practice Quiz

1. What does cost basis generally refer to?
2. Why can cost basis be more complex than simply the original purchase price?
3. What happens if cost basis is understated when calculating a taxable gain?

## Answer Key

1. The original value used to determine a taxable gain or loss when an investment is eventually sold.
2. Because additional purchases over time, including reinvested dividends, each establish their own cost basis, making the total position's basis a combination of multiple transactions rather than a single figure.
3. The calculated taxable gain is overstated, potentially resulting in paying more tax than actually necessary.

## Key Takeaways
- Cost basis is the original value used to calculate taxable gain or loss on a sale, and it's often more complex than a single purchase price.
- Reinvested dividends and multiple purchases over time each establish their own cost basis, requiring careful tracking.
- Accurate cost basis tracking directly affects the calculated taxable gain, with real consequences for over- or understating it.
