## Core Content

A **stop-loss order** is an instruction to sell a security automatically if its price falls to a specified level, designed to help limit losses on a position without requiring the investor to monitor the price constantly.

Here's the "why" it doesn't guarantee an exact exit price: once the stop price is reached, a standard stop-loss order typically converts into a **market order** — an order to sell immediately at the best currently available price, not specifically at the stop price itself. If the price is moving quickly, or if the security gaps down significantly (jumping past the stop price with no trading in between, which can happen around major news events), the order may execute at a price meaningfully worse than the stop price that triggered it.

[VERIFIED FACT] A **stop-limit order** is a variation that sets both a stop price (triggering the order) and a limit price (the worst price the investor is willing to accept) — this protects against an unexpectedly bad execution price, but introduces a different risk: if the price moves past the limit price too quickly, the order might not execute at all, leaving the position unsold during the exact decline the investor was trying to protect against.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An investor sets a stop-loss order at $45 on a stock currently trading at $50, intending to limit her loss if the price declines. [ASSUMPTION] If negative news causes the stock to gap down overnight, opening the next day at $38 with no trading in between $45 and $38, her stop-loss order would trigger and execute near the new, lower opening price — not at her intended $45 stop price — resulting in a larger loss than she may have expected the stop-loss to protect against.

## Practice Quiz

1. What does a stop-loss order do?
2. Why doesn't a standard stop-loss order guarantee execution exactly at the specified stop price?
3. What trade-off does a stop-limit order introduce compared to a standard stop-loss order?

## Answer Key

1. It automatically triggers a sale if a security's price falls to a specified level, helping limit losses without requiring constant price monitoring.
2. Because once triggered, it typically converts into a market order, executing at the best currently available price — which can be meaningfully worse than the stop price during fast-moving conditions or a price gap.
3. It sets a limit on the worst acceptable execution price, protecting against a bad fill, but risks the order not executing at all if the price moves past that limit too quickly.

## Key Takeaways
- A stop-loss order automatically triggers a sale when a specified price is reached, but typically executes as a market order at the best available price, not necessarily the stop price itself.
- A significant price gap can result in an execution price meaningfully worse than the stop price.
- A stop-limit order sets a worst acceptable price, protecting against a bad fill, but risks not executing at all if the price moves too quickly.
