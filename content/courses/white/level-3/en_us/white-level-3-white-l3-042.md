By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

## Core Content

**Liquidity** describes how easily an asset can be bought or sold without significantly affecting its price, generally reflecting how much regular trading activity (volume) occurs in that security. A widely traded, well-known stock is typically highly liquid: large numbers of shares change hands constantly, and buy and sell orders can usually be filled quickly at prices very close to the last traded price.

Here's the "why" a thinly traded security behaves differently: with fewer buyers and sellers actively trading, the **bid-ask spread** (the gap between the highest price a buyer is currently willing to pay and the lowest price a seller is currently willing to accept) tends to be wider. [VERIFIED FACT] A wider spread means an investor buying and then immediately selling a thinly traded security would generally lose more to that spread than they would with a highly liquid one, even before considering any price movement — it's an implicit cost of trading in a less liquid market.

Liquidity risk matters even for a long-term, infrequent trader: if you eventually need to sell a thinly traded position, especially in a larger size or during a period of market stress, you may need to accept a less favorable price, or sell more gradually over time, to avoid pushing the price down further with your own selling activity.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A large, widely known company's stock trades millions of shares daily, with a bid-ask spread of just a few cents — an investor can generally buy or sell a typical position quickly with minimal spread cost. A small, thinly traded company's stock might trade only a few thousand shares on a typical day, with a much wider spread. [ASSUMPTION] An investor needing to sell a meaningful position in that thinly traded stock might find that her own selling activity pushes the price down further than a comparable sale in the highly liquid stock would, simply because there are fewer buyers readily available to absorb the shares at the current price.
