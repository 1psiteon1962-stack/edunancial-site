## Core Content

An **IPO (Initial Public Offering)** is the process by which a private company first sells shares to the public, becoming a publicly traded company. Here's the "why" investing around an IPO carries distinct risk compared to buying an established public company: a newly public company typically has a much shorter public track record — less historical data on how its stock trades, how management communicates with public shareholders, and how the business performs under the scrutiny and reporting requirements of being publicly traded.

[VERIFIED FACT] IPO share prices are typically set based on the company's own projections and investment bank valuations rather than an extended period of actual public market trading, and newly public stocks have historically often experienced significant price volatility in their early trading period, as the broader market forms its own independent view of the company's value that may differ meaningfully from the IPO price.

This connects back to Lesson 4's volatility-versus-risk distinction: this early volatility doesn't necessarily reflect a change in the underlying business — it can simply reflect the market's price discovery process working through much more limited public information than exists for an established company.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A company completes its IPO at a set offering price. [ASSUMPTION] In its first weeks and months of public trading, the stock might swing significantly above and below that offering price, as different investors form varying opinions about its value with limited historical trading data to anchor those views. An investor buying immediately at the IPO price is exposed to this early price-discovery volatility in a way that an investor buying shares of a company with years of established public trading history typically is not.
