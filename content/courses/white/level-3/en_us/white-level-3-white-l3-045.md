## Core Content

A **stock buyback (or share repurchase)** happens when a company uses its own cash to buy back shares of its own stock from the market, reducing the total number of shares outstanding. This is a different mechanism for returning value to shareholders than the dividend payments discussed in Lesson 17.

Here's the "why" a buyback's effect differs from a dividend: rather than distributing cash directly to shareholders (which, as Lesson 17 explained, corresponds to a roughly equivalent drop in share price), a buyback reduces the number of shares outstanding — meaning each remaining share represents a slightly larger ownership stake in the company. [VERIFIED FACT] If the company's overall value and earnings stay the same while the share count shrinks, key per-share metrics like earnings per share tend to increase, which can support a higher share price, all else being equal.

A meaningful tax-related difference between the two approaches: a dividend generally creates an immediate taxable event for the shareholder receiving it (subject to applicable tax rules), whereas a buyback doesn't directly create a taxable event for shareholders who don't sell — any benefit shows up as a potential increase in the remaining shares' value, which shareholders only realize (and potentially owe tax on) if and when they choose to sell.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A company has $1 million in profit and 1 million shares outstanding, giving it $1 in earnings per share. If it uses available cash to buy back 100,000 shares, reducing the share count to 900,000, that same $1 million in profit now translates to roughly $1.11 in earnings per share — a higher figure purely from the reduced share count, assuming profit stays the same. A shareholder who doesn't sell any shares doesn't receive an immediate cash payment (unlike a dividend) and doesn't face an immediate taxable event, but her existing shares may become worth somewhat more if the market values the improved per-share metrics accordingly.

## Practice Quiz

1. What is a stock buyback?
2. How does a buyback's mechanism for returning value differ from a dividend's?
3. What is a key tax-related difference between a buyback and a dividend for a shareholder who doesn't sell?

## Answer Key

1. A company using its own cash to repurchase shares of its own stock, reducing the total number of shares outstanding.
2. A dividend distributes cash directly to shareholders (with an offsetting price drop, per Lesson 17); a buyback reduces the share count, increasing each remaining share's proportional ownership stake without a direct cash distribution.
3. A dividend generally creates an immediate taxable event for the shareholder; a buyback doesn't directly create a taxable event for a shareholder who doesn't sell, since any benefit shows up only as a potential value increase in the shares they still hold.

## Answers and Explanations

1. This is the foundational definition for the lesson.
2. This is the central mechanical distinction between the two approaches, contrasted directly with Lesson 17.
3. This highlights a meaningful, practical difference in how and when shareholders are affected by taxes.

## Key Takeaways
- A stock buyback reduces shares outstanding, increasing each remaining share's proportional ownership stake.
- This differs mechanically from a dividend, which distributes cash directly with a corresponding price adjustment.
- Buybacks generally don't create an immediate taxable event for shareholders who don't sell, unlike dividends.
