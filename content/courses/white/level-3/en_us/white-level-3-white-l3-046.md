## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
- Define beta as a measure of relative volatility
- Interpret what a beta above, below, or equal to 1.0 generally suggests
- Explain a limitation of beta as a risk measure

## Core Content

**Beta** is a statistical measure describing how a stock's price has tended to move relative to the broader market — typically a benchmark index. A beta of 1.0 suggests a stock has historically moved roughly in line with the market. A beta above 1.0 suggests historically greater volatility than the market (moving further in the same direction as the market's moves); a beta below 1.0 suggests historically less volatility than the market.

Here's the "why" this can be a useful, if limited, tool: beta gives a quick, standardized way to compare how sensitive different stocks have been to overall market movements — useful when thinking about how a stock might contribute to a portfolio's overall volatility (connecting to the diversification and correlation concepts from Lessons 2 and 3).

[VERIFIED FACT] Beta is calculated using historical price data and reflects past relative volatility, not a guarantee of future behavior — and it specifically measures sensitivity to broad market movements, not a stock's total risk, meaning two stocks with the same beta could still have very different company-specific risk profiles (Lesson 2) that beta alone doesn't capture.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] Stock A has a beta of 1.5, suggesting it has historically moved about 50% more than the broader market in either direction — if the market rises 10%, Stock A might have historically risen around 15%, and the reverse in a decline. Stock B has a beta of 0.6, suggesting historically more muted moves relative to the market. [ASSUMPTION] An investor building a portfolio might use these historical beta figures as one input when thinking about how each stock could contribute to overall portfolio volatility — while remembering that beta reflects past patterns and doesn't capture each company's specific, individual risks separate from broad market movements.

## Practice Quiz

1. What does beta measure?
2. What does a beta above 1.0 generally suggest about a stock's historical volatility relative to the market?
3. What limitation does this lesson identify about using beta as a complete risk measure?

## Answer Key

1. How a stock's price has tended to move relative to the broader market, typically a benchmark index.
2. That the stock has historically moved with greater volatility than the market — moving further in the same direction as market moves.
3. Beta reflects past relative volatility to broad market movements specifically, not a guarantee of future behavior, and doesn't capture a stock's company-specific risk separate from the broader market.

## Answers and Explanations

1. This is the foundational definition for the lesson.
2. This confirms interpretation of the specific numeric threshold.
3. This is an important caution against treating beta as a complete measure of a stock's total risk.

## Key Takeaways
- Beta measures a stock's historical volatility relative to the broader market, with 1.0 representing market-level volatility.
- A beta above 1.0 suggests historically greater relative volatility; below 1.0 suggests historically less.
- Beta reflects past patterns and market-related volatility specifically — it doesn't capture company-specific risk or guarantee future behavior.
