## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
- Explain why diversifying across asset classes addresses a type of risk that diversifying within stocks alone cannot

- Explain why combining assets with historically different behavior patterns can smooth overall portfolio performance

## Core Content

Lessons 2 and 3 covered diversification and correlation within a category of similar investments — mainly stocks. This lesson extends the same underlying logic to a broader level: diversifying across entirely different **asset classes** — stocks, bonds, real estate (Lesson 32's REITs, for example), cash, and others.

Here's the "why" this addresses a type of risk stock-only diversification cannot: even a well-diversified stock portfolio, spread across many companies and sectors, remains exposed to the market-wide risk that affects stocks broadly (as established in Lesson 2) — a general stock market downturn affects nearly all stocks together, regardless of how well-diversified the stock selection is. [VERIFIED FACT] Different asset classes have historically shown varying, and sometimes low or negatively correlated, relationships with each other during different market conditions — bonds, for example, have at times performed differently than stocks during stock market downturns, though this relationship isn't fixed or guaranteed and can shift depending on the specific economic environment (connecting to Lesson 43's rate-cycle discussion in the RED track).

Combining asset classes with genuinely different behavior patterns, rather than diversifying only within one asset class, addresses the market-wide risk that stock-only diversification specifically cannot reduce.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An investor holds a well-diversified portfolio of 50 different stocks across many sectors. During a broad stock market downturn, nearly all of her holdings decline together, since the downturn reflects market-wide risk that her stock diversification, however thorough, cannot protect against. [ASSUMPTION] If she had also allocated a portion of her portfolio to bonds, and those bonds performed differently during that same downturn — as they have during some historical periods — her overall portfolio's decline might have been meaningfully smaller than a stock-only portfolio's, even one that was extremely well-diversified within the stock asset class alone.

## Practice Quiz

1. What type of risk does diversifying across asset classes address that diversifying only within stocks cannot?
2. Why can even a very well-diversified stock portfolio still experience significant declines during a market-wide downturn?
3. Is the relationship between different asset classes' performance during a downturn fixed and guaranteed? Why or why not?

## Answer Key

1. Market-wide risk — the risk that affects an entire asset class (like stocks) broadly, regardless of how diversified the individual holdings within that asset class are.
2. Because market-wide risk affects nearly all stocks together, and diversification within stocks primarily addresses company-specific risk (Lesson 2), not this broader market-level risk.
3. No — different asset classes' relative performance during various market conditions has varied historically and isn't fixed or guaranteed, so this benefit should be understood as a historical tendency, not an assured outcome.

## Answers and Explanations

1. This is the central distinction the lesson is built around, extending the Lesson 2/3 framework to a broader scale.
2. This directly reinforces the market-wide risk concept first introduced in Lesson 2.
3. This is an important, appropriately hedged caveat against overconfidence in historical asset class relationships.

## Key Takeaways
- Diversifying across asset classes addresses market-wide risk that diversifying only within one asset class (like stocks) cannot.
- Different asset classes have historically shown varying, sometimes low or negative, correlation with each other during different conditions.
- This relationship isn't fixed or guaranteed, and can shift depending on the broader economic environment.
