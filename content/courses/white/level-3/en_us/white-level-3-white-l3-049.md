## Core Content

This track has covered several specific ways emotion and psychology can distort investment decisions: loss aversion (Lesson 27), recency bias (Lesson 28), performance chasing (Lesson 25), and the broader behavior gap they collectively create (Lesson 26). A **written investment plan** — sometimes called an investment policy statement — is a practical tool designed to counter all of these at once.

Here's the "why" writing a plan down in advance matters: decisions made calmly, in advance, before a specific market event has triggered an emotional reaction, tend to be more consistent with an investor's actual long-term goals than decisions made reactively in the middle of a stressful downturn or an exciting rally. A written plan — specifying target asset allocation (Lesson 34), rebalancing rules (Lesson 14), and general criteria for buying or selling — gives an investor a pre-committed reference point to return to, rather than improvising a response while emotions are actively running high.

[VERIFIED FACT] This approach doesn't eliminate the emotional experience of market volatility, but it can reduce the likelihood of acting on that emotion in ways that contradict an investor's own previously stated, calmly considered goals and strategy.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An investor writes an investment plan during a calm period, specifying her target asset allocation, a plan to rebalance annually, and criteria she'll use before making any significant changes to her strategy. [ASSUMPTION] Months later, during a sharp market downturn, she feels a strong urge to sell everything and move to cash. Referring back to her written plan — created before this specific emotional moment — reminds her of her original long-term reasoning and rebalancing rules, potentially helping her avoid a reactive decision that her calmer, earlier self had specifically anticipated and planned to resist.
