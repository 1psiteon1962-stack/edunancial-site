# Decision-Making Under Uncertainty

## Learning Objectives
- Explain why leaders rarely get to decide with complete information
- Describe a basic framework for making decisions under uncertainty
- Identify the difference between a reversible and irreversible decision

## Core Content

New leaders often wait for certainty before acting — more data, more time, more confirmation. The problem is that certainty rarely arrives, and waiting too long is itself a decision, usually the wrong one. Leadership means making sound calls with incomplete information, not perfect information.

A useful framework: first, ask how reversible the decision is. A reversible decision (you can undo it or adjust course if it doesn't work) can be made faster with less analysis, because the cost of being wrong is low. An irreversible decision (hard or impossible to undo) deserves more time and more input, because the cost of being wrong is high.

Second, ask what you'd need to believe for each option to be the right call, and check which of those beliefs are most likely true based on what you actually know. This forces you to reason explicitly instead of just going with a gut feeling you can't explain.

Third, accept that some decisions will turn out wrong even when made well. Good decision-making is judged by the quality of the process and the information available at the time, not just the outcome.

## Worked Example

A small business owner is deciding whether to sign a one-year lease on a bigger space. This is a relatively irreversible decision (breaking a lease early is costly), so it deserves careful analysis: current revenue trends, realistic growth projections, and a cash flow cushion in case growth is slower than hoped.

Compare that to deciding whether to try a new marketing post format for a week. That's highly reversible — if it doesn't work, stop and try something else next week. That decision doesn't need the same depth of analysis as the lease.

## Practice Quiz

1. Why is waiting for full certainty often the wrong approach for a leader?
2. What's the first question the framework suggests asking about a decision?
3. Why do reversible decisions deserve less analysis time than irreversible ones?
4. How should good decision-making be judged, according to this lesson?

## Answer Key
1. Because certainty rarely arrives, and waiting too long is itself a costly decision.
2. How reversible is this decision?
3. Because the cost of being wrong is lower when you can undo or adjust the decision later.
4. By the quality of the process and information available at the time, not just the outcome.

## Answers and Explanations
1. This addresses the common trap of "analysis paralysis" in leadership.
2. Reversibility is the first filter the framework applies to calibrate how much analysis a decision deserves.
3. Lower stakes for being wrong justify faster, lighter-weight decision-making.
4. This distinction protects against unfairly judging a good decision-making process just because an outcome didn't work out.

## Key Takeaways
- Leaders must act on incomplete information — waiting for certainty is itself a costly choice.
- Assess reversibility first: reversible decisions can move fast, irreversible ones deserve more care.
- Judge decision-making by the quality of the process, not just whether the outcome happened to work out.
