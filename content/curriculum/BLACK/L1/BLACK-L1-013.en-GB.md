# Giving Feedback Without Demoralising

## Learning Objectives
- Explain why avoiding hard feedback ultimately hurts a team
- Identify the components of feedback that improves rather than demoralises
- Practice delivering a piece of critical feedback constructively

## Core Content

Many new leaders avoid giving hard feedback because it feels uncomfortable, worrying it will hurt morale or damage the relationship. Ironically, avoiding necessary feedback usually hurts a team more in the long run — people can't improve at something they don't know is a problem, and eventually the accumulated frustration comes out anyway, often at a worse time and in a worse way.

Effective feedback is specific, tied to behaviour rather than character, and paired with a path forward. "You're not detail-oriented" attacks character and gives no direction. "The last two reports had numbers that didn't match the source data — can we build in a double-check step before submission?" is specific, behaviour-focused, and offers a concrete next step.

Timing also matters. Feedback given close to the actual event, rather than weeks later in a formal review, is both more useful and less likely to feel like an ambush, because the specifics are still fresh for both people.

## Worked Example

A team member has been consistently late to client calls. Avoiding the issue: saying nothing and quietly resenting it, or reassigning client calls without explanation. Demoralising feedback: "You clearly don't care about this job." Constructive feedback: "I've noticed you've been a few minutes late to the last three client calls — is something going on that's making the timing hard, and how can we fix it going forward?" This addresses the specific behaviour, stays curious rather than accusatory, and invites a solution.

## Practice Quiz

1. Why does avoiding hard feedback ultimately hurt a team more?
2. What are the two key components of effective feedback, according to this lesson?
3. Why does timing matter when giving feedback?
4. Rewrite this demoralising feedback constructively: "You're really bad at managing your time."

## Answer Key
1. Because people can't improve at problems they don't know exist, and frustration builds up and surfaces later, often worse.
2. Being specific and tied to behaviour rather than character, paired with a path forward.
3. Feedback given close to the event is more useful and less likely to feel like an ambush than delayed feedback.
4. Answers will vary but should focus on specific behaviour and offer a path forward (example: "The last few deadlines slipped past the agreed date — can we talk through what's making the timing tough and set up a check-in system?")

## Answers and Explanations
1. This addresses the long-term cost of avoiding necessary but uncomfortable feedback.
2. Specificity and behaviour-focus (rather than character attacks) are the core mechanics of constructive feedback.
3. Fresh, timely feedback keeps the specifics clear for both people and prevents surprise reactions.
4. A correct rewrite avoids character judgement and offers a concrete, collaborative next step.

## Key Takeaways
- Avoiding hard feedback usually hurts a team more than delivering it constructively.
- Effective feedback is specific, behaviour-focused, and paired with a path forward, not a character judgement.
- Timely feedback, given close to the event, is more useful and less likely to feel like an ambush.
