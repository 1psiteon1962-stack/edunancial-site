# The "Everybody Said Yes But Meant No" Problem

## Learning Objectives
- Explain why verbal agreement doesn't always reflect true commitment
- Identify ways to verify real commitment before relying on it
- Apply this case study's lesson to planning that depends on other people

## Core Content

One of the more painful leadership lessons is discovering that people can say "yes" without really believing something will happen — and then be genuinely caught off guard when it actually does. This isn't necessarily dishonesty in a malicious sense; it's often a kind of social avoidance, where saying yes in the moment feels easier than voicing doubt, especially about a plan that seems unlikely to actually come together.

The leadership lesson here is about clarity and verification upfront, not just about getting an initial yes. A leader planning something that depends on other people's commitments needs to distinguish between polite agreement and real, verified intent — through follow-up confirmations, specific commitments (dates, actions, materials), and paying attention to hesitation or vagueness that might signal an unconvinced "yes."

This doesn't mean assuming bad faith in everyone who agrees to something. It means building verification into the planning process itself, so a collapse doesn't happen all at once, right when everything else is already locked in.

## Worked Example

Someone lined up guests for a public access political TV show, secured a studio and camera crew, and had everything ready to go — only to find that when they came back to confirm, guest after guest suddenly had an excuse not to appear. The real issue wasn't malice: the guests had agreed without truly believing the show would actually happen, and were caught off guard when it did.

The leadership lesson drawn from this: be very clear and explicit upfront, and verify commitments as the plan develops, rather than assuming an early yes will still hold once things become real. A stronger process might have included specific confirmation checkpoints — reconfirming each guest a set number of days before the taping, with a genuine, direct question about their actual intent to show up.

## Practice Quiz

1. Why can someone say "yes" without truly intending to follow through?
2. What does the leadership lesson focus on — getting a yes, or something else?
3. Name one way to verify real commitment before relying on it.
4. In the worked example, what happened when the plan reached the moment of actually needing people to show up?

## Answer Key
1. Saying yes in the moment can feel easier than voicing doubt about something they don't believe will actually happen.
2. Verifying real, ongoing commitment, not just securing an initial agreement.
3. Any reasonable answer: follow-up confirmations, specific commitments (dates/actions), noticing hesitation or vagueness.
4. The guests, who had agreed without truly believing it would happen, backed out when it became real.

## Answers and Explanations
1. This reframes the failure as a social dynamic rather than necessarily dishonest intent.
2. The lesson shifts leadership focus from securing agreement to verifying ongoing, genuine commitment.
3. These verification methods build a safeguard into the planning process itself.
4. This illustrates the exact failure mode the lesson is built around, using a real case study.

## Key Takeaways
- A "yes" isn't always genuine commitment — sometimes it reflects social avoidance rather than real intent.
- Leadership planning that depends on others requires ongoing verification, not just an initial agreement.
- Build confirmation checkpoints into plans that depend on other people's commitments, especially before high-stakes moments.
