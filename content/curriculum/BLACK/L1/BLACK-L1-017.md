# Pivoting When the Original Plan Collapses

## Learning Objectives
- Explain the difference between a plan failing and a goal failing
- Identify what resources can be repurposed when a plan collapses
- Apply the pivot principle to a new hypothetical scenario

## Core Content

When a fully prepared plan collapses completely, the instinct can be to treat the whole effort as a failure and walk away. A more useful leadership instinct is to ask: did the underlying goal fail, or did just this specific method fail? Very often, the resources, effort, and infrastructure already built can be redirected toward a different method that still serves the same underlying goal.

This requires honestly assessing what's actually still available after the collapse — what assets, relationships, or capabilities remain usable — and being willing to redesign around them rather than assuming everything has to start over from zero. A leader who can make this shift quickly, without excessive time spent mourning the original plan, preserves momentum that a slower, more resistant leader would lose.

## Worked Example

A person had built out a full studio setup and camera crew for a political interview show, only to have every lined-up guest back out at the last moment. The original plan (a political interview show) was dead. But the studio, the crew, and the underlying ambition to build something valuable were still very much alive.

Rather than abandoning the effort, the person pivoted to a bilingual music video program — a format that didn't depend on unreliable guest commitments the same way an interview show did. The pivot worked because the goal (build something valuable with this studio and crew) was separated from the specific method (a political interview show) that had failed.

## Practice Quiz

1. What question should a leader ask when a plan collapses?
2. What should a leader assess honestly after a plan fails?
3. Why does moving quickly to redesign matter?
4. In the worked example, what specifically survived the collapse of the original plan?

## Answer Key
1. Did the underlying goal fail, or did just this specific method fail?
2. What assets, relationships, or capabilities are still usable after the collapse.
3. Because a slow, resistant response loses momentum that a quick pivot preserves.
4. The studio, the camera crew, and the underlying ambition to build something valuable.

## Answers and Explanations
1. This question is the central reframing tool for handling a collapsed plan constructively.
2. Honest assessment of remaining resources is the practical first step toward a pivot.
3. Momentum is a real, finite resource that a slow recovery process wastes.
4. This shows the concrete assets that made the pivot to a new format possible.

## Key Takeaways
- When a plan collapses, ask whether the goal failed or just the specific method.
- Honestly assess what resources and capabilities remain usable, and redesign around them.
- Moving quickly to pivot preserves momentum that a slower response would lose.
