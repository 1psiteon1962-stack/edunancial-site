# The Sunk-Cost Trap

## Learning Objectives
- Define the sunk-cost fallacy in leadership decision-making
- Explain why past investment shouldn't determine future decisions
- Practice evaluating a decision based only on future costs and benefits

## Core Content

The sunk-cost trap is the tendency to keep investing time, money, or effort into something because of what's already been invested, rather than based on what makes sense going forward. "We've already put so much into this" feels like a reason to continue, but it isn't actually relevant to whether continuing is the right call — the money and time already spent are gone either way, regardless of what happens next.

A useful discipline: evaluate a decision as if you were encountering it fresh right now, based only on future costs and expected future benefits, ignoring what's already been spent. If a project no longer makes sense going forward, the fact that a lot has already been invested doesn't change that — it just makes the decision to stop feel more painful.

This matters especially for leaders, because admitting a project or plan isn't working can feel like admitting a personal failure, which creates emotional pressure to keep going even when the numbers or circumstances clearly say otherwise.

## Worked Example

A business owner has invested significant time and money building a product that market feedback increasingly suggests isn't going to work. The sunk-cost trap says: "We've put too much in to stop now." A clearer evaluation, ignoring past investment: "Based on what we know now, does continuing to invest make sense compared to redirecting that time and money elsewhere?" If the honest answer is no, the amount already spent doesn't change that — it's already gone regardless of the decision made today.

## Practice Quiz

1. Define the sunk-cost fallacy in your own words.
2. Why is past investment not actually relevant to a forward-looking decision?
3. What discipline does the lesson suggest for evaluating a decision?
4. Why does the sunk-cost trap hit leaders especially hard emotionally?

## Answer Key
1. Continuing to invest in something because of what's already been spent, rather than what makes sense going forward.
2. Because the past investment is gone regardless of the current decision — it shouldn't factor into a forward-looking evaluation.
3. Evaluate the decision as if encountering it fresh, based only on future costs and benefits.
4. Because stopping can feel like admitting personal failure, creating emotional pressure to continue despite the evidence.

## Answers and Explanations
1. This defines the core cognitive bias the lesson addresses.
2. Sunk costs are, by definition, unrecoverable regardless of the decision made now.
3. This discipline strips away the emotional pull of past investment from the actual decision.
4. Personal identification with a project intensifies the emotional difficulty of the sunk-cost trap for leaders specifically.

## Key Takeaways
- The sunk-cost trap is continuing to invest based on past spending rather than future value.
- Past investment is gone either way and shouldn't factor into a forward-looking decision.
- Evaluate decisions fresh, based only on future costs and benefits, to avoid this trap.
