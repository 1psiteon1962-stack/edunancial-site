# Staying Goal-Anchored While the Method Changes

## Learning Objectives
- Explain why teams need a stable goal even when tactics shift
- Identify the risk of a team losing sight of the goal during repeated pivots
- Practice communicating a stable goal through a changing method

## Core Content

When a leader has to change approach repeatedly — due to setbacks, new information, or a collapsed original plan — there's a real risk that the team starts to feel directionless, even if the underlying goal never actually changed. Frequent tactical shifts can look, from the outside, like a lack of clear direction, even when the leader has a very clear and stable "why" driving every change.

The fix is deliberate communication: explicitly separating the goal (which is staying the same) from the method (which is changing) every time a shift happens. "We're still trying to accomplish X — this new approach is just a better way to get there than what we tried before" reassures a team that the ground isn't constantly shifting under them, even when the day-to-day tactics are.

Leaders who fail to make this distinction explicit risk a team that becomes exhausted or cynical about "yet another new plan," even when each individual pivot was a reasonable, well-justified adjustment.

## Worked Example

A leader has pivoted a project's approach three times in two months due to changing market feedback. Without clear communication, the team starts grumbling about "constant changes" and losing confidence in the leader's direction. The leader addresses this directly in a team meeting: "I know this is the third approach we've tried — the goal hasn't changed at all, we're still building toward [specific outcome]. Each pivot has been based on real feedback that told us the previous method wasn't the right way to get there. That's not indecision — that's course-correcting toward the same destination." This explicit framing rebuilds confidence that the changes reflect responsiveness, not directionlessness.

## Practice Quiz

1. What risk does a leader face when changing tactics repeatedly?
2. What's the fix for this risk, according to this lesson?
3. Why might frequent pivots look like a lack of direction, even when the goal hasn't changed?
4. In the worked example, how did the leader rebuild the team's confidence?

## Answer Key
1. The team may start to feel directionless or exhausted, even if the underlying goal never changed.
2. Explicitly and repeatedly separating the stable goal from the changing method whenever a shift happens.
3. Because tactical changes are visible day-to-day, while the stable underlying goal is often not restated often enough to stay top of mind.
4. By explicitly explaining that the goal stayed the same and each pivot was a reasoned response to real feedback.

## Answers and Explanations
1. Repeated tactical shifts without clear communication can be mistaken for a lack of stable direction.
2. Deliberate, repeated communication is the practical fix for this perception risk.
3. Visibility asymmetry — tactics are seen constantly, the stable goal has to be actively restated to stay visible.
4. Explicit reframing reassured the team that the changes reflected responsiveness, not indecision.

## Key Takeaways
- Repeated tactical shifts risk making a team feel directionless, even when the underlying goal hasn't changed.
- Leaders must explicitly separate and communicate the stable goal from the changing method during every pivot.
- Clear communication turns "yet another new plan" into visible, reasoned course-correction toward the same destination.
