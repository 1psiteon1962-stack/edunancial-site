# Delegation Frameworks

## Learning Objectives
- Identify different levels of delegation and when to use each
- Explain why matching oversight level to task risk and person experience matters
- Practice choosing an appropriate delegation level for a given scenario

## Core Content

Not all delegation should look the same. Delegating a low-stakes task to an experienced team member calls for a very different level of oversight than delegating a high-stakes task to someone newer to the role. A useful framework has a few levels: "do it and tell me what you did" (full autonomy, appropriate for experienced people on lower-risk tasks), "do it, but check with me before finalizing" (partial autonomy, appropriate for either higher-stakes tasks or less experienced people), and "let's do this together" (close collaboration, appropriate for new, high-stakes, or unusually complex situations).

Choosing the wrong level in either direction causes problems. Too much oversight on a task someone is fully capable of handling wastes both people's time and can feel like a lack of trust. Too little oversight on a task where someone genuinely needs more support can lead to costly mistakes and a discouraging experience for the person who wasn't set up to succeed.

The right level isn't fixed forever — as someone gains experience and demonstrates reliability on a given type of task, the appropriate oversight level should decrease over time, which is itself a form of investment in their growth.

## Worked Example

A new hire is given a client-facing task with real revenue stakes at the "do it and tell me what you did" level, without adequate preparation, and makes a costly mistake that a more appropriate "let's do this together" level would have caught. Compare that to an experienced team member being required to check in on every small decision for a routine task they've handled successfully dozens of times — this wastes time and can start to feel like a lack of trust. Matching the delegation level to both the task's stakes and the person's actual experience avoids both failure modes.

## Practice Quiz

1. Name the three delegation levels described in this lesson.
2. What two factors should determine which level is appropriate?
3. What happens when too much oversight is applied to a capable person on a low-risk task?
4. What happens when too little oversight is applied to someone who needs more support?

## Answer Key
1. "Do it and tell me," "do it but check before finalizing," and "let's do this together."
2. The task's stakes/risk level and the person's actual experience with that type of task.
3. It wastes time for both people and can feel like a lack of trust.
4. It can lead to costly mistakes and a discouraging experience for someone who wasn't set up to succeed.

## Answers and Explanations
1. These three levels represent a practical spectrum of delegation oversight.
2. Matching oversight to both task risk and individual experience is the core principle of the framework.
3. Over-oversight has real costs, not just under-oversight, which is often the more discussed failure mode.
4. Under-oversight on a genuinely difficult or high-stakes task creates its own real risks.

## Key Takeaways
- Delegation should flex across levels of oversight, from full autonomy to close collaboration.
- The right level depends on both the task's stakes and the individual's actual experience with that type of work.
- Oversight level should decrease over time as someone demonstrates reliability, which is itself an investment in their growth.
