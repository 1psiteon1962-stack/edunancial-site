# Reading a Basic P&L as a Leader

## Learning Objectives
- Identify the basic sections of a profit and loss statement
- Explain what a leader should be looking for when reviewing a P&L
- Practice interpreting a simplified P&L example

## Core Content

A profit and loss statement, or P&L, summarizes revenue, expenses, and the resulting profit or loss over a specific period. A leader doesn't need to be an accountant to read one usefully — they need to understand the basic structure well enough to spot trends and ask good questions.

The basic structure: revenue at the top (total money coming in), then expenses broken into categories (cost of goods sold, operating expenses like rent and salaries, and other costs), with profit or loss at the bottom, after all expenses are subtracted from revenue. A leader reviewing a P&L should look for trends over time (is revenue growing or shrinking, are certain expense categories growing faster than revenue), not just the single bottom-line number for one period in isolation.

The goal isn't to replace a bookkeeper or accountant's expertise — it's to be informed enough to ask sharp questions, notice concerning trends early, and make decisions (from the earlier lesson on financial literacy) grounded in real numbers rather than a general feeling about how the business is doing.

## Worked Example

A leader reviewing three months of P&L statements notices that revenue has stayed roughly flat, but a specific expense category — software subscriptions — has crept up 40% over the same period, without a clear corresponding increase in usage or value. This trend wouldn't be obvious from a single month's snapshot, but comparing periods reveals it clearly. That observation leads to a useful, specific question for the team: why has this cost grown, and is it delivering proportional value?

## Practice Quiz

1. What are the three basic components of a P&L?
2. What should a leader look for when reviewing a P&L, beyond the single bottom-line number?
3. Is the goal of this skill to replace a bookkeeper or accountant?
4. In the worked example, what specific trend did comparing multiple periods reveal?

## Answer Key
1. Revenue, expenses (broken into categories), and the resulting profit or loss.
2. Trends over time — whether revenue and expense categories are growing, shrinking, or moving out of proportion with each other.
3. No — the goal is to be informed enough to ask sharp questions and notice trends, not to replace specialist expertise.
4. Software subscription costs rising 40% without a corresponding increase in usage or value, while revenue stayed flat.

## Answers and Explanations
1. This structure is the basic template underlying every P&L statement.
2. Trend analysis reveals patterns a single snapshot would miss entirely.
3. The lesson explicitly frames this as informed oversight, not a replacement for financial specialists.
4. This example demonstrates the value of comparing multiple periods rather than a single isolated number.

## Key Takeaways
- A P&L shows revenue, expenses, and resulting profit or loss over a period.
- Leaders should look for trends across periods, not just a single bottom-line number in isolation.
- The goal is informed oversight and sharp questions, not replacing a bookkeeper or accountant's expertise.
