# What a Leader Needs to Know About Cash Flow

## Learning Objectives
- Distinguish cash flow from profit
- Explain why a profitable business can still run into serious trouble
- Identify basic practices that help a leader monitor cash flow

## Core Content

Profit and cash flow are not the same thing, and confusing them is one of the more dangerous mistakes a leader can make. Profit is what's left after expenses are subtracted from revenue, on paper, over a period. Cash flow is about timing — when money actually arrives and when it actually has to go out. A business can be profitable on paper and still run out of actual cash to pay bills, if income arrives later than expenses are due.

This commonly happens when a business extends credit to customers (they owe money but haven't paid yet) while still having to pay its own bills — rent, payroll, suppliers — on a fixed schedule. The business might be profitable in the sense that it will eventually collect more than it owes, but if the timing doesn't line up, it can face a genuine cash crisis despite being profitable on paper.

Leaders should track cash flow separately from profit: how much cash is actually on hand right now, what's coming in and when, and what's going out and when. A simple cash flow forecast — projecting the next few weeks or months of expected cash in and out — catches timing problems before they become emergencies.

## Worked Example

A consulting business bills clients on 60-day payment terms, but has to pay its own contractors within 15 days. Even though the business is genuinely profitable — clients eventually pay more than the contractors cost — the timing gap between paying contractors quickly and collecting from clients slowly can create a real cash shortage in the meantime. A leader tracking cash flow separately from profit would see this gap coming and could arrange a short-term solution (a line of credit, adjusted payment terms) before it becomes an emergency, rather than being caught off guard by a "profitable" business that suddenly can't make payroll.

## Practice Quiz

1. What's the difference between profit and cash flow?
2. Why can a profitable business still run out of cash?
3. What commonly causes this kind of timing mismatch?
4. What practice helps a leader catch cash flow problems before they become emergencies?

## Answer Key
1. Profit is what's left after expenses on paper over a period; cash flow is about the actual timing of money in and out.
2. Because income can arrive later than expenses are due, creating a timing gap even when the business is profitable overall.
3. Extending credit to customers while still having fixed payment obligations of its own on a faster schedule.
4. A simple cash flow forecast projecting expected cash in and out over the coming weeks or months.

## Answers and Explanations
1. This distinction is the central concept the lesson teaches — profit and cash flow answer different questions.
2. Timing mismatches, not lack of underlying profitability, are the actual cause of many cash crises.
3. Payment term mismatches between what a business owes and what it's owed are a common source of this problem.
4. Forecasting surfaces timing gaps proactively, before they become urgent emergencies.

## Key Takeaways
- Profit and cash flow are different concepts — profit is on-paper, cash flow is about actual timing.
- A genuinely profitable business can still run into serious trouble if cash timing doesn't line up.
- Tracking cash flow separately, with a simple forecast, catches timing problems before they become crises.
