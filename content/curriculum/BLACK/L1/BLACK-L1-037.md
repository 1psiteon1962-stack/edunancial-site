# ROI Thinking Applied to Leadership Decisions

## Learning Objectives
- Define ROI thinking in plain terms
- Apply ROI thinking to non-financial leadership decisions
- Practice evaluating a leadership decision through an ROI lens

## Core Content

ROI, or return on investment, is normally discussed in financial terms — how much return a dollar spent generates. But the underlying logic — weighing what you're putting in against what you're likely to get back — applies just as usefully to non-financial leadership decisions, like where to invest time, attention, or organizational energy.

Applying ROI thinking to leadership means asking, for any significant investment of time or energy: what's the expected return here, and is it proportional to what's being invested? A leader who spends hours perfecting a minor internal document is investing time with very low expected return, while the same hours spent addressing a genuine bottleneck slowing the whole team down would have a much higher return.

This isn't about reducing every decision to a cold calculation — some investments (like relationship-building or team culture) have real but harder-to-quantify returns. It means staying conscious of proportionality: matching the size of an investment of time and energy to the actual expected value of the outcome, rather than spending disproportionate effort on low-value activities out of habit or avoidance of a harder priority.

## Worked Example

A leader spends an entire afternoon perfecting the formatting of an internal memo that only a few people will read once, while a genuine, ongoing bottleneck in the fulfillment process continues to cost the business real money and customer satisfaction. Applying ROI thinking would redirect that afternoon toward the fulfillment bottleneck instead — a much higher expected return on the same amount of invested time and energy.

## Key Takeaways
- ROI thinking applies beyond finance — to any investment of time, attention, or organizational energy.
- The core question is whether an investment's expected return is proportional to what's being put in.
- Staying conscious of proportionality prevents disproportionate effort on low-value activities at the expense of higher-value priorities.
