# Risk Tolerance as a Leadership Trait

## Learning Objectives
- Explain what risk tolerance means in a leadership context
- Identify the difference between healthy risk tolerance and recklessness
- Practice calibrating risk tolerance to a specific decision's actual stakes

## Core Content

Risk tolerance is how much uncertainty and potential downside a leader is willing to accept in pursuit of a potential upside. Every leader has some natural tendency toward caution or boldness, but effective leadership isn't about having a fixed level of risk tolerance — it's about calibrating that tolerance appropriately to each specific decision's actual stakes, rather than applying the same instinct to everything.

Healthy risk tolerance takes on real, considered risk when the potential upside justifies it and the downside is survivable. Recklessness takes on risk without honestly evaluating the downside, often driven by overconfidence or impatience. Excessive caution, on the opposite end, avoids reasonable risks even when the potential upside clearly justifies them, out of fear rather than genuine analysis.

This connects directly to the earlier lesson on reversibility in decision-making: a leader's risk tolerance should generally be higher for reversible decisions with a survivable downside, and lower for irreversible decisions with a potentially catastrophic downside, rather than a single fixed comfort level applied uniformly everywhere.

## Worked Example

A leader considers two decisions in the same week: testing a new, unproven marketing channel with a small, defined budget (low stakes, reversible, worth trying even without certainty of success), and taking on a large amount of debt to fund rapid expansion based on optimistic projections (high stakes, hard to reverse, deserving much more caution and analysis). Treating both decisions with the same risk tolerance — either uniformly cautious or uniformly bold — would be a mistake. Calibrating risk tolerance to each decision's actual stakes produces better outcomes than a fixed personal disposition applied everywhere.

- Risk tolerance should be calibrated to each specific decision's stakes, not applied as a fixed personal disposition.
- Healthy risk tolerance honestly weighs a survivable downside against a justified upside — recklessness and excessive caution both skip that honest evaluation.
- Higher risk tolerance fits reversible, survivable decisions; lower risk tolerance fits irreversible, high-stakes ones.
