# Succession Thinking: What Happens If You're Not There

## Learning Objectives
- Explain why succession thinking matters even for small organizations
- Identify the basic questions succession planning should answer
- Practice a simple succession thought exercise for a small operation

## Core Content

Succession planning sounds like something only large corporations with formal executive transitions need, but the underlying question applies to any organization, even a very small one: if the leader were suddenly unavailable — illness, emergency, or simply stepping away — what would happen? Organizations that have never seriously considered this question are often more fragile than they realize, because critical knowledge and decision-making authority may be concentrated entirely in one person.

Basic succession thinking doesn't require a formal, elaborate plan for a small business. It requires answering a few honest questions: who would make key decisions if I weren't available, even temporarily? Is critical information (passwords, key relationships, how things actually work) documented somewhere accessible, or only in my head? Is there at least one other person who understands enough about the business to keep it functioning in an emergency?

This connects directly to the earlier lesson on roles versus people — a business over-dependent on one specific individual, without any documentation or backup, is fragile in exactly the way that lesson warned against, just applied at the very top of the organization.

## Worked Example

A solo business owner has never considered what would happen if they were suddenly hospitalized for an extended period. Walking through the succession thinking exercise reveals: nobody else has access to key accounts, no one else knows which suppliers are reliable and which aren't, and there's no documented process for the few recurring decisions that need to be made weekly. Recognizing this, the owner spends a few hours documenting key access information and briefing a trusted family member or employee on the basics — a small investment that meaningfully reduces the business's fragility to an unexpected absence.

## Practice Quiz

1. Why does succession thinking matter even for a very small organization?
2. Name the three basic questions succession thinking should answer.
3. How does this lesson connect to the earlier roles-versus-people lesson?
4. In the worked example, what specific gaps did the succession thinking exercise reveal?

## Answer Key
1. Because critical knowledge and decision-making authority may be concentrated entirely in one person, creating real fragility.
2. Who would make key decisions in my absence, is critical information documented, and is there at least one other person who could keep things functioning?
3. It applies the roles-versus-people principle (avoiding over-dependence on one undocumented individual) at the very top of the organization.
4. No one else had account access, knew reliable suppliers, or had a documented process for recurring decisions.

## Answers and Explanations
1. Concentration of critical knowledge in one person creates a specific, identifiable organizational fragility.
2. These three questions form a practical, minimal succession thinking exercise for any size organization.
3. The same undocumented single-point-of-failure risk applies whether it's a mid-level role or the top leader.
4. This example demonstrates concrete, previously invisible risks surfaced by the exercise.

## Key Takeaways
- Succession thinking applies to organizations of any size, not just large corporations with formal plans.
- Basic succession thinking answers who decides, what's documented, and who else could keep things running.
- This connects directly to avoiding over-dependence on one undocumented individual, even at the very top of an organization.
