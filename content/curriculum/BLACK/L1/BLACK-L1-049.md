# Capstone: Designing a 90-Day Leadership Action Plan

## Learning Objectives
- Synthesize concepts from across the track into a concrete personal action plan
- Identify specific, measurable leadership goals for a defined time period
- Practice structuring a leadership development plan with real accountability checkpoints

## Core Content

Knowledge without application stays theoretical. A 90-day leadership action plan takes the concepts from across this track and turns them into specific, concrete commitments with a defined timeline, rather than leaving them as abstract ideas to apply "eventually."

A useful 90-day plan should identify: one or two specific leadership behaviors to develop (drawing on any lesson in this track — delegation, feedback, accountability, whatever's most relevant to your actual situation), how progress will be measured or checked (connecting to the KPI lesson — vague intentions are hard to track, specific ones aren't), and built-in checkpoints (at 30, 60, and 90 days) to honestly assess progress and adjust the plan based on what's actually being learned along the way.

The plan should be specific enough to actually act on Monday morning, not a vague aspiration like "become a better leader." "Delegate at least one recurring task I'm currently doing myself by day 30, using the delegation framework from this track" is specific and checkable. "Get better at delegating" is not.

## Worked Example

A new manager builds a 90-day plan: Days 1–30, document one currently undocumented role (connecting to the roles-vs-people lesson) and delegate one task using the appropriate oversight level from the delegation framework. Days 31–60, practice giving specific, behavior-focused feedback (from the feedback lesson) at least weekly, tracking how the team responds. Days 61–90, run a basic succession thinking exercise for their own role, identifying what's still only in their head. At each 30-day checkpoint, they honestly assess what worked, what didn't, and adjust the next phase accordingly, rather than rigidly following the original plan regardless of what's actually happening.

## Practice Quiz

1. Why does the lesson emphasize turning concepts into a concrete plan rather than leaving them abstract?
2. Name the three components a useful 90-day plan should include.
3. Why is "delegate one task by day 30" better than "get better at delegating"?
4. Draft one specific, checkable goal for your own hypothetical 90-day leadership plan, drawing on any lesson from this track.

## Answer Key
1. Because knowledge without application stays theoretical and doesn't actually change behavior.
2. Specific behaviors to develop, a way to measure progress, and built-in checkpoints to assess and adjust.
3. Because it's specific and checkable, while the vague version gives no way to know if it actually happened.
4. Answers will vary but should be specific and measurable, drawing on a concept covered earlier in the track.

## Answers and Explanations
1. This is the practical rationale for building a concrete plan rather than relying on abstract intention alone.
2. These three components structure a plan that's actually actionable and trackable.
3. Specificity is what makes a goal genuinely checkable rather than aspirational and vague.
4. A correct answer connects a specific goal to a specific concept from the track, with a clear way to measure it.

## Key Takeaways
- Turning leadership concepts into a concrete, time-bound plan is what actually changes behavior, not abstract intention alone.
- A useful plan identifies specific behaviors, a way to measure progress, and built-in checkpoints for honest assessment.
- Specific, checkable goals ("delegate one task by day 30") are far more useful than vague aspirations ("get better at delegating").
