## BLACK-L2-015
### Conflict Resolution: Understanding Sources of Workplace Conflict

**1. Learning Objectives**
By the end of this lesson, the learner will be able to:
- Identify common sources of workplace conflict.
- Explain why understanding the source matters more than addressing surface symptoms alone.
- Apply this concept to a hypothetical conflict scenario.

**2. Core Content**
Common sources of workplace conflict include unclear or competing expectations (echoing Lesson 23's expectation-setting discussion), differing communication or work styles (echoing Lesson 7's situational leadership discussion of individual differences), resource or recognition scarcity (competition for limited opportunities or acknowledgment), and genuine differences in values or priorities regarding how work should be approached.

Understanding a conflict's actual underlying source matters more than addressing only its surface symptoms, echoing ORANGE-L2-027's objection-handling reframing and Lesson 11's active listening discussion: two team members who appear to be arguing about a specific task assignment might actually be experiencing an underlying conflict rooted in unclear role expectations or a perceived recognition imbalance, meaning a resolution addressing only the surface task assignment without addressing the underlying source is likely to see the conflict resurface in a different form later.

**3. Worked Example**
Mediating a dispute between two team members that appears, on the surface, to be about a specific project responsibility, a leader applies active listening (Lesson 11) to understand each person's genuine underlying concern, discovering the actual source is an unclear division of responsibilities that has left both team members feeling uncertain about their respective roles, rather than a genuine disagreement about the specific task itself. Addressing this underlying role clarity issue, rather than simply assigning the specific disputed task to one person, resolves the conflict more durably than a surface-level fix would have.

**4. Practice Quiz**
1. What is a common source of workplace conflict, per this lesson?
   a) Perfectly clear, non-competing expectations with no ambiguity
   b) Unclear or competing expectations, differing work styles, or resource/recognition scarcity
   c) A type of tax credit
   d) A source entirely unrelated to any workplace dynamic
2. Why does understanding a conflict's underlying source matter more than addressing surface symptoms alone?
   a) Surface symptoms always fully reflect the underlying cause with no distinction
   b) A resolution addressing only surface symptoms without the underlying source is likely to see the conflict resurface in a different form
   c) Underlying sources never actually exist in workplace conflicts
   d) Active listening has no relationship to understanding conflict sources
3. True or False: A dispute appearing to be about a specific task assignment always has no deeper underlying source worth investigating.
4. What did the leader's active listening reveal about the team members' apparent task dispute?
   a) The dispute was purely about the specific task with no deeper underlying issue
   b) The actual underlying source was an unclear division of responsibilities, not the specific task itself
   c) Active listening revealed nothing useful about the situation
   d) The team members had no genuine underlying concern at all

**5. Answer Key**
1. b  2. b  3. False  4. b

**6. Answers and Explanations**
1. Unclear or competing expectations, differing work styles, and resource/recognition scarcity are common sources of workplace conflict.
2. Addressing only surface symptoms without the underlying source is likely to see the conflict resurface in a different form later.
3. A surface-level dispute often has a deeper underlying source worth investigating through active listening.
4. The leader's active listening revealed an underlying role clarity issue distinct from the surface-level task dispute.

**7. Key Takeaways**
- Common conflict sources include unclear expectations, differing work styles, and resource or recognition scarcity.
- Understanding the actual underlying source matters more than addressing surface symptoms alone.
- A surface-level fix without addressing the underlying source often sees the conflict resurface differently.
- This echoes ORANGE-L2-027's objection-handling reframing and Lesson 11's active listening discussion.
