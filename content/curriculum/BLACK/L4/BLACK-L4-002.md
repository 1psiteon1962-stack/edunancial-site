# Situational Leadership Analysis: Why the Same Leader Should Adapt Style to the Follower's Readiness

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a widespread public health disruption halting global shipping and manufacturing for an extended stretch, snarling supply chains across many industries at once. As a widespread disruption forces leaders to manage team members with genuinely different experience levels and different comfort handling ambiguity, some discover that applying an identical leadership style to every team member regardless of their specific readiness produces meaningfully worse results than adapting that style to each person's actual situation.

## Learning Objectives
- Explain the general premise of situational leadership: that the appropriate leadership style depends on a specific follower's readiness level for a specific task, rather than a single style being universally appropriate.
- Explain why a highly directive style is generally more appropriate for a follower with low readiness (low competence, low or high commitment) for a specific task.
- Explain why a more delegative, hands-off style is generally more appropriate for a follower with high readiness (high competence, high commitment) for that same task.
- Explain why the same individual may require a genuinely different leadership style for different specific tasks, since readiness is task-specific rather than a fixed, general personal trait.

## Core Content
VERIFIED FACT: the situational leadership model holds that the most effective leadership style depends on a specific follower's readiness level for a specific task at hand — a combination of that person's actual competence (skill and knowledge) and commitment (confidence and motivation) for that particular task — rather than any single leadership style being universally appropriate regardless of who is being led or what task is actually involved.

Here is the "why" a highly directive style is generally more appropriate for a follower with low readiness for a specific task: a follower who has low competence for a given task genuinely needs specific, detailed direction regarding exactly how to perform that task, since they do not yet possess the skill or knowledge to figure this out reliably on their own, meaning a highly directive leadership style (providing specific instructions and closely monitoring execution) generally serves this follower's actual developmental need for this particular task considerably better than a more hands-off, delegative approach would, which would likely leave a genuinely low-competence follower struggling without the specific guidance they actually need.

This is why a more delegative, hands-off style is generally more appropriate for a follower with high readiness for that same task: a follower who has both high competence and high commitment for a given task has already demonstrated the skill and the genuine motivation to execute that task well independently, meaning continuing to apply the same highly directive style this follower may have needed earlier in their development would likely feel unnecessarily restrictive and could even undermine their confidence and motivation, since a genuinely capable, motivated follower generally benefits more from delegated autonomy and trust than from continued close direction they no longer actually need. This is why the same individual may require a genuinely different leadership style for different specific tasks, since readiness is task-specific rather than a fixed, general personal trait: a team member might have high readiness for one specific task they have performed many times before, warranting a delegative approach for that task, while having genuinely low readiness for a different, entirely new task they have never attempted, warranting a more directive approach for that specific new task — this is precisely why situational leadership requires a leader to assess readiness on a task-by-task basis, rather than assigning a single, fixed leadership style to a specific individual across every task they might be asked to perform.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: A leader manages a team member who has extensive experience and demonstrated skill in one specific area of responsibility, appropriately delegating that specific work with considerable autonomy and minimal direct oversight. When this same team member is assigned an entirely new task they have never previously performed, the leader appropriately shifts to a more directive style for this specific new task, providing detailed guidance and closer monitoring until the team member develops genuine competence and confidence in this particular area as well — illustrating precisely why situational leadership requires assessing and adapting to readiness on a task-by-task basis, rather than applying a single, fixed style to this team member across every kind of task they might be asked to perform.

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** This dependence of appropriate leadership style on specific follower readiness for a specific task is precisely the general premise situational leadership describes.

**Q2.** This genuine developmental need for specific guidance when competence is low is precisely why a directive style serves a low-readiness follower's actual needs for that particular task.

**Q3.** This recognition that continued close direction becomes counterproductive once genuine competence and commitment are demonstrated is precisely why a delegative style suits a high-readiness follower better.

**Q4.** This task-specific nature of readiness, rather than a fixed general trait, is precisely why the same individual may require genuinely different leadership styles depending on the specific task actually involved.

**Q5.** This direct illustration of task-specific readiness assessment driving genuinely different leadership approaches for the same individual exemplifies precisely the situational leadership principle this lesson describes.

## Key Takeaways
- Situational leadership holds that the most effective leadership style depends on a follower's specific readiness (competence and commitment) for a specific task.
- A directive style generally suits low-readiness followers who genuinely need specific guidance they do not yet have the skill to provide themselves.
- A delegative style generally suits high-readiness followers, since continued close direction becomes unnecessary and potentially counterproductive once genuine competence and commitment are demonstrated.
- Readiness is task-specific, meaning the same individual may warrant genuinely different leadership styles depending on which specific task is actually involved.
