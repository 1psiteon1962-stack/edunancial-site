# Organisational Culture Analysis: Why Culture Is What People Actually Do, Not What Is Officially Stated

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a populous nation's real estate and consumer credit markets, after years of rapid expansion, beginning to show signs of a sharp correction as defaults start rising. As a credit-fueled boom unwinds and businesses face genuine pressure to cut corners, some organisations discover that their officially stated values mean very little compared to what leaders and employees actually do when facing this kind of real, difficult pressure.

## Learning Objectives
- Explain why an organisation's genuine culture is defined by the actual, consistent behaviours people exhibit, particularly when unobserved, rather than by officially stated values or a written mission statement.
- Explain why a leader's own specific behaviour functions as a particularly powerful cultural signal, disproportionately shaping what employees actually believe the organisation genuinely values.
- Explain why a gap between stated values and actual organisational behaviour undermines employee trust more than simply never having stated those values at all.
- Explain why genuine culture change requires changing actual incentives and modelled behaviour, not merely updating a written values statement.

## Core Content
VERIFIED FACT: an organisation's genuine culture is generally defined by the actual, consistent behaviours people within that organisation exhibit, particularly the behaviours they exhibit when they believe they are not being closely observed or held immediately accountable, rather than by whatever values an organisation has officially stated in a written mission statement or values document, meaning the true test of an organisation's genuine culture lies in what people actually do under real, difficult circumstances, not in what the organisation claims to value in its official communications.

Here is the "why" a leader's own specific behaviour functions as a particularly powerful cultural signal, disproportionately shaping what employees actually believe the organisation genuinely values: employees generally observe and draw conclusions from what their leaders actually do, especially under genuine pressure or when a leader's own specific stated values might conflict with a more expedient or personally convenient course of action, considerably more than they draw conclusions from what any official document states, meaning a leader who behaves inconsistently with the organisation's officially stated values sends employees a considerably more powerful, more credible signal about the organisation's genuine actual priorities than the official statement itself ever could, regardless of how carefully that official statement may have been crafted.

This is why a gap between stated values and actual organisational behaviour undermines employee trust more than simply never having stated those values at all: an organisation that explicitly states specific values, but then behaves inconsistently with those stated values, particularly under genuine pressure, creates a specific, visible credibility gap that employees generally notice and remember, actively undermining trust in the organisation's other communications as well, since employees reasonably wonder what other stated commitments might similarly diverge from actual practice — this specific credibility cost is generally considered worse than an organisation that simply never made the specific values claim in the first place, since the explicit claim followed by inconsistent behaviour creates an active, visible contradiction rather than simply an absence of any stated commitment. This is why genuine culture change requires changing actual incentives and modelled behaviour, not merely updating a written values statement: since genuine culture is defined by actual, consistent behaviour rather than official statements, changing an organisation's genuine culture requires changing the actual incentives, actual accountability mechanisms, and actual behaviour leaders themselves model, rather than simply revising the written values document — an organisation attempting to change its culture purely by updating its official statement, without any corresponding change in actual incentives or leader behaviour, generally fails to produce any genuine change in how people actually behave, since the underlying behavioural drivers the genuine culture actually reflects remain entirely unchanged.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: An organisation's official values statement prominently emphasises honesty and transparency, but as a credit-fueled boom unwinds and genuine financial pressure mounts, several leaders within the organisation begin quietly cutting corners and withholding unfavorable information from stakeholders. Employees observing this actual leadership behaviour conclude that the organisation's genuine, actual priority is short-term results over the stated commitment to honesty, and this observed gap between stated values and actual behaviour damages employee trust considerably more than if the organisation had never made the honesty claim at all, illustrating precisely why genuine culture is defined by actual behaviour, particularly under pressure, rather than by official statements.

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** This definition based on actual, consistent behaviour, particularly when unobserved, rather than official statements, is precisely what defines genuine organisational culture.

**Q2.** This disproportionate weight employees place on observed leader behaviour over official statements is precisely why a leader's own conduct functions as such a powerful cultural signal.

**Q3.** This creation of an active, visible contradiction, rather than a mere absence of any stated commitment, is precisely why this specific gap carries a particularly significant trust cost.

**Q4.** This need to address the actual underlying behavioural drivers, rather than merely the official statement, is precisely why genuine culture change requires more than updating a values document.

**Q5.** This direct illustration of observed behaviour contradicting stated values, and employees drawing conclusions from the actual behaviour rather than the official statement, exemplifies precisely why this lesson defines genuine culture by actual conduct rather than official claims.

## Key Takeaways
- An organisation's genuine culture is defined by actual, consistent behaviour, particularly when unobserved, rather than officially stated values.
- A leader's own behaviour functions as a particularly powerful cultural signal, since employees weight observed conduct more heavily than official statements.
- A gap between stated values and actual behaviour undermines trust more than never stating those values, since it creates a visible, active contradiction.
- Genuine culture change requires changing actual incentives and leader-modelled behaviour, not merely updating a written values statement.
