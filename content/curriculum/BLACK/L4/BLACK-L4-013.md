# Vision Communication Analysis: Why a Vision Must Be Specific Enough to Guide Daily Decisions

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a widespread public health disruption halting global shipping and manufacturing for an extended stretch, snarling supply chains across many industries at once. As a widespread disruption forces employees across an organization to make fast, independent decisions without direct supervision, leaders discover that their organization's stated vision, if too abstract or generic, provides employees no genuine guidance for these actual, concrete decisions, while a more specific vision genuinely helps.

## Learning Objectives
- Explain why an organizational vision that remains too abstract or generic fails to actually guide employees' day-to-day decision-making.
- Explain why an effective vision must be specific enough that an employee facing a genuine, concrete decision can reasonably determine which choice the vision would actually favor.
- Explain the practical test of whether a vision is genuinely specific enough: whether a reasonable employee could use it to resolve an actual, concrete tradeoff they might face.
- Explain why a vision's specificity must be balanced against genuine flexibility, since an overly narrow, rigidly specific vision can fail to accommodate legitimate situations the vision's authors did not specifically anticipate.

## Core Content
VERIFIED FACT: an organizational vision that remains too abstract or generic ("we strive for excellence," "we put our customers first") generally fails to actually guide employees' day-to-day decision-making, since this kind of generic language could apply equally to almost any organization and provides no genuine, concrete basis for determining which specific choice a real employee facing an actual, concrete decision should actually make, meaning employees are effectively left to determine their own specific priorities without genuine guidance from the vision itself, despite the vision's stated intent to provide exactly this kind of guidance.

Here is the "why" an effective vision must be specific enough that an employee facing a genuine, concrete decision can reasonably determine which choice the vision would actually favor: a genuinely effective vision translates its broader aspiration into specific enough terms that a reasonable employee, facing an actual tradeoff (speed versus thoroughness, cost versus quality, a specific customer's immediate request versus a broader policy), can reasonably determine which specific choice the organization's genuine priorities would actually favor in that particular situation, meaning the vision functions as an actual, practical decision-making tool for employees making real, everyday choices, rather than merely serving as an inspirational but practically unhelpful statement disconnected from actual daily decision-making.

This is why the practical test of whether a vision is genuinely specific enough asks whether a reasonable employee could use it to resolve an actual, concrete tradeoff they might face: a useful diagnostic question for evaluating whether a specific organizational vision is genuinely specific enough is to consider a realistic, concrete decision a typical employee might actually face, and ask whether that vision, as actually stated, provides genuine, practical guidance for resolving that specific decision, or whether the vision remains so abstract that it could be invoked to justify essentially any choice the employee might have made regardless — a vision failing this practical test generally requires further specification before it can genuinely serve its intended decision-guiding purpose. This is why a vision's specificity must be balanced against genuine flexibility, since an overly narrow, rigidly specific vision can fail to accommodate legitimate situations its authors did not specifically anticipate: OPINION and an important balancing consideration, a vision specified so narrowly and rigidly that it only addresses a very specific set of anticipated situations risks providing poor or actively counterproductive guidance in a genuinely different situation the vision's original authors simply did not anticipate, meaning sound vision communication generally aims for a specific middle ground — specific enough to provide genuine guidance for the range of realistic decisions employees are likely to actually face, while retaining enough principled flexibility to remain sensibly applicable to legitimate situations that were not specifically anticipated in advance.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: An organization's initial vision statement, "we put our customers first," provides employees facing a genuine tradeoff between honoring a specific, unusual customer request and following a standard organizational policy no genuine, practical guidance for resolving that actual decision, since the generic statement could be invoked to justify either choice. After revising the vision to specifically articulate the organization's actual priority — for example, prioritizing long-term customer trust over short-term policy consistency in ambiguous cases, while still retaining principled flexibility for genuinely exceptional situations — employees facing this same kind of real tradeoff now have a genuinely useful, specific basis for determining which choice the organization's actual priorities would favor, illustrating precisely why vision specificity matters for genuine, practical decision guidance.

## Key Takeaways
- An overly abstract or generic vision provides employees no genuine, concrete basis for actual day-to-day decision-making.
- An effective vision must be specific enough that a reasonable employee facing a real tradeoff can determine which choice the organization's genuine priorities would favor.
- The practical test of vision specificity asks whether a reasonable employee could use it to resolve an actual, concrete decision, rather than it being invokable to justify any choice.
