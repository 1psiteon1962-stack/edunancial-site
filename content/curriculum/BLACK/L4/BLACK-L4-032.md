# Strategic Planning Analysis: Why Strategy Requires Genuine Tradeoffs, Not Just Aspiration

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a widespread public health disruption halting global shipping and manufacturing for an extended stretch, snarling supply chains across many industries at once. As a widespread disruption forces organizations to genuinely prioritize among many possible responses with limited resources, those with strategic plans consisting only of broad aspirational goals discover these plans provide no actual guidance for the genuine tradeoffs the disruption now requires them to make.

## Learning Objectives
- Explain why a genuine strategy requires making explicit, difficult tradeoffs about what an organization will deliberately choose not to pursue.
- Explain why a strategic plan consisting only of aspirational goals, without these genuine tradeoffs, fails to actually function as a genuine strategy, connecting to this level's Lesson 13 vision specificity discussion.
- Explain why genuine tradeoffs are difficult to articulate specifically because they require explicitly acknowledging that some genuinely desirable goal will not be fully pursued, at least not immediately.
- Explain why an organization's actual resource allocation decisions, not merely its stated strategic priorities, provide the most reliable evidence of what its genuine strategy actually is.

## Core Content
VERIFIED FACT: OPINION and a widely cited principle in strategic management, a genuine strategy requires making explicit, difficult tradeoffs about what an organization will deliberately choose not to pursue, at least not as a genuine priority, since a strategy attempting to simultaneously pursue every desirable goal without any genuine prioritization among them is not actually a strategy at all, but rather a list of aspirations lacking the genuine prioritization and tradeoff decisions that distinguish an actual strategy from a simple wish list of desirable outcomes.

Here is the "why" a strategic plan consisting only of aspirational goals fails to actually function as a genuine strategy, connecting to this level's Lesson 13 vision specificity discussion: echoing Lesson 13's discussion of why a vision must be specific enough to guide actual decisions, a strategic plan stating only broad, aspirational goals ("we will be the industry leader in quality, cost, and speed") without genuinely specifying which of these goals will actually take priority when they inevitably come into tension with one another provides no genuine guidance for the actual, concrete tradeoff decisions an organization will inevitably face, meaning this kind of aspiration-only plan fails the same practical decision-guiding test Lesson 13 applied to organizational vision, providing inspiring language without the genuine specificity a real strategy actually requires.

This is why genuine tradeoffs are difficult to articulate specifically because they require explicitly acknowledging that some genuinely desirable goal will not be fully pursued, at least not immediately: OPINION and an honest observation regarding why genuine strategic tradeoffs are often avoided, explicitly stating that an organization will prioritize one genuinely desirable goal over another genuinely desirable goal requires openly acknowledging that the deprioritized goal, however genuinely desirable in the abstract, will not actually receive the organization's full priority, a specific acknowledgment that can feel uncomfortable or even risky to state explicitly, since doing so opens the strategy to genuine, specific critique from anyone who would have preferred the deprioritized goal receive greater emphasis — this discomfort is precisely why many organizations default to vague, aspiration-only strategic language that avoids this kind of explicit, potentially uncomfortable tradeoff articulation, even though this same avoidance is exactly what prevents the resulting plan from actually functioning as a genuine, useful strategy. This is why an organization's actual resource allocation decisions provide the most reliable evidence of what its genuine strategy actually is, echoing this level's Lesson 8 discussion of actual behavior over official statements: precisely because a stated strategic plan can remain comfortably vague regarding genuine tradeoffs, an organization's actual pattern of resource allocation — where its budget, its talent, and its leadership attention actually go in practice — reveals its genuine, actual strategic priorities considerably more reliably than its officially stated strategic language, echoing the same broader principle this level's earlier organizational culture discussion applied to stated values versus actual behavior, now applied specifically to the relationship between stated strategy and actual resource allocation.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: An organization's strategic plan initially states only broad, aspirational goals — being the industry leader in quality, cost, and speed simultaneously — providing no genuine guidance when a widespread disruption forces the organization to make an actual tradeoff between maintaining quality standards and reducing costs under genuine resource constraints. After revising its strategy to explicitly state that quality will take priority over cost minimization when the two genuinely conflict, even while acknowledging this means the organization will not always be the absolute lowest-cost option, the organization's leadership now has genuine, specific guidance for resolving this real tradeoff, and its subsequent actual resource allocation decisions consistently reflect this explicitly stated priority, illustrating precisely why genuine strategy requires explicit tradeoffs rather than aspiration alone.
