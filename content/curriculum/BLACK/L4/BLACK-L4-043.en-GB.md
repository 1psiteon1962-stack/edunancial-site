# Scenario Planning Analysis: Why Preparing for Multiple Futures Beats Betting on a Single Predicted One

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a central bank raising its benchmark interest rate sharply and repeatedly over a short stretch of months to fight rising inflation. As a central bank's sharply rising interest rates create genuine uncertainty about future economic conditions, organisations that had prepared for several genuinely plausible future scenarios, rather than betting entirely on a single predicted outcome, respond considerably more effectively than organisations whose entire strategy depended on one specific predicted future actually materialising.

## Learning Objectives
- Explain why deliberately preparing for several genuinely plausible future scenarios produces more resilient organisational strategy than betting entirely on a single predicted future, connecting to this level's Lesson 7 decision-making-under-uncertainty discussion.
- Explain why scenario planning does not require accurately predicting which specific scenario will actually occur, but rather requires identifying actions that remain reasonable across several genuinely plausible different scenarios.
- Explain why an organisation whose entire strategy depends on a single predicted future becomes genuinely vulnerable if that specific predicted future does not actually materialise.
- Explain why scenario planning still requires genuine, disciplined prioritisation among the identified scenarios, rather than attempting to prepare equally and fully for every conceivable future with no meaningful prioritisation at all.

## Core Content
Building directly on this level's Lesson 7 decision-making-under-uncertainty discussion, VERIFIED FACT, deliberately preparing for several genuinely plausible future scenarios, rather than betting an organisation's entire strategy on a single specific predicted future, produces more resilient organisational strategy given the genuine, well-documented unpredictability of significant future developments, since Lesson 7 established that genuine certainty about the future rarely actually arrives, meaning an organisation that has deliberately considered several genuinely plausible different futures, rather than committing entirely to a single predicted one, is considerably better positioned to respond effectively regardless of which specific future actually materialises.

Here is the "why" scenario planning does not require accurately predicting which specific scenario will actually occur, but rather requires identifying actions that remain reasonable across several genuinely plausible different scenarios: the genuine value of scenario planning lies specifically in identifying strategic actions and organisational capabilities that would remain genuinely reasonable and valuable across several different plausible futures, rather than in accurately predicting which single specific future will actually occur, meaning an organisation practising genuine scenario planning is not attempting the genuinely difficult, often practically impossible task of accurately forecasting the single correct future, but rather the more genuinely achievable task of identifying robust actions that would serve the organisation reasonably well regardless of which of several genuinely plausible futures actually materialises.

This is why an organisation whose entire strategy depends on a single predicted future becomes genuinely vulnerable if that specific predicted future does not actually materialise: an organisation that has committed its entire strategy and resource allocation to a single specific predicted future, without genuinely considering plausible alternative futures, faces genuine, significant vulnerability if that specific predicted future does not actually materialise as expected, since this kind of organisation has no genuine alternative strategic preparation to fall back upon, echoing this level's earlier organisational resilience discussion regarding the genuine fragility that can result from optimising entirely around a single assumed set of conditions, without any genuine preparation for a genuinely different set of conditions actually occurring instead. This is why scenario planning still requires genuine, disciplined prioritisation among identified scenarios, rather than attempting to prepare equally and fully for every conceivable future: OPINION and an important practical constraint, since an organisation's actual resources for genuine scenario preparation are inherently finite, sound scenario planning generally requires genuinely, disciplined prioritisation among a limited number of the most genuinely plausible and most strategically consequential scenarios, rather than attempting the practically impossible task of preparing equally and fully for literally every conceivable future scenario with no meaningful prioritisation at all, meaning genuine scenario planning involves a deliberate, disciplined balance between genuine preparedness for multiple plausible futures and the practical, resource-based necessity of focusing that preparation on a genuinely manageable, prioritised set of the most plausible and consequential scenarios.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: An organisation facing genuine uncertainty from sharply rising interest rates deliberately identifies several genuinely plausible future scenarios — continued rate increases, a plateau, and a subsequent reversal — and identifies specific strategic actions and organisational capabilities that would serve the organisation reasonably well across all three of these plausible scenarios, rather than betting its entire strategy on a single predicted outcome. When the actual future turns out to differ somewhat from what most analysts had originally predicted, this organisation remains reasonably well positioned, since its strategy had genuinely accounted for this kind of plausible alternative from the outset, unlike a different organisation that had committed its entire strategy and resource allocation to a single predicted future that ultimately did not actually materialise.
