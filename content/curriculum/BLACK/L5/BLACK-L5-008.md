# Raising Capital: Orchestrating Investor Relations, Dilution, Governance, and Mission

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): A founder of a fast-growing software company receives two offers: a large investment from a fund that wants a board seat and rapid growth, and a smaller loan from a bank that requires a personal guarantee. A third option is to grow slowly using the company's own profits.

## Learning Objectives
- Compare equity investment, debt, and self-funding as ways to finance growth.
- Explain dilution and why the percentage of ownership a founder keeps matters.
- Explain how outside investors change governance and expectations.
- Apply an orchestrated evaluation to choose a financing path aligned with the company's mission.

## Core Content
PRINCIPLE: Money always comes with conditions. A leader raising capital is not only choosing an amount; they are choosing partners, obligations, and a pace of growth.

Equity investment exchanges ownership for money. The company does not have to repay it, but the founder's percentage of ownership shrinks — this is dilution. Equity investors usually expect a large return within a certain number of years, often through a sale of the company. They may require board seats, approval rights over major decisions, and regular reporting. These terms change how the company is governed.

Debt must be repaid with interest, but the founder keeps ownership. Lenders may require collateral, financial covenants, or a personal guarantee, which places the founder's personal assets at risk — a connection to the GREEN and PURPLE tracks. Debt works best when cash flow is predictable.

Self-funding preserves control and avoids outside obligations, but growth is limited by profits, and a competitor with outside funding may move faster.

Orchestration means matching the financing to the mission and the business model. A company that intends to stay independent and serve a specific community may be poorly matched with an investor who needs a sale within five years. A company in a race for market share may need outside equity. The leader should also consider personal risk tolerance and how each option affects employees and customers.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: The software founder writes down the company's ten-year goals: remain founder-led, serve small businesses at affordable prices, and avoid a forced sale. The fund's terms would require doubling revenue every year and a likely sale. The founder instead takes a smaller amount of equity from an investor whose goals match a slower timeline, negotiates limited board rights, and uses a modest credit line without a personal guarantee for working capital. Growth is slower than the fund promised, but the company's mission and the founder's control remain intact.

## Practice Quiz
**Q1. What is dilution?**
A) The interest rate on a loan
B) A decrease in company revenue
C) A tax on investment income
D) The reduction in a founder's ownership percentage when new equity is issued

**Q2. What does a personal guarantee on a loan mean for the founder?**
A) The bank guarantees the company's success
B) The loan never has to be repaid
C) The founder's personal assets may be at risk if the company cannot repay
D) The founder gives up all ownership

**Q3. How can equity investors change a company's governance?**
A) They have no influence once the money is received
B) They may require board seats, approval rights, and reporting
C) They always manage daily operations
D) They can only influence the company's marketing

**Q4. Why should financing be matched to the company's mission?**
A) Investors' expectations about growth and exit can conflict with the company's long-term goals
B) Mission has no connection to financing
C) All investors have identical goals
D) Debt is always better than equity

**Q5. In the example, why did the founder decline the large fund offer?**
A) The fund offered too little money
B) Equity is illegal for software companies
C) The founder wanted to grow faster
D) Its growth and sale expectations conflicted with the company's long-term goals

## Answer Key
Q1: D
Q2: C
Q3: B
Q4: A
Q5: D

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** Issuing new shares spreads ownership across more holders, reducing each existing owner's percentage.

**Q2.** A personal guarantee makes the founder personally responsible for the debt.

**Q3.** Investors often negotiate rights that give them a voice in major decisions.

**Q4.** Investor timelines and exit expectations shape company behavior, so they must fit the mission.

**Q5.** Rapid growth and a likely sale contradicted the founder's goals of independence and affordability.

## Key Takeaways
- Every source of capital comes with conditions: ownership, repayment, control, or pace.
- Equity dilutes ownership and changes governance; debt keeps ownership but adds repayment risk.
- Personal guarantees put a founder's personal assets at risk.
- Choose financing that fits the mission, the business model, and the leader's risk tolerance.
