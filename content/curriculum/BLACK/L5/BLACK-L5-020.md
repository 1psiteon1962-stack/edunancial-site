# Own or Lease: Orchestrating Real Estate, Capital, Tax, and Flexibility for a Growing Organization

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): A dental practice with two locations is offered the chance to buy the building it currently leases. The owner likes the idea of building equity but is also considering opening a third location in the next two years.

## Learning Objectives
- Compare owning and leasing business premises across capital, risk, and flexibility.
- Explain how buying property can compete with investment in the core business.
- Explain why tax and legal structure affect the decision.
- Apply an orchestrated framework connecting real estate decisions to business strategy.

## Core Content
PRINCIPLE: For a business, real estate is both an operating need and an investment. Combining the two can be powerful, but the RED track's principles must be coordinated with the business's own priorities.

Owning offers control over the space, protection from rent increases, and the potential for appreciation. It also requires a large down payment, ongoing maintenance, and responsibility for property risks. Capital used for a building is capital not available for equipment, staff, marketing, or new locations — the core activities that usually produce the business's highest returns.

Leasing preserves capital and flexibility. A business can move, expand, or shrink more easily. The tradeoff is less control, exposure to rent increases, and no ownership benefit.

Structure and tax (PURPLE and GREEN) matter. Some owners hold property in a separate entity that leases it to the operating business. This can separate business risk from property risk and may have tax and estate planning benefits, depending on jurisdiction and professional advice.

Strategy should decide. If the business expects to stay in the location for many years and has surplus capital beyond its growth needs, ownership may make sense. If growth plans are uncertain or capital is limited, leasing may be wiser.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: The dental practice owner models both options. Buying the building would use most of the cash planned for the third location. The owner negotiates a ten-year lease renewal with a right of first refusal to buy the building later, protecting the option without committing capital now. The third location opens on schedule. Three years later, with strong cash flow, the owner buys the original building through a separate property entity on professional advice, leasing it back to the practice.

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** Capital tied up in property is unavailable for other investments.

**Q2.** Leasing keeps cash available and allows easier changes in space.

**Q3.** A separate entity can isolate risks and support planning goals, subject to professional advice.

**Q4.** Real estate decisions should serve the business strategy.

**Q5.** The right of first refusal preserved the opportunity without committing capital immediately.

## Key Takeaways
- Business real estate is both an operating need and an investment.
- Owning provides control and potential appreciation but uses capital and adds responsibility.
- Leasing preserves capital and flexibility.
- Let strategy, tenure, and capital needs decide, and seek professional advice on structure.
