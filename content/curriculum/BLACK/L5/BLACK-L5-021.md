# Technology Transformation: Orchestrating Investment, Change Management, and Operational Risk

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): A wholesale distributor buys an expensive new inventory and ordering system. Six months later, staff still keep paper records "just in case," order errors have increased, and the vendor blames the company's processes.

## Learning Objectives
- Explain why technology projects often fail at adoption rather than at installation.
- Identify the people, process, data, and risk elements of a technology change.
- Explain how to phase a technology change to protect operations.
- Apply an orchestrated plan for a technology transformation.

## Core Content
PRINCIPLE: Technology changes how people work. If the people and processes do not change with it, the technology rarely delivers its promised value. Level 4 explained that many change efforts fail at adoption; technology projects are a common example.

Leaders must coordinate four elements. People: users need to understand why the change matters, be trained properly, and have a voice in how the system is configured. Process: the system should support improved processes, not simply automate old, inefficient ones. Data: old information must be cleaned and moved accurately, or the new system starts with bad data. Risk: operations must continue during the transition, so fallback plans and testing are essential.

Investment includes more than the purchase price. Training time, temporary productivity loss, consultants, data migration, and ongoing support all belong in the budget. Underestimating these costs leads to rushed implementations.

Phasing reduces risk. A pilot with one team, location, or product line exposes problems while they are still small. Running old and new systems in parallel for a limited time can protect critical operations, though it requires extra effort. Clear success measures show when the organization is ready to move on.

Ownership matters. A project needs a leader inside the business — not only the vendor — who is accountable for results and empowered to make decisions.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: The distributor's owner resets the project. A respected warehouse supervisor becomes the internal project lead. The team maps how orders actually flow and removes three unnecessary approval steps before reconfiguring the system. Inventory data is cleaned and recounted. One warehouse switches fully while the second keeps the old process for six weeks. Staff receive hands-on training and a simple help guide. After the pilot meets its accuracy target, the second warehouse switches. Order errors drop below their original level.

## Practice Quiz
**Q1. Why do technology projects often fail?**
A) Software never works
B) Technology is always too cheap
C) People and processes do not change with the technology
D) Vendors never provide training

**Q2. Why should processes be improved before being automated?**
A) Processes never need improvement
B) Automating an inefficient process only makes inefficiency faster
C) Software automatically fixes every process
D) It is required by the vendor

**Q3. What costs belong in a technology budget besides the purchase price?**
A) Training, productivity loss, data migration, consultants, and ongoing support
B) None
C) Only the vendor's license fee
D) Only hardware

**Q4. Why is a pilot useful?**
A) It delays the project indefinitely
B) It eliminates the need for training
C) It guarantees success
D) It exposes problems while they are still small

**Q5. In the example, who led the reset project?**
A) The vendor
B) An outside consultant only
C) A respected warehouse supervisor inside the business
D) No one

## Answer Key
Q1: C
Q2: B
Q3: A
Q4: D
Q5: C

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** Value comes from changed behavior, not just installed software.

**Q2.** Automation magnifies whatever process it supports, good or bad.

**Q3.** The full cost includes the human and transition effort.

**Q4.** Small-scale testing reveals issues before they affect the whole business.

**Q5.** An internal owner with credibility drove adoption and accountability.

## Key Takeaways
- Technology delivers value only when people and processes change with it.
- Coordinate people, process, data, and risk.
- Budget for the full cost of change, not just the purchase price.
- Pilot, phase, and assign an internal owner accountable for results.
