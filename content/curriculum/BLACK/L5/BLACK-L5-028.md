# Diversification Versus Focus: Orchestrating Opportunity, Capability, and Capital

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): A successful coffee roaster supplies cafés and grocery stores. The owner is considering opening retail cafés, launching a line of tea products, and starting an online subscription service — all at the same time.

## Learning Objectives
- Explain the benefits and risks of diversification.
- Explain why focus often produces stronger results for smaller organizations.
- Identify the capability and capital questions that should guide expansion.
- Apply an orchestrated framework to prioritize new opportunities.

## Core Content
PRINCIPLE: Every new opportunity competes for the same limited money, management time, and attention. Diversification can reduce dependence on one market, but spreading resources too thinly can weaken the core business that funds everything else.

Diversification makes sense when new opportunities use existing strengths — customers, skills, brand, or distribution — and when the organization has enough capacity to support them. Related diversification, such as selling new products to existing customers, is usually less risky than unrelated diversification.

Focus is often powerful for smaller organizations. Becoming excellent in one area builds reputation, efficiency, and pricing power. Many companies grow faster by deepening their core than by entering several new markets at once.

Leaders should ask: How large is the opportunity? Does it use our existing strengths? Do we have the people and systems to execute? How much capital is required, and what return is likely? What happens to the core business if leadership attention shifts?

Sequencing is often the answer. Pursue one opportunity at a time, prove it, then add the next.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: The coffee roaster evaluates the three options. Retail cafés require large capital and new management skills. Tea products use existing grocery relationships but need new sourcing knowledge. The online subscription uses existing roasting capacity, brand, and customer interest, with modest investment. The owner launches the subscription first, sets a target for one year, and postpones the other ideas. The subscription succeeds and later provides the customer data used to test a single café location.

## Practice Quiz
**Q1. Why can diversification weaken a business?**
A) It always increases profits
B) It removes all risk
C) It is illegal for small businesses
D) It can spread money and management attention too thinly

**Q2. When does diversification usually make the most sense?**
A) When it is unrelated to anything the company knows
B) When the core business is struggling and cash is low
C) When it uses existing strengths and the organization has capacity
D) Always

**Q3. Why can focus be powerful for smaller organizations?**
A) It prevents all growth
B) It builds reputation, efficiency, and pricing power
C) It eliminates competition
D) It requires no investment

**Q4. What does sequencing mean in this context?**
A) Pursuing one opportunity at a time, proving it, then adding the next
B) Pursuing all opportunities simultaneously
C) Avoiding opportunities entirely
D) Choosing opportunities randomly

**Q5. Why did the roaster choose the subscription service first?**
A) It required the largest investment
B) It required new management skills
C) It was unrelated to coffee
D) It used existing strengths with modest investment

## Answer Key
Q1: D
Q2: C
Q3: B
Q4: A
Q5: D

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** Limited resources spread across many initiatives weaken all of them.

**Q2.** Using existing strengths reduces risk and increases the chance of success.

**Q3.** Excellence in one area creates advantages that are hard to copy.

**Q4.** Sequencing reduces risk and preserves focus.

**Q5.** It fit the company's strengths and capacity.

## Key Takeaways
- Every opportunity competes for limited resources.
- Diversify around existing strengths and available capacity.
- Focus often produces stronger results for smaller organizations.
- Sequence opportunities: prove one before adding the next.
