# Cash Flow Leadership: Orchestrating Working Capital, Payroll, Growth, and Reserves

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): A profitable construction subcontractor struggles to make payroll every few months because large clients pay slowly while materials and wages must be paid immediately.

## Learning Objectives
- Explain the difference between profit and cash flow.
- Identify the components of working capital.
- Explain how leaders forecast and manage cash.
- Apply an orchestrated cash flow plan.

## Core Content
PRINCIPLE: Profit shows whether a business earns more than it spends over time. Cash flow shows whether it has money available when bills are due. A profitable business can still fail if it runs out of cash.

Working capital includes money owed by customers (receivables), money owed to suppliers (payables), and inventory. Slow customer payments, large inventory, or short supplier terms can trap cash.

Leaders improve cash flow by invoicing quickly, setting clear payment terms, following up consistently on overdue accounts, negotiating supplier terms, reducing excess inventory, and requiring deposits for large projects.

Cash forecasting is essential. A rolling thirteen-week cash forecast helps identify shortages before they happen, allowing time to arrange financing, delay purchases, or accelerate collections.

Reserves provide resilience. Many organizations aim to keep enough cash to cover a set number of months of essential expenses, depending on risk and industry.

Growth consumes cash. Leaders must ensure expansion plans include enough working capital.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: The subcontractor introduces deposits for new projects, invoices weekly instead of monthly, and assigns one person to follow up on overdue accounts. The owner negotiates longer payment terms with a key supplier and creates a thirteen-week cash forecast reviewed every Monday. A small credit line is arranged before it is needed. Payroll stress disappears within three months.

**Q3. Why is a thirteen-week cash forecast useful?**
A) It replaces accounting records
B) It eliminates all risk

**Q4. Why does growth consume cash?**
A) Growth always produces immediate cash
B) Customers pay in advance automatically
C) Expenses and investments often occur before customers pay
D) Growth eliminates expenses

**Q5. In the example, which action improved collections?**
A) Ignoring overdue invoices
B) Invoicing weekly and following up on overdue accounts
C) Removing payment terms
D) Paying suppliers early

## Answer Key
Q1: B
Q2: A
Q3: D
Q4: C
Q5: B

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** Revenue may be earned before cash is received.

**Q2.** These items determine how much cash is tied up in operations.

**Q3.** Early warning creates time to respond.

**Q4.** Growth increases spending before collections catch up.

**Q5.** Faster invoicing and follow-up accelerate cash inflows.

## Key Takeaways
- Cash flow determines survival; profit alone is not enough.
- Manage receivables, payables, and inventory deliberately.
- Use rolling cash forecasts to anticipate shortages.
- Maintain reserves and plan for the cash needs of growth.
