# Building the Executive Team: Orchestrating Complementary Skills, Trust, and Decision Rights

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): A founder has hired three senior leaders, but meetings are filled with unclear responsibilities, repeated debates, and decisions that are later reversed.

## Learning Objectives
- Explain why executive teams need complementary skills.
- Explain the importance of clear decision rights.
- Explain how trust supports productive disagreement.
- Apply an orchestrated approach to strengthening an executive team.

## Core Content
PRINCIPLE: An executive team is more than a group of skilled individuals. Its effectiveness depends on how members work together.

Complementary skills matter. A strong team combines strategic thinking, operational execution, financial discipline, sales and customer understanding, and people leadership.

Decision rights must be clear. Team members should know which decisions they own, which require consultation, and which belong to the chief executive or board.

Trust allows productive disagreement. Leaders who trust each other can challenge ideas without personal conflict.

Shared accountability matters. Executives should own both their functions and the organization's overall success.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: The founder holds a planning session to define responsibilities and decision rights. The team agrees on a monthly scorecard and adopts a rule that disagreements are discussed openly during meetings, but decisions are supported publicly once made. Reversed decisions decline and execution improves.

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** Diverse strengths improve decisions.

**Q2.** Clear decision rights reduce confusion.

**Q3.** Trust supports honest debate.

**Q4.** Executives must think beyond their departments.

**Q5.** Open debate followed by unified support improved execution.

## Key Takeaways
- Build executive teams with complementary skills.
- Define decision rights clearly.
- Use trust to support constructive disagreement.
- Share accountability for organizational success.
