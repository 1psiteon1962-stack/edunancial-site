## Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Define gross margin, operating margin, and net margin
- Calculate each margin from basic financial data
- Identify the cost categories that affect each margin level
- Explain why two businesses with the same revenue can have very different financial health
- Apply margin analysis to evaluate a business's financial performance

## Core Content

### Why Revenue Alone Tells You Nothing

A business with $2 million in revenue sounds more successful than a business with $400,000 in revenue. But if the first business spends $1.95 million to generate that revenue, it earns $50,000. The second business spends $200,000 and earns $200,000.

Revenue is vanity. Margin is reality.

The three profit margin metrics provide a structured view of where money is earned and lost as revenue flows through the business.

### Gross Margin — The First Filter

**Gross margin** measures how much revenue remains after the direct costs of producing and delivering the product or service.

**Gross margin = (Revenue − Cost of Goods Sold) ÷ Revenue × 100**

Cost of goods sold (COGS) includes:
- Raw materials and components
- Direct labor used to produce the product or deliver the service
- Manufacturing overhead directly tied to production

What gross margin is NOT:
- Rent for the head office
- Marketing expenses
- Management salaries
- Interest on loans

Example: A business earns $500,000 in revenue. Its COGS — materials, direct labor, and production overhead — total $300,000. Gross margin = ($500,000 − $300,000) ÷ $500,000 = 40%.

Gross margin benchmarks vary widely by industry. Software can run 70–80%. Construction often runs 15–25%. Retail averages 25–50%. Comparing your gross margin to industry norms is the starting point for cost analysis.

### Operating Margin — After Running the Business

**Operating margin** measures how much remains after paying all operating expenses — including overhead costs not captured in COGS.

**Operating margin = Operating profit ÷ Revenue × 100**

Operating expenses include:
- Rent and facilities
- Marketing and advertising
- Management and administrative salaries
- Research and development
- Technology and software subscriptions

Operating profit (also called EBIT — earnings before interest and taxes) reflects the true profitability of the core business, before financing costs and taxes.

Example: The same business from above has $500,000 in revenue, $300,000 in COGS, and $150,000 in operating expenses. Operating profit = $500,000 − $300,000 − $150,000 = $50,000. Operating margin = 10%.

### Net Margin — What the Owner Actually Keeps

**Net margin** measures how much of each dollar of revenue becomes profit after all costs — including interest on debt and taxes.

**Net margin = Net profit ÷ Revenue × 100**

Net profit is what flows to the owner after the government and lenders take their share. It is the ultimate measure of a business as an income-generating asset.

Example: From the $50,000 operating profit above, the business pays $5,000 in interest on a loan and $12,000 in taxes. Net profit = $33,000. Net margin = $33,000 ÷ $500,000 = 6.6%.

### The Margin Stack

Viewing all three margins together reveals where value is created and destroyed:

| Metric | Amount | Margin |
|--------|--------|--------|
| Revenue | $500,000 | 100% |
| Cost of Goods Sold | $(300,000) | |
| **Gross Profit** | **$200,000** | **40%** |
| Operating Expenses | $(150,000) | |
| **Operating Profit** | **$50,000** | **10%** |
| Interest + Taxes | $(17,000) | |
| **Net Profit** | **$33,000** | **6.6%** |

This stack tells you immediately where to focus. If gross margin is healthy but operating margin is low, overhead costs are the problem. If operating margin is healthy but net margin is low, debt or tax structure needs attention.

### Margin Is Not Fixed — It Is Managed

Margins can be improved through deliberate action:

**Improving gross margin:**
- Raise prices without proportionate cost increases
- Reduce material costs through supplier negotiations or substitution
- Improve labor efficiency through training or process redesign
- Eliminate low-margin products or services from the offering

**Improving operating margin:**
- Reduce overhead by consolidating functions, renegotiating leases, or eliminating unused subscriptions
- Increase revenue without increasing overhead (operating leverage)
- Automate repetitive administrative tasks

**Improving net margin:**
- Refinance high-interest debt
- Structure the business to take advantage of legitimate tax deductions
- Reinvest profit in growth rather than distributing it in ways that trigger tax events

1. Estimate the gross margin for the business you identified in earlier lessons. Write the calculation: (Revenue − Direct Costs) ÷ Revenue.
