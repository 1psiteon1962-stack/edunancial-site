## Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Explain why pricing is a financial decision, not a marketing decision
- Identify the four primary pricing strategies and when to use each
- Calculate the minimum viable price required to achieve a target profit
- Recognize common pricing mistakes and their financial consequences
- Build a pricing model for a product or service

## Core Content

### Pricing Is a Financial Decision

Most business operators approach pricing as a marketing question: What will customers pay? What is the competition charging?

These are relevant inputs — but the primary question is financial: What price produces the profit this business needs to survive and grow?

A price set below the minimum viable level will destroy the business regardless of how many units it sells. Selling more of a product that loses money at each sale accelerates the path to failure.

### The Mathematics of Pricing

The relationship between price and profit is not linear — it is leveraged.

Example: A product costs $70 to produce and sells for $100. Gross margin is 30%.

| Scenario | Price | COGS | Gross Profit per Unit | Margin |
|----------|-------|------|-----------------------|--------|
| Current | $100 | $70 | $30 | 30% |
| +10% price | $110 | $70 | $40 | 36% |
| −10% price | $90 | $70 | $20 | 22% |

A 10% price increase produces a 33% increase in gross profit per unit. A 10% price cut reduces gross profit by 33%. Pricing decisions have outsized effects on profitability.

### The Four Primary Pricing Strategies

**1. Cost-Plus Pricing**
Add a markup percentage to the total cost of producing the product or service.

Formula: Price = Cost × (1 + Markup %)

Simple and ensures cost recovery. The weakness: it ignores customer value and competitive dynamics. You may be leaving money on the table or pricing based on cost inefficiencies you should instead eliminate.

**2. Value-Based Pricing**
Set the price based on the value the product delivers to the customer, not the cost of producing it.

A software tool that saves a business 10 hours per week at a labor cost of $50/hour creates $500/week in value. A $200/month subscription price is a small fraction of the value created — and the customer will pay it readily.

Value-based pricing requires understanding the customer's problem, the cost of that problem, and the value of solving it. It typically produces higher margins than cost-plus pricing.

**3. Competitive Pricing**
Set prices relative to competitors — at a premium, at parity, or at a discount.

Premium pricing positions the product as superior and commands higher margin. Discount pricing aims for volume. Parity pricing is the default for undifferentiated commodities.

The risk of competitive pricing: it anchors your price to a competitor's economics, which you do not understand. Competing on price alone leads to margin erosion for all participants.

**4. Tiered / Bundle Pricing**
Offer multiple versions at different price points — a basic tier, a standard tier, and a premium tier.

Tiered pricing serves different customer segments, maximizes revenue from high-value customers, and provides an entry point for price-sensitive customers. It also makes the middle tier appear as the "value" choice, increasing average transaction value.

### Calculating Minimum Viable Price

Before choosing a pricing strategy, calculate the minimum price at which the business can sustain operations:

**Minimum viable price = (Fixed costs + Variable costs + Target profit) ÷ Units sold**

Example:
- Fixed costs (rent, salaries, software): $20,000/month
- Variable costs per unit: $30
- Target monthly profit: $10,000
- Expected unit sales: 500 units

Minimum viable price = ($20,000 + (500 × $30) + $10,000) ÷ 500
= ($20,000 + $15,000 + $10,000) ÷ 500
= $45,000 ÷ 500
= $90 per unit

Any price below $90 will fail to cover costs and profit targets at 500 units.

### The Most Common Pricing Mistakes

**Underpricing to win customers:** Attracting customers with unsustainable prices creates a business built on customers who will leave when prices rise.

**Cost-plus pricing without understanding value:** If your costs are high due to inefficiency, cost-plus pricing embeds those inefficiencies into your price.

**Matching the cheapest competitor:** There is always someone willing to lose money faster than you. Price competition with the cheapest provider destroys the market for everyone.

**Never raising prices:** Inflation, rising costs, and increasing value all justify regular price reviews. A business that has not raised prices in three years has given customers a compounding discount.

**Discounting without understanding margin impact:** A 20% discount on a 25% margin product eliminates almost all profit. Know your numbers before offering discounts.
