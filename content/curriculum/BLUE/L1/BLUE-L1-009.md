## Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Identify the five primary categories of business risk
- Evaluate each risk by likelihood and financial impact
- Explain the financial practices that build business resilience
- Calculate the minimum cash reserve a business should maintain
- Apply a risk mitigation framework to a specific business situation

## Core Content

### Why Business Risk Is a Financial Subject

Risk in business is not an abstract concept — it is a financial reality with measurable consequences.

Every risk, if it materializes, either reduces revenue, increases costs, or both. A business without a framework for identifying and managing risk will eventually encounter a disruption it cannot absorb.

Financial resilience — the ability to survive setbacks without catastrophic loss — is as important as profit optimization. A business that earns high margins but carries no cash reserve is more fragile than a lower-margin business with six months of expenses in reserve.

### The Five Primary Categories of Business Risk

**1. Revenue Concentration Risk**
When a business depends on a small number of customers for a large share of its revenue, losing one customer can cause serious financial harm.

Warning signal: One customer accounts for more than 20% of total revenue.

Mitigation: Diversify the customer base. Set a strategic limit on the percentage any single customer can represent.

**2. Market Risk**
Demand for the business's product or service changes due to external factors — economic conditions, competitive entry, regulatory change, technology disruption.

Mitigation: Monitor industry trends. Maintain flexibility in the product or service offering. Avoid capital-intensive commitments based on single-scenario demand forecasts.

**3. Operational Risk**
A failure in people, processes, or technology that disrupts the business's ability to deliver to customers.

Examples: key employee departure, equipment failure, data breach, supplier disruption.

Mitigation: Document processes so knowledge is not confined to individuals. Maintain backup suppliers. Invest in cybersecurity and data backup. Cross-train employees in critical functions.

**4. Financial Risk**
The risk that the business cannot meet its financial obligations — loan payments, payroll, rent — due to insufficient cash flow or excessive debt.

Mitigation: Maintain a minimum cash reserve. Limit debt to amounts serviceable from conservative revenue projections. Monitor cash flow weekly.

**5. Legal and Regulatory Risk**
The risk of financial loss from lawsuits, regulatory penalties, tax non-compliance, or contractual disputes.

Mitigation: Use clear, well-drafted contracts for customers and suppliers. Maintain required licenses and insurance. Consult qualified legal and tax advisors before entering significant transactions.

### Evaluating Risk — Likelihood and Impact

Not all risks deserve equal attention. Evaluate each risk on two dimensions:

**Likelihood:** How probable is this risk materializing within the next 12 months?
- High (more than 50% probability)
- Medium (10–50% probability)
- Low (less than 10% probability)

**Financial impact:** If this risk materializes, what is the estimated financial cost?
- Severe (threatens business survival or causes more than 30 days of operational disruption)
- Moderate (requires meaningful financial resources to resolve; less than 30 days disruption)
- Minor (absorbed within normal operating cash flow)

Prioritize mitigation for risks that are both high likelihood and high impact. Accept risks that are low impact and low likelihood. Insurance and contingency planning address risks that are low likelihood but high impact.

### The Minimum Cash Reserve

The most fundamental financial resilience practice is maintaining a cash reserve sufficient to survive a revenue interruption.

**Minimum cash reserve = Monthly fixed operating expenses × 3 (or up to 6 for higher-risk businesses)**

Monthly fixed operating expenses include:
- Payroll for employees who cannot immediately be reduced
- Rent and lease obligations
- Debt service (loan minimum payments)
- Insurance and essential subscriptions
- Utilities and facilities costs

A business that consistently draws its cash balance to near zero has no capacity to absorb an unexpected cost or a slow revenue month. A single bad month can trigger a chain reaction: late vendor payments, damaged supplier relationships, inability to make payroll, and customer dissatisfaction from delayed delivery.

### Building Financial Resilience

Financial resilience is built deliberately:

1. **Maintain the cash reserve** — treat it as non-negotiable. Fund it before distributions to owners.
2. **Diversify revenue sources** — no single customer, channel, or product should be existential.
3. **Carry appropriate insurance** — general liability, professional liability, property, and key-person life insurance as appropriate to the business.
4. **Document critical processes** — so key employee departure does not destroy operational capacity.
5. **Review risk quarterly** — the business's risk profile changes as it grows. Risks that were minor at $200,000 in revenue may be critical at $2,000,000.

Resilience is not a one-time achievement. It requires ongoing attention as the business changes.

## Application

Before proceeding to the next lesson, complete the following:

1. List three risks that could materially harm the business you have been analyzing. Classify each as high, medium, or low likelihood and severe, moderate, or minor impact.
