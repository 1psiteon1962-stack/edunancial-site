## Core Content

### Topic 1: What Inventory Is
Inventory refers to the goods a business holds for the purpose of selling to customers — raw materials, work-in-progress, or finished products waiting to be sold. On a balance sheet (BLUE-L1-025), inventory counts as an asset.

### Topic 2: The Obvious Cost — Purchase Price
The most visible cost of inventory is simply what it cost to buy or produce it. This is the number most new business owners think about first.

### Topic 3: The Hidden Costs — Holding Costs
[ESTIMATE, ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] Beyond purchase price, inventory carries "holding costs": storage space (rent for the space it occupies), insurance, risk of damage or spoilage, risk of becoming outdated or unsellable, and the opportunity cost of cash tied up in unsold goods that could have been used elsewhere in the business.

### Topic 4: Why Excess Inventory Can Hurt Even Profitable Businesses
[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A business that overstocks inventory can find its cash tied up in unsold goods, directly connecting back to the cash flow risk from BLUE-L1-016 — a profitable-looking business can still struggle to pay bills if too much cash is locked into inventory sitting on shelves.

---

## Key Definitions
- **Inventory:** Goods a business holds for the purpose of selling to customers, including raw materials, work-in-progress, and finished products.
- **Holding Cost:** The ongoing cost of storing and maintaining inventory, beyond its original purchase price — including storage, insurance, risk of loss, and opportunity cost of tied-up cash.

## Worked Examples
[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A retailer buys $20,000 of seasonal merchandise that doesn't sell as quickly as expected. Beyond the $20,000 purchase cost, the retailer pays ongoing storage costs, and that $20,000 in cash isn't available to buy new, faster-selling inventory — a real, compounding cost beyond the sticker price.

## Common Mistakes
- Thinking of inventory cost as only the purchase price
- Overstocking inventory in anticipation of demand that doesn't materialize
- Ignoring the cash flow impact of cash tied up in unsold goods

## FAQ
**Q: Is holding some inventory always bad?**
A: [OPINION] No — having enough inventory to meet demand is necessary for many businesses; the concern is specifically about holding more than needed, not inventory itself.

**Q: How can a business reduce holding costs?**
A: [ESTIMATE] Common approaches include more accurate demand forecasting, ordering smaller quantities more frequently, and improving inventory turnover — deeper inventory management strategies are covered in later lessons.

## Key Takeaways
- Inventory is goods held for sale, counted as an asset on the balance sheet
- Purchase price is only the most visible cost — holding costs add up beyond that
- Holding costs include storage, insurance, risk of loss, and opportunity cost of tied-up cash
- Excess inventory can create real cash flow problems even in a profitable-looking business

Explain, referencing BLUE-L1-016, how too much inventory could create a cash flow problem even for a business that eventually sells everything at a profit.
