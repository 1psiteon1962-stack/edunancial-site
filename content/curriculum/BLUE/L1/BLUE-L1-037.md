## Executive Summary

Market research doesn't require a big budget or a formal study — it requires genuine curiosity about whether people actually want what's being offered. This lesson explains what market research actually involves and practical, low-cost ways a new business can still do it.

## Core Content

### Topic 1: What Market Research Actually Involves
Market research is the process of gathering information about a target market's needs, preferences, and behavior — to test whether a business idea actually solves a real problem people will pay for, rather than assuming it does.

### Topic 2: Low-Cost Method One — Direct Conversations
[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] Simply talking directly to potential customers — asking what problems they currently face, what they've tried, and what they'd value — costs nothing but time and can reveal more than an expensive formal study, if done honestly and without leading questions.

### Topic 3: Low-Cost Method Two — Observing Existing Behavior
Looking at how people currently solve the problem a business hopes to address — what they currently pay for, what complaints show up in reviews of existing options — reveals real behavior, which is often more reliable than what people say they'd do hypothetically.

### Topic 4: Low-Cost Method Three — Small-Scale Testing
[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] Offering a small, low-cost version of a product or service to a limited group before fully launching — sometimes called a "pilot" — tests real willingness to pay, rather than just interest or opinion.

---

## Key Definitions
- **Market Research:** The process of gathering information about a target market's needs, preferences, and behavior to test a business idea's viability.
- **Pilot:** A small-scale test of a product or service offered to a limited group before a full launch.

## Worked Examples
[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] Before fully launching a meal-prep delivery service, an entrepreneur offers a small trial to 10 neighbors at a modest price, observing whether they actually pay and reorder — real behavior, gathered at minimal cost, rather than guessing based on assumptions alone.

## Common Mistakes
- Assuming market research requires an expensive formal study to be valid
- Asking leading questions that produce falsely positive feedback
- Relying only on what people say they'd do, rather than observing what they actually do

## FAQ
**Q: Is it ever acceptable to skip market research entirely?**
A: [OPINION] Skipping it entirely carries real risk — building a product before confirming genuine demand is one of the most common reasons new businesses struggle, even with an otherwise reasonable idea.

**Q: What's the difference between what people say and what they do?**
A: People often express polite interest that doesn't reflect actual willingness to pay — observing real behavior (Topic 3) or testing real payment (Topic 4) tends to reveal more reliable information than opinions alone.

## Key Takeaways
- Market research gathers real information to test whether an idea solves a genuine, paid-for problem
- Direct conversations with potential customers cost nothing but time
- Observing existing behavior reveals more than hypothetical opinions
- Small-scale testing (a pilot) tests real willingness to pay before a full launch

## Practical Exercise
Design a simple, low-cost market research plan (using at least two of the three methods from this lesson) for a hypothetical business idea of your choosing.
