## Main Lesson

### Topic 1: What an Employee Is
An employee typically works under the business's direct control — set hours, specific methods dictated by the employer, and ongoing, integrated involvement in the business's operations.

### Topic 2: What an Independent Contractor Is
An independent contractor typically operates with more independence — controlling how and when the work gets done, often working for multiple clients, and providing their own tools or equipment, delivering a specific result rather than ongoing, controlled labor.

### Topic 3: Why the Label Alone Doesn't Decide It
[REGION-SPECIFIC RULE] Many jurisdictions look at the actual working relationship — degree of control, independence, and integration into the business — rather than simply accepting whatever label a business assigns. Calling someone a "contractor" doesn't make them one if the actual relationship looks like employment.

### Topic 4: Why Misclassification Is a Real Risk
[REGION-SPECIFIC RULE] Misclassifying an employee as a contractor can expose a business to back taxes, penalties, and legal liability if the classification is challenged and found incorrect — a risk businesses sometimes underestimate when trying to reduce costs or paperwork.

## Key Definitions
- **Employee:** A worker under the business's direct control regarding hours, methods, and ongoing integration into operations.
- **Independent Contractor:** A worker who controls how and when work is completed, often serves multiple clients, and delivers a specific result rather than ongoing controlled labor.
- **Misclassification:** Incorrectly labeling a worker as a contractor or employee, inconsistent with the actual nature of the working relationship.

## Worked Examples
[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A business calls a worker a "contractor" but sets their exact daily hours, dictates precisely how tasks must be done, and requires them to work exclusively for that one business — this actual relationship may legally resemble employment despite the label used.

## Common Mistakes
- Assuming the label used in an agreement automatically determines legal classification
- Misclassifying workers to avoid payroll taxes or benefits obligations
- Not understanding that classification rules vary by jurisdiction

## FAQ
**Q: Can a business simply choose to call a worker a contractor to avoid employee obligations?**
A: [REGION-SPECIFIC RULE] Not reliably — many jurisdictions evaluate the actual working relationship, not just the label, specifically to prevent this kind of intentional misclassification.

**Q: What determines the classification in practice?**
A: [REGION-SPECIFIC RULE] Factors commonly considered include degree of control over work methods/hours, whether the worker serves multiple clients, who provides tools/equipment, and how integrated the worker is into ongoing business operations — specific legal tests vary by jurisdiction.

## Key Takeaways
- Employees typically work under direct business control; contractors typically control their own methods and schedule
- The label used in an agreement doesn't automatically determine legal classification
- Many jurisdictions evaluate the actual working relationship instead
- Misclassification carries real risk, including back taxes and penalties
