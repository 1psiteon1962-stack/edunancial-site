## BLUE-L2-002
### Choosing a Business Structure: Sole Proprietor, Partnership, LLC, Corporation

**1. Learning Objectives**
By the end of this lesson, the learner will be able to:
- Describe the basic structure of a sole proprietorship, partnership, LLC, and corporation.
- Compare general liability and tax treatment across these structures.
- Recognize entity choice as jurisdiction-specific and requiring professional guidance.

**2. Core Content**
A sole proprietorship is the default structure for an individual running a business without formally registering another entity type — simple to start, but generally offering no separation between personal and business liability. A partnership involves two or more owners sharing a business, with liability and tax treatment varying by partnership type (general versus limited) and jurisdiction. A Limited Liability Company (LLC) is a commonly used structure, echoing the RED-track LLC discussion (RED-L2-012), generally offering liability protection separating personal assets from business obligations when properly formed and maintained, with flexible tax treatment options depending on jurisdiction and elections made. A corporation is a more formal structure, generally offering liability protection and a distinct tax treatment, with additional formalities (such as a board and required record-keeping) beyond what an LLC typically requires.

Each structure represents a different tradeoff between simplicity, liability protection, tax treatment, and formality requirements. As with the RED-track entity discussion, this is a REGION-SPECIFIC and highly individualized decision requiring a business attorney and accountant, since rules, available structures, and tax elections vary significantly by jurisdiction and change over time.

**3. Worked Example**
Comparing structures before launching her consulting business, Yolanda considers a sole proprietorship (simplest to start, but no liability separation) against an LLC (added liability protection, moderate formality, flexible tax treatment). Given that her consulting work carries some professional liability risk, she consults an attorney and accountant, who help her form an LLC properly, including opening a separate business bank account and establishing appropriate operating documents from the outset, rather than starting as a sole proprietor and converting later.

**4. Practice Quiz**
1. What is a sole proprietorship's general liability characteristic?
   a) Complete separation from personal liability
   b) Generally no separation between personal and business liability
   c) Guaranteed corporate tax treatment
   d) Requires a board of directors
2. What does an LLC generally offer, when properly formed and maintained?
   a) No liability protection whatsoever
   b) Liability protection separating personal assets from business obligations
   c) Mandatory public stock offerings
   d) Automatic corporate tax status in every jurisdiction
3. True or False: Entity structure rules and available options are identical in every jurisdiction.
4. Why did Yolanda choose to form an LLC from the start rather than beginning as a sole proprietor?
   a) She had no liability concerns
   b) Her professional liability risk made the added protection worth the added formality
   c) LLCs are always free to form
   d) It was the only legal option available to her

**5. Answer Key**
1. b  2. b  3. False  4. b

**6. Answers and Explanations**
1. Sole proprietorships generally offer no separation between personal and business liability.
2. LLCs, when properly formed and maintained, generally offer liability protection separating personal and business assets.
3. Entity structure rules vary significantly by jurisdiction, requiring professional guidance.
4. Yolanda's specific liability exposure in consulting work justified the added protection and formality of an LLC from the outset.

**7. Key Takeaways**
- Sole proprietorships, partnerships, LLCs, and corporations differ in liability protection, tax treatment, and formality.
- LLCs generally offer liability protection with more flexibility than corporations, when properly maintained.
- This decision echoes the RED-track entity discussion and is equally jurisdiction-specific.
- A business attorney and accountant should guide entity selection based on the specific business and its risks.
