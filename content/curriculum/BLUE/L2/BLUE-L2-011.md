## BLUE-L2-011
### Cash Flow vs. Profit: Why a Profitable Business Can Still Fail

**1. Learning Objectives**
By the end of this lesson, the learner will be able to:
- Distinguish cash flow from profit at a conceptual level.
- Explain why a profitable business can still fail from a cash flow problem.
- Identify common causes of this mismatch.

**2. Core Content**
Profit is the difference between total revenue and total expenses over a period, an accounting measure that does not necessarily reflect when cash actually moves in and out of a business. Cash flow refers to the actual timing of cash moving in and out — a business can be profitable on paper (having earned more than it spent) while simultaneously running out of actual cash to pay its bills, if the timing of收入 and expenses does not align.

Common causes of this mismatch include slow-paying customers (revenue is "earned" and recorded, but the cash has not actually arrived yet), large upfront inventory purchases (cash goes out well before the resulting sales bring cash back in), and rapid growth itself (a growing business often needs to spend more on inventory, staff, or equipment before the increased revenue from that growth actually arrives). This is one of the most common and financially fatal misunderstandings among new business owners, echoing the RED-track cash flow discussion (RED-L2-006) but specific to the unique timing patterns of operating a business.

**3. Worked Example**
Growing quickly, a small business owner reviews her books and sees a healthy profit for the quarter — revenue clearly exceeds expenses on paper. Yet she struggles to pay her upcoming supplier invoice, because a large portion of that "profit" exists as unpaid customer invoices (money owed to her, but not yet received) rather than cash actually sitting in her bank account. Recognizing this mismatch, she begins tracking cash flow separately from profit going forward, and starts requiring faster payment terms from slower-paying customers to align her actual cash timing more closely with her paper profitability.

**5. Answer Key**
1. b  2. b  3. False  4. b

**6. Answers and Explanations**
1. Profit is an accounting measure of revenue minus expenses; cash flow reflects the actual timing of cash movement.
2. Slow-paying customers, large upfront purchases, and rapid growth are common causes of profitable businesses running short on cash.
3. A business can be profitable on paper while genuinely running out of actual cash due to timing mismatches.
4. The owner addressed the mismatch by tracking cash flow separately and tightening customer payment terms.

**7. Key Takeaways**
- Profit measures revenue minus expenses on paper; cash flow measures the actual timing of cash movement.
- A profitable business can still fail if it runs out of actual cash due to timing mismatches.
- Slow-paying customers, upfront inventory costs, and rapid growth are common causes of this mismatch.
- Tracking cash flow separately from profit is essential, echoing the RED-track cash flow discussion in a business context.
