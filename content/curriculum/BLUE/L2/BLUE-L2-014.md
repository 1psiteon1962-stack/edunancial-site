## BLUE-L2-014
### How Pricing Changes Revenue, Margin, and Customer Acquisition

**1. Learning Objectives**
By the end of this lesson, the learner will be able to:
- Trace how a pricing change ripples through revenue, margin, and customer acquisition together.
- Explain why raising or lowering price is not a simple, isolated decision.
- Apply this multi-piece thinking to a hypothetical pricing change.

**2. Core Content**
Building on Lesson 13, this lesson applies Level 2 "what else does this move affect" thinking directly to pricing. Raising price generally increases margin per sale, but can reduce total sales volume if some customers are no longer willing to pay the higher price, meaning total revenue's direction depends on which effect dominates — margin increase or volume decrease. Lowering price generally increases sales volume (assuming demand responds to lower price) but reduces margin per sale, with total revenue again depending on which effect dominates.

Customer acquisition is affected too: pricing changes can shift which customer segment a business attracts — a higher price can position a business as premium, potentially attracting a different, sometimes more loyal or higher-spending customer base, while a lower price can attract more price-sensitive customers who may be more likely to switch to a competitor over a small future price difference. This is why a pricing decision should never be evaluated purely on its immediate effect on a single sale, but on its full ripple effect across volume, margin, total revenue, and the type of customer it attracts.

**3. Worked Example**
Considering a price increase for her cupcake business (Lesson 12), Marisol models several scenarios: if she raises her price and loses only a small percentage of her sales volume, her total revenue and margin both improve. If she raises her price and loses a larger percentage of her volume than anticipated, her total revenue could actually decline despite the higher per-unit margin. She also considers how a higher price might shift her customer base toward those seeking a premium product experience, potentially changing her marketing approach and target customer profile going forward, not just her bottom line for the current month.

**4. Practice Quiz**
1. What does raising price generally do to margin per sale, all else equal?
   a) Decreases it
   b) Generally increases it
   c) Has no effect
   d) Eliminates it entirely
2. Why doesn't a pricing decision only affect a single sale in isolation?
   a) Pricing has no broader effects
   b) It ripples through total volume, total revenue, margin, and the type of customer attracted
   c) Only volume is ever affected
   d) Only margin is ever affected
3. True or False: Raising price always guarantees higher total revenue, regardless of the effect on sales volume.
4. What did Marisol consider beyond the immediate per-unit margin effect of a price increase?
   a) Nothing else mattered
   b) How volume changes and shifts in her customer base could affect total revenue and positioning
   c) Only her own personal preference
   d) The price increase had no other effects

**5. Answer Key**
1. b  2. b  3. False  4. b

**6. Answers and Explanations**
1. Raising price generally increases margin per sale, all else being equal.
2. Pricing decisions ripple through volume, total revenue, margin, and customer base composition together.
3. Total revenue depends on the balance between margin increase and any resulting volume decrease, not price alone.
4. Marisol considered the full ripple effect, including potential volume changes and shifts in her customer base.

**7. Key Takeaways**
- Pricing changes ripple through sales volume, margin, total revenue, and customer base composition together.
- Raising price generally increases margin but may reduce volume; the net effect on total revenue depends on which dominates.
- Pricing can also shift which customer segment a business attracts, affecting positioning beyond the immediate sale.
- A pricing decision should be evaluated on its full ripple effect, not a single isolated metric.
