# Unit Economics Analysis: Why Profitable Growth Requires Positive Per-Unit Margins

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a large trading bloc suddenly imposing steep tariffs on a broad category of imported manufactured goods, raising costs for companies that depend on those imports as inputs. As tariffs raise input costs across an industry, some businesses discover their per-unit profit was thinner than they realized, even as total revenue kept climbing. This lesson explains why looking at the per-unit picture, not just the top-line trend, is the real test of a sustainable business.

## Learning Objectives
- Explain what unit economics means.
- Calculate a basic per-unit contribution margin.
- Explain why growing revenue with negative unit economics is not sustainable growth.
- Apply unit economics thinking to evaluate whether a business model can scale profitably.

## Core Content
Unit economics refers to the direct revenues and costs associated with a single unit of a business's product or service — for example, one customer, one subscription, or one item sold — isolated from the company's fixed overhead costs. VERIFIED FACT: the core building block is contribution margin, calculated as the revenue from one unit minus the variable costs directly attributable to producing and delivering that one unit, which shows how much each additional unit sold contributes toward covering the business's fixed costs and, eventually, profit.

Here is the "why" this per-unit view matters more than total revenue growth alone, especially relevant when input costs rise unpredictably as in the tariff scenario above: a business can show impressive, rapidly growing total revenue while actually losing more money with every additional unit sold, if the variable cost of producing each unit exceeds the revenue that unit generates. In that situation, growth does not solve the underlying problem — it makes it worse, since each new sale adds to the loss rather than moving the business toward profitability, a trap sometimes summarized as "losing money on every unit but making it up in volume," which does not actually work.

This is why sound unit economics is considered a foundational prerequisite for scalable growth, distinct from overall company profitability (which also depends on fixed costs and total volume): a business with strong, positive unit economics can generally reach overall profitability simply by growing enough to cover its fixed costs, while a business with negative unit economics cannot reach profitability through growth alone — it must first fix the underlying per-unit cost or pricing problem, regardless of how large it eventually becomes.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: A company sells a subscription box for $40, with $45 in variable costs (materials, packaging, and shipping, inflated by new tariffs on imported components) to produce and deliver each box. Each unit sold loses the company $5, meaning that every additional customer, however impressive the resulting revenue growth looks, makes the company's total losses larger, not smaller. Until the company either raises its price, redesigns the product to reduce input costs, or finds a cheaper, non-tariffed supply source, aggressive growth in customer count will only accelerate the company's cash losses rather than move it toward profitability.

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** Unit economics isolates the per-unit financial picture, separate from the business's broader fixed cost structure.

**Q2.** Contribution margin isolates how much each individual unit contributes toward covering fixed costs and eventual profit.

**Q3.** Negative unit economics means growth compounds losses rather than resolving them, since each unit sold loses money.

**Q4.** Positive unit economics means volume alone can eventually produce profitability, while negative unit economics requires fixing the underlying cost or pricing structure first.

**Q5.** Since each unit sold loses $5, scaling the number of units sold directly scales up the company's total losses rather than resolving them.

## Key Takeaways
- Unit economics isolates the revenue and costs of a single unit, distinct from a business's overall fixed cost structure.
- Contribution margin (unit revenue minus unit variable cost) shows how much each unit contributes toward fixed costs and profit.
- Growing revenue with negative unit economics increases losses rather than solving the underlying profitability problem.
- Positive unit economics is a prerequisite for growth to eventually produce profitability; negative unit economics requires a structural fix first.
