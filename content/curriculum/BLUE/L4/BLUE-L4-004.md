# Break-Even Analysis: Why Fixed and Variable Costs Determine the Point of Profitability

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a central bank raising its benchmark interest rate sharply and repeatedly over a short stretch of months to fight rising inflation. As borrowing costs rise sharply, a small business owner financing new equipment with a loan needs to know exactly how many units they must sell each month just to cover the loan payment and other fixed costs. This lesson introduces the calculation built for exactly that question.

## Learning Objectives
- Distinguish between fixed costs and variable costs in a business.
- Calculate a business's break-even point in units.
- Explain how a change in fixed costs, variable costs, or price affects the break-even point.
- Apply break-even analysis to evaluate the impact of a new fixed cost, such as a loan payment.

## Core Content
Fixed costs are business expenses that remain the same regardless of how many units are sold (such as rent, insurance, and, notably in the scenario above, a fixed loan payment), while variable costs change directly with the number of units produced or sold (such as materials and direct labor per unit). VERIFIED FACT: break-even point in units is calculated as total fixed costs divided by contribution margin per unit (price per unit minus variable cost per unit, echoing Lesson 1's unit economics concept) — the number of units a business must sell to generate enough total contribution margin to exactly cover its fixed costs, at which point the business has zero profit and zero loss.

Here is the "why" this calculation directly answers the business owner's question in the scenario above: taking on a new fixed cost, such as a loan payment for equipment, raises total fixed costs, which mechanically raises the break-even point — the business must now sell more units each month than before just to reach the same zero-profit starting line, before any actual profit can be generated. Understanding this new, higher break-even point before taking on the debt allows the business owner to realistically assess whether their expected sales volume can comfortably clear that higher bar, rather than discovering the shortfall only after the loan payments have already begun.

This is why break-even analysis is a foundational business planning tool, distinct from average unit economics but built on the same underlying building blocks: it does not tell a business owner whether they will actually achieve a given sales volume, but it does clearly establish the specific volume threshold required before any profit begins to accumulate, and how sensitive that threshold is to changes in price, variable cost, or fixed cost — information essential for evaluating any decision, like taking on new debt or a new lease, that changes the business's fixed cost base.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: A small manufacturing business has $8,000 in monthly fixed costs and sells its product for $50 per unit, with $30 in variable cost per unit, producing a $20 contribution margin per unit. Its current break-even point is $8,000 / $20 = 400 units per month. If the owner takes on a new equipment loan adding $2,000 per month in fixed costs (interest rates having risen since the loan was taken out), total fixed costs rise to $10,000, and the new break-even point becomes $10,000 / $20 = 500 units per month — an additional 100 units the business must now sell every month, before any loan was taken, just to reach the same zero-profit point as before.

Q2: B

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** This is the fundamental distinction: fixed costs are volume-independent, while variable costs scale directly with production or sales volume.

**Q2.** This formula determines how many units must be sold for total contribution margin to exactly cover total fixed costs.

**Q3.** Since break-even point is calculated by dividing fixed costs by contribution margin per unit, an increase in fixed costs directly raises the required sales volume.

**Q4.** With total fixed costs rising to $10,000 and contribution margin per unit remaining at $20, the new break-even point is 500 units per month.

**Q5.** While it does not forecast actual sales, it provides essential, decision-relevant information about the volume required to reach profitability under different cost assumptions.

## Key Takeaways
- Fixed costs remain constant regardless of sales volume; variable costs scale directly with units produced or sold.
- Break-even point in units equals total fixed costs divided by contribution margin per unit.
- Taking on a new fixed cost, like a loan payment, raises total fixed costs and therefore raises the break-even point.
- Break-even analysis does not predict future sales but clearly establishes the volume threshold and its sensitivity to cost and price changes.
