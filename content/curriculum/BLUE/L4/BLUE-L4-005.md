# Working Capital Analysis: Why a Profitable Business Can Still Run Out of Cash

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a widespread public health disruption halting global shipping and manufacturing for an extended stretch, snarling supply chains across many industries at once. As a widespread supply disruption stretches out delivery times and payment cycles across an industry, some genuinely profitable businesses suddenly find themselves unable to pay their own bills on time. This lesson explains the specific financial mechanism behind that seemingly contradictory situation.

## Learning Objectives
- Explain what working capital measures.
- Explain the cash conversion cycle and its three main components.
- Explain why a profitable business can still experience a cash shortage.
- Identify strategies a business can use to manage working capital more effectively.

## Core Content
Working capital equals current assets (cash, accounts receivable, and inventory, among others) minus current liabilities (accounts payable and other short-term obligations), representing the short-term liquid resources a business has available to fund its ongoing operations. VERIFIED FACT: a related and more specific concept, the cash conversion cycle, measures the time it takes a business to convert its investments in inventory and other resources into actual cash received from customers, combining days inventory outstanding (how long inventory sits before being sold), days sales outstanding (how long it takes to collect cash from customers after a sale), and days payable outstanding (how long the business takes to pay its own suppliers).

Here is the "why" a genuinely profitable business, on paper, can still run out of cash, directly connecting to the delivery and payment cycle stretching described in the scenario above: profit, as reported on an income statement, is an accrual accounting concept (recall Lesson 42 from the WHITE track's free cash flow analysis) that does not necessarily align with the actual timing of cash moving in and out of the business. A business can report a genuine profit on a sale while the actual cash from that sale has not yet been collected (sitting in accounts receivable), while simultaneously needing to pay its own suppliers, employees, and other obligations in cash right now — if the gap between when cash goes out and when cash comes in stretches too long, or grows too large as the business scales, the business can face a genuine cash shortage despite being profitable on paper.

This is why managing working capital is a distinct operational discipline from managing profitability: strategies include negotiating longer payment terms with suppliers (extending days payable outstanding), collecting from customers more quickly (reducing days sales outstanding, sometimes through early-payment discounts or stricter credit terms), and managing inventory levels more efficiently (reducing days inventory outstanding) — none of which necessarily change the business's reported profit margin, but all of which directly affect whether the business has enough actual cash on hand to keep operating, especially during a period of industry-wide disruption to normal payment and delivery timelines like the one described above.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: A growing, genuinely profitable distribution business reports a healthy 15% net margin, but a supply chain disruption extends its typical inventory turnover time and causes several major customers to delay their own payments, stretching the company's cash conversion cycle significantly. Meanwhile, the company's own suppliers, facing their own cash pressures from the same disruption, begin demanding faster payment. The combined effect — cash going out faster while cash coming in arrives more slowly — creates a genuine cash shortage that threatens the company's ability to meet payroll, despite its underlying profitability remaining fundamentally sound.

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** Working capital specifically captures the short-term liquidity position available to a business, distinct from its overall size or long-term financial structure.

**Q2.** This metric specifically tracks the timing gap between cash going out (for inventory and operations) and cash coming back in from customers.

**Q3.** The timing mismatch between accrual-based profit recognition and actual cash movement is the core reason profitable businesses can still face liquidity problems.

**Q4.** These specific working capital management strategies directly affect cash timing without necessarily changing the underlying profitability of each sale.

**Q5.** The simultaneous slowdown in cash coming in and acceleration in cash required to go out created the specific working capital squeeze described in the scenario.

## Key Takeaways
- Working capital measures short-term liquid resources (current assets minus current liabilities) available to fund ongoing operations.
- The cash conversion cycle tracks how long it takes to convert inventory investment into actual collected cash from customers.
- A profitable business can still run out of cash due to the timing mismatch between accrual-based profit and actual cash movement.
- Managing supplier payment terms, customer collection speed, and inventory levels can improve working capital without necessarily changing reported profit margin.
