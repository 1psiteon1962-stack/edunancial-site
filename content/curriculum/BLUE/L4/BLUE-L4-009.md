# Cap Table Analysis: Why Equity Dilution Changes Ownership Even Without Selling Shares

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a central bank raising its benchmark interest rate sharply and repeatedly over a short stretch of months to fight rising inflation. As borrowing costs rise sharply, some growing businesses that might previously have relied more heavily on debt financing turn instead to raising additional equity capital, and existing owners want to understand exactly how that decision will affect their ownership stake. This lesson explains the mechanics behind that concern.

## Learning Objectives
- Explain what a capitalization table (cap table) tracks.
- Explain what equity dilution means and why it occurs when new shares are issued.
- Calculate a basic ownership percentage change resulting from a new equity raise.
- Explain why dilution is not necessarily a bad outcome for existing owners, depending on the resulting change in overall company value.

## Core Content
A capitalization table (cap table) is a record of a company's ownership structure, listing all owners (founders, employees with equity compensation, and outside investors) along with the type and number of shares or ownership units each holds, and the resulting percentage ownership each represents. VERIFIED FACT: when a company raises additional equity capital by issuing new shares to new investors, the total number of outstanding shares increases, which mechanically reduces (dilutes) the percentage ownership of all existing shareholders, even though those existing shareholders have not sold any of their own shares and their absolute number of shares owned has not changed.

Here is the "why" this dilution occurs specifically because ownership percentage is a function of an individual's shares divided by the total shares outstanding: if a company has 1,000,000 total shares outstanding and an existing owner holds 100,000 of them (a 10% stake), and the company then issues 250,000 new shares to a new investor in an equity raise, total shares outstanding rise to 1,250,000, and the existing owner's unchanged 100,000 shares now represent only 8% of the company (100,000 / 1,250,000), even though that owner still holds the exact same number of shares as before.

This is why dilution, despite the seemingly negative connotation of the term, is not automatically a bad outcome for existing owners, connecting to the funding decision described in the scenario above: if the capital raised through the equity issuance is used effectively to grow the company's overall value by more than the percentage diluted away, an existing owner's smaller percentage stake in a now-larger, more valuable company can still represent a larger absolute dollar value than their larger percentage stake in the smaller, less capitalized company would have been worth — the relevant comparison is not the percentage change alone, but the change in the dollar value of the owner's stake, which depends on how effectively the newly raised capital is deployed. This is also why choosing equity financing over debt financing, as businesses might do more often when debt has become expensive (as in the scenario above), involves this real dilution tradeoff that a debt-financed alternative, which does not dilute ownership at all, would not create — though debt, of course, carries its own distinct risk of required repayment regardless of business performance (echoing the leverage principles covered elsewhere in this curriculum).

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: A founder owns 100,000 shares (10%) of a company valued at $10 million, making their stake worth $1 million. The company raises additional equity capital by issuing 250,000 new shares to an investor, diluting the founder's ownership to 8% of the now-larger 1,250,000 total shares outstanding. If the newly raised capital is used effectively and the company's value grows to $15 million as a result, the founder's now-smaller 8% stake is worth $1.2 million — a larger dollar value than their pre-dilution 10% stake, despite owning a smaller percentage of the company, illustrating why dilution is not automatically a loss for existing owners if the capital raised generates sufficient additional value.

## Answer Key
Q1: B
Q2: B
Q3: B

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** The cap table is specifically a record of who owns what portion of a company, not a financial performance statement.

**Q2.** The mathematical relationship between an individual's fixed share count and a growing total share count is the direct mechanism behind dilution.

**Q3.** The relevant comparison is the change in absolute dollar value of the owner's stake, not simply the percentage change, and effective use of the newly raised capital can offset or exceed the dilution effect.

**Q4.** The increase in overall company value, driven by effective use of the new capital, was large enough to increase the founder's stake's dollar value despite the reduced percentage ownership.

**Q5.** The choice between financing types involves weighing the dilution cost of equity against the mandatory repayment risk and leverage exposure of debt, a tradeoff that can shift depending on the cost and availability of each option.

## Key Takeaways
- A cap table records a company's ownership structure, including all shareholders and their respective ownership percentages.
- Issuing new shares dilutes existing shareholders' percentage ownership, since ownership percentage depends on shares held divided by total shares outstanding.
- Dilution is not automatically negative for existing owners if the capital raised increases the company's overall value by more than the percentage diluted away.
- Choosing equity financing over debt financing involves accepting dilution in exchange for avoiding mandatory debt repayment and leverage risk.
