# Customer Concentration Risk Analysis: Why Relying on a Few Large Customers Is a Double-Edged Sword

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a large trading bloc suddenly imposing steep tariffs on a broad category of imported manufactured goods, raising costs for companies that depend on those imports as inputs. As new tariffs disrupt trade flows, a business that depends heavily on just two large customers for the majority of its revenue watches nervously as those customers reconsider their own sourcing strategies in response. This lesson examines the specific risk created by that kind of customer concentration.

## Learning Objectives
- Explain what customer concentration risk means for a business.
- Calculate a basic customer concentration percentage.
- Explain why customer concentration creates risk even when the concentrated relationships are currently strong and profitable.
- Identify strategies a business can use to manage or reduce customer concentration risk.

## Core Content
Customer concentration risk refers to the risk a business faces when a large percentage of its total revenue depends on a small number of customers, meaning the loss of even one or two of those key relationships could have a severe, disproportionate impact on the business's overall financial health. VERIFIED FACT: a commonly used, though not universally standardized, threshold flagged in business valuation and lending contexts is when a single customer represents more than roughly 10% of total revenue, or when a small handful of customers together represent a majority of total revenue — situations generally considered to warrant closer risk scrutiny.

Here is the "why" customer concentration creates real risk even when the concentrated relationships are currently strong, profitable, and seemingly stable, directly relevant to the trade disruption scenario above: a business's own performance and profitability can depend heavily on decisions made by external parties (the concentrated customers) that the business itself does not control — a shift in that customer's own sourcing strategy, financial condition, ownership, or strategic priorities, potentially triggered by something entirely outside the selling business's control (such as a tariff-driven change in the customer's own sourcing decisions), can suddenly and severely affect the selling business's revenue, with limited ability to quickly replace that lost revenue given how concentrated it had become.

This is why customer concentration is a specific, often heavily weighted factor in business valuation (connecting to Lesson 8's valuation multiple analysis) and in lending decisions: a business with the exact same current revenue and profitability, but with that revenue spread across a broad, diversified customer base, is generally considered lower risk, and often commands a higher valuation multiple and easier access to financing, than an otherwise identical business with the same revenue concentrated in just one or two key accounts. This is why actively working to diversify a customer base — even when the concentrated relationships feel secure in the present — is a commonly recommended risk management practice, similar in underlying logic to the asset diversification principles covered in other parts of this curriculum, simply applied to the concentration of customer relationships rather than investment assets.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: A manufacturing business generates 65% of its total revenue from just two large customers. When new tariffs disrupt those two customers' own supply chains, both customers independently decide to restructure their sourcing strategies, and one of them shifts a significant portion of its purchasing to an alternative supplier unaffected by the same tariff exposure. This single decision, made entirely by the customer and outside the manufacturing business's control, immediately reduces the manufacturer's total revenue by nearly 30%, an impact that a similarly sized business with a more diversified customer base, none representing more than 5% of total revenue, would not have experienced to nearly the same degree from the same external trade disruption.

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** Customer concentration risk specifically concerns the vulnerability created by revenue dependence on a small number of key accounts.

**Q2.** The vulnerability stems from dependence on factors outside the business's own control, regardless of how positive the current relationship appears.

**Q3.** The elevated risk associated with concentration is a commonly weighted factor in both valuation and lending decisions, generally working against businesses with high concentration.

**Q4.** The severity of the impact was a direct consequence of how concentrated the business's revenue was in just a small number of customer relationships.

**Q5.** Proactive diversification of the customer base is a widely recommended practice for reducing this specific type of business risk, echoing broader diversification principles applied elsewhere in this curriculum.

## Key Takeaways
- Customer concentration risk describes the vulnerability created when a large share of revenue depends on a small number of customers.
- This risk exists even when concentrated relationships are currently strong, since the business depends on decisions made by external parties it does not control.
- Concentrated customer bases are generally viewed as higher risk in valuation and lending contexts than diversified ones.
- Actively diversifying the customer base is a commonly recommended risk management strategy, even when current concentrated relationships feel secure.
