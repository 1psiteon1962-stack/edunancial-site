# Pricing Strategy Analysis: Why Price Elasticity Determines the Right Response to Rising Costs

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a large trading bloc suddenly imposing steep tariffs on a broad category of imported manufactured goods, raising costs for companies that depend on those imports as inputs. As tariffs raise input costs across an industry, some businesses successfully pass most of that cost increase on to customers through higher prices with little effect on sales volume, while others that try the same approach see sales collapse. This lesson explains the specific factor that determines which outcome a given business is likely to experience.

## Learning Objectives
- Explain what price elasticity of demand measures.
- Distinguish between relatively elastic and relatively inelastic demand.
- Explain why a business's ability to pass rising costs on to customers through higher prices depends on its specific demand elasticity.
- Identify factors that tend to make demand for a product more or less elastic.

## Core Content
Price elasticity of demand measures how sensitive the quantity of a product customers are willing to buy is to a change in that product's price, generally expressed as the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price. VERIFIED FACT: demand is described as relatively elastic when a given percentage price change produces a larger percentage change in quantity demanded (customers are highly price-sensitive), and relatively inelastic when a given percentage price change produces a smaller percentage change in quantity demanded (customers are less price-sensitive).

Here is the "why" this specific characteristic determines whether raising prices in response to a cost increase, like the tariff-driven cost shock in the scenario above, is likely to help or hurt a business: a business facing relatively inelastic demand for its product (perhaps because it offers a unique, differentiated product with few close substitutes, or because the product represents a small share of customers' overall budgets, or because customers have limited ability to easily switch to an alternative) can often pass most of a cost increase on to customers through higher prices without losing a proportionally large amount of sales volume, preserving its overall profitability. A business facing relatively elastic demand (perhaps because it sells a commodity-like product with many close, readily available substitutes) risks losing a disproportionately large share of its sales volume if it raises prices by the same percentage, potentially making the price increase counterproductive — the resulting drop in volume could reduce total revenue and profit by more than the cost increase itself would have, had the business simply absorbed it instead.

This is why understanding a business's own specific demand elasticity, rather than assuming all businesses can or should respond identically to a common cost shock like a broad tariff increase, is essential to sound pricing strategy: factors that tend to make demand more elastic include the availability of close substitute products, the product representing a larger share of a customer's overall budget (making customers more price-conscious), and greater price transparency and ease of comparison shopping, while factors that tend to make demand more inelastic include product differentiation and brand loyalty, the product being a smaller share of the customer's budget, and higher switching costs or limited availability of substitutes.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: Two businesses facing the identical 15% tariff-driven increase in the cost of a key imported component both consider raising prices by a similar percentage to offset the cost increase. Business A sells a highly differentiated, branded product with strong customer loyalty and few close substitutes, and it successfully raises prices by 12% with only a 3% decline in sales volume, largely preserving its overall profit margin. Business B sells a more commodity-like product with several readily available, similarly priced competitor alternatives, and when it raises prices by the same 12%, it loses 25% of its sales volume as price-sensitive customers switch to competitors, resulting in a larger overall decline in total revenue and profit than if Business B had simply absorbed some of the cost increase instead of passing it fully on to customers.

A) A business's total revenue over a fixed period
B) How sensitive the quantity of a product customers are willing to buy is to a change in that product's price

D) The interest rate charged on a business's outstanding debt

**Q2. What characterizes relatively inelastic demand?**
A) A given percentage price change produces a larger percentage change in quantity demanded
B) A given percentage price change produces a smaller percentage change in quantity demanded, meaning customers are less price-sensitive
C) Demand that changes randomly with no relationship to price at all
D) Demand that is identical for every product regardless of its characteristics

**Q3. Why can a business with relatively inelastic demand generally pass a cost increase on to customers more successfully than a business with elastic demand?**
A) Inelastic demand has no relationship to how customers respond to price increases

D) Price elasticity has no bearing on whether raising prices is a sound strategic response to rising costs

**Q4. Which of the following factors tends to make demand for a product more elastic?**
A) Strong brand loyalty and a highly differentiated product with no close substitutes
B) The availability of close substitute products and greater price transparency, allowing easier comparison shopping
C) The product representing a very small share of the customer's overall budget
D) High switching costs that make it difficult for customers to change to an alternative

**Q5. In the example, why did Business B experience a larger overall decline in revenue and profit than Business A after both raised prices by a similar percentage?**
A) Business B actually raised its prices by a much smaller percentage than Business A
B) Business B's more commodity-like product with readily available substitutes made its demand more elastic, causing a much larger proportional decline in sales volume than Business A experienced
C) Business A's demand was more elastic than Business B's demand
D) Both businesses experienced identical declines in sales volume and revenue

## Answer Key
Q1: B
Q2: B
Q3: B
Q4: B
Q5: B

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** Price elasticity specifically captures the relationship between price changes and the resulting change in quantity demanded.

**Q2.** Inelastic demand describes a situation where customers continue purchasing at similar volumes even as price changes, indicating lower price sensitivity.

**Q3.** The lower price sensitivity characteristic of inelastic demand means a price increase is less likely to trigger a large offsetting decline in sales volume.

**Q4.** Available substitutes and easy price comparison both increase customers' ability and willingness to switch away from a product following a price increase, making demand more elastic.

**Q5.** The differing elasticity of demand for each business's specific product directly explains why the same percentage price increase produced such different sales volume and profitability outcomes.

## Key Takeaways
- Price elasticity of demand measures how sensitive quantity demanded is to a change in price.
- Inelastic demand means customers are less price-sensitive, allowing cost increases to be passed on with a smaller effect on sales volume.
- Elastic demand means customers are more price-sensitive, making cost pass-through riskier and potentially counterproductive.
- Product differentiation, brand loyalty, and limited substitutes tend to make demand more inelastic, while available substitutes and price transparency tend to make it more elastic.
