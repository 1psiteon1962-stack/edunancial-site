# Sensitivity Analysis: Why Testing a Business Plan Against Changed Assumptions Reveals Hidden Fragility

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a large trading bloc suddenly imposing steep tariffs on a broad category of imported manufactured goods, raising costs for companies that depend on those imports as inputs. As tariffs threaten to raise costs for a business's planned new product line, the business owner realizes their original financial projections rested on several specific assumptions that suddenly look much less certain than when the plan was first built. This lesson introduces a structured way to test exactly how much any single assumption actually matters to a plan's overall viability.

## Learning Objectives
- Explain what sensitivity analysis means in the context of business financial planning.
- Explain the process of varying one assumption at a time to isolate its individual effect on a plan's outcome.
- Explain why sensitivity analysis reveals which assumptions a business plan's viability depends on most heavily.
- Apply sensitivity analysis thinking to evaluate a business plan facing a specific new cost risk, such as a tariff.

## Core Content
Sensitivity analysis is a structured financial planning technique that tests how much a business plan's projected outcome (such as projected profit or break-even timing, connecting to Lesson 4) changes when one specific underlying assumption is varied, while holding all other assumptions constant. VERIFIED FACT: this "one variable at a time" approach isolates the individual effect of each specific assumption, distinguishing it from simply building a single best-guess projection using several assumptions blended together, which does not reveal how much any one specific assumption actually matters to the overall result.

Here is the "why" this individual-variable approach reveals which assumptions a business plan's viability depends on most heavily, directly relevant to the tariff-driven cost uncertainty in the scenario above: a business owner might discover, through this kind of sensitivity testing, that their plan's overall viability is highly sensitive to a specific cost assumption (such as the price of a key imported input now subject to a tariff) — meaning even a moderate change in that one specific assumption meaningfully changes whether the plan is projected to succeed or fail — while the same plan might be relatively insensitive to a different assumption (such as a modest change in a secondary marketing expense line), meaning that second assumption could turn out quite differently than projected without meaningfully changing the plan's overall viability.

This is why sensitivity analysis is a valuable planning discipline, distinct from simply hoping a single best-guess projection turns out to be roughly accurate: identifying which specific assumptions a plan is most sensitive to allows a business owner to focus their limited attention and risk management effort specifically on the assumptions that matter most (in the scenario above, closely monitoring the tariff situation and potentially developing contingency plans, such as alternative, non-tariffed suppliers per Lesson 13's vendor diversification analysis, specifically for that high-sensitivity cost assumption) rather than spreading equal attention and concern across every assumption in the plan regardless of how much any individual assumption actually matters to the plan's ultimate viability.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: A business owner planning a new product launch runs a sensitivity analysis on their financial projection, testing the individual effect of varying five different key assumptions — input cost, unit price, sales volume, marketing spend, and shipping cost — one at a time. The analysis reveals that a 20% increase in input cost (now a real possibility given a newly announced tariff) would push the product from a projected profit into an outright loss, while an even larger 30% increase in shipping cost would only modestly reduce the projected profit without threatening the plan's overall viability. Armed with this specific insight, the owner focuses their risk management effort specifically on securing an alternative, non-tariffed input supplier, rather than spending equal time worrying about the comparatively low-impact shipping cost assumption.

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** This one-variable-at-a-time approach is the defining methodology of sensitivity analysis, distinguishing it from a single blended best-guess projection.

**Q2.** This isolation of individual variable effects is precisely what allows a business owner to identify which specific assumptions deserve the most attention and risk management focus.

**Q3.** The varying structural role and weight of different assumptions within a financial model explains why some specific assumptions can dramatically affect overall viability while others have only a modest effect.

**Q4.** This targeted allocation of limited planning and risk management attention toward the highest-impact assumptions is the practical, actionable benefit sensitivity analysis provides.

**Q5.** The specific results of the sensitivity analysis directly and appropriately guided the owner's decision to prioritize risk management effort toward the assumption shown to matter most to the plan's overall success.

## Key Takeaways
- Sensitivity analysis tests how much a business plan's outcome changes when one assumption is varied individually, holding others constant.
- This approach isolates each assumption's individual effect, revealing which specific assumptions matter most to a plan's overall viability.
- Plans can be highly sensitive to some assumptions and relatively insensitive to others, depending on each assumption's specific role in the financial model.
- Identifying high-sensitivity assumptions allows a business owner to focus limited risk management effort where it matters most, rather than spreading equal attention across every assumption.
