# Business Pivot Analysis: Why Changing a Core Business Model Requires Weighing Sunk Costs Correctly

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a widespread public health disruption halting global shipping and manufacturing for an extended stretch, snarling supply chains across many industries at once. As a widespread disruption fundamentally changes customer behavior and demand patterns across an industry, some businesses recognize that their original business model may no longer be viable and must decide whether to fundamentally pivot to a new approach, while others cling to their original plan largely because of how much they have already invested in it. This lesson examines the specific reasoning error that can prevent a genuinely necessary pivot from happening.

## Learning Objectives
- Explain what a business pivot means.
- Explain the sunk cost fallacy and why it can distort a pivot decision.
- Explain why a sound pivot decision should be based only on forward-looking costs and benefits, not on how much has already been invested.
- Apply this reasoning to evaluate whether a business facing a significantly changed market should pivot or continue with its original model.

## Core Content
A business pivot refers to a fundamental change in a business's core strategy, product, target market, or business model, typically undertaken in response to evidence that the original approach is not achieving sufficient success or is no longer well-suited to current market conditions. VERIFIED FACT: the sunk cost fallacy describes the common reasoning error of factoring in past costs that have already been incurred and cannot be recovered (the "sunk cost") when making a forward-looking decision, even though those past costs are, by definition, irrelevant to what will actually produce the best outcome going forward, since they cannot be changed regardless of what decision is now made.

Here is the "why" the sunk cost fallacy specifically distorts pivot decisions, directly relevant to the changed-market scenario above: a business owner who has invested substantial time, money, and personal identity into a specific original business model often feels a strong, understandable psychological pull to continue with that original approach, specifically because of how much has already been invested in it — a pull that has nothing to do with whether continuing that original approach is actually the best forward-looking decision given current market conditions, and everything to do with a natural human reluctance to "waste" or abandon a significant prior investment, even when that investment is already gone regardless of what is decided now.

This is why a sound pivot decision should evaluate only the forward-looking costs and expected benefits of each available path — continuing the current approach versus pivoting to an alternative — comparing what each path is expected to cost and produce from this point forward, entirely independent of how much has already been spent on the original approach up to this point: if the original business model, evaluated purely on its own forward-looking merits given current market conditions (such as the fundamentally changed customer behavior described in the disruption scenario above), is genuinely unlikely to succeed, the fact that substantial resources have already been invested in that original approach does not change that forward-looking assessment, and continuing to pursue a genuinely non-viable path specifically because of how much has already been invested in it represents exactly the reasoning error the sunk cost fallacy describes, often making a bad situation worse by continuing to invest additional resources into a path that objective, forward-looking analysis suggests is unlikely to succeed regardless.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: A business owner who has invested three years and a substantial amount of capital into a specific business model discovers that a widespread industry disruption has fundamentally and permanently changed customer behavior in a way that makes the original model's core assumptions no longer valid going forward. Despite recognizing this reality, the owner initially resists pivoting to a different approach, reasoning that abandoning the original model would mean their three years of effort and capital investment were "wasted." A trusted advisor points out that this reasoning reflects the sunk cost fallacy — the three years and capital already invested are gone regardless of what decision is made now, and the only relevant question is which path forward, continuing the now-poorly-suited original model or pivoting to a new approach better aligned with the changed market, offers the better forward-looking outcome from this point on, a reframing that ultimately leads the owner to pursue the pivot.

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** A pivot specifically involves this kind of fundamental strategic change, distinguishing it from smaller, more incremental business adjustments.

**Q2.** The sunk cost fallacy specifically identifies this common reasoning error, where irrecoverable past investments improperly influence decisions that should be based only on forward-looking considerations.

**Q3.** The irrecoverable nature of sunk costs means they provide no useful information about which forward-looking path is actually likely to produce the best outcome, making them properly irrelevant to the decision.

**Q4.** Compounding the fallacy by investing further resources into an already non-viable path, purely to avoid acknowledging the sunk cost, typically deepens the business's losses rather than recovering them.

**Q5.** By correctly reframing the decision around forward-looking considerations rather than the irrecoverable past investment, the advisor helped the owner avoid the sunk cost fallacy and make a more objectively sound strategic choice.

## Key Takeaways
- A business pivot is a fundamental strategic change typically undertaken when evidence suggests the original approach is no longer sufficiently viable.
- The sunk cost fallacy describes the reasoning error of factoring in irrecoverable past costs when making a forward-looking decision.
- A sound pivot decision should evaluate only the forward-looking costs and benefits of each available path, independent of how much has already been invested.
- Continuing a non-viable original approach specifically because of prior investment often compounds losses rather than recovering them, exemplifying the sunk cost fallacy in action.
