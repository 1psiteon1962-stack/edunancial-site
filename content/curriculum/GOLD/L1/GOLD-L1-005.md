# Dollar-Cost Averaging Basics

## Learning Objectives
- Define dollar-cost averaging in plain terms
- Explain why this approach reduces the impact of trying to guess the perfect time to buy
- Practice applying the concept to a simple example

## Core Content

Dollar-cost averaging, often abbreviated DCA, means investing a fixed amount of money at regular intervals — say, monthly — regardless of what the price is doing at that moment, rather than trying to time a single large purchase for the "perfect" moment. Because the amount invested stays the same each time, you naturally buy more units when the price is low and fewer units when the price is high, without needing to predict anything.

This matters because consistently predicting the best time to buy is extremely difficult, even for professionals, and trying to do it often leads to hesitation, missed opportunities, or buying at emotionally-driven bad times (like buying when prices are rising and everyone's excited, and hesitating when prices are down and everyone's nervous — exactly backwards from ideal). DCA removes that decision entirely by making the timing automatic and consistent.

## Worked Example

ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: Suppose someone invests $50 every month into silver, regardless of price. In a month when silver costs $25 per ounce, that $50 buys 2 ounces. In a month when the price drops to $20 per ounce, the same $50 buys 2.5 ounces — automatically buying more when it's cheaper. Over many months, this evens out the average price paid per ounce, without the investor ever having to guess which month was the "best" time to buy.

## Practice Quiz

1. Define dollar-cost averaging in your own words.
2. What happens to the number of units bought when the price is low, under DCA?
3. Why is trying to time a single "perfect" purchase difficult?
4. If someone invests $100 monthly and the price is $10 one month and $20 the next, how many total units do they own after both months?

## Answer Key
1. Investing a fixed amount at regular intervals regardless of price, rather than trying to time a single purchase.
2. More units get bought when the price is low, since the fixed dollar amount stretches further.
3. Because consistently predicting price movements is extremely difficult, even for professionals.
4. 10 units in month one ($100/$10) plus 5 units in month two ($100/$20) equals 15 total units.

## Answers and Explanations
1. This captures the core mechanic of DCA — fixed amount, regular interval, no price prediction required.
2. This is the built-in benefit of DCA: automatically buying more when prices are favorable.
3. Difficulty of prediction is the underlying reason DCA is a useful, emotion-resistant alternative.
4. Applying the formula directly across both months demonstrates the practical mechanics of DCA.

## Key Takeaways
- Dollar-cost averaging means investing a fixed amount at regular intervals, regardless of price.
- This naturally buys more units when prices are low and fewer when prices are high, without requiring prediction.
- DCA removes the difficult, often emotionally-driven challenge of trying to time the "perfect" purchase.
