# Opportunity Cost of Holding Metals

## Learning Objectives
- Define opportunity cost in plain terms
- Apply the concept specifically to precious metals versus income-producing investments
- Understand why this tradeoff matters for overall allocation decisions

## Core Content

Opportunity cost is the value of the next-best alternative given up when a choice is made. Every dollar allocated to precious metals is a dollar not allocated to something else — potentially a dividend-paying stock, an interest-bearing bond, or another investment capable of generating ongoing income or different growth characteristics.

Since precious metals don't produce income on their own (a point emphasized in an earlier lesson), the opportunity cost of holding them specifically includes whatever income or growth that same money might have generated in an alternative investment. This doesn't mean precious metals are a poor choice — it means their value proposition (store of value, inflation hedge, diversification, discussed throughout this track) needs to be weighed honestly against what's being given up by not allocating that same money elsewhere.

This concept reinforces why precious metals are typically discussed as one piece of a diversified portfolio, covered in an earlier lesson, rather than as a sole or dominant investment. A thoughtful allocation weighs the specific benefits precious metals provide against this real opportunity cost, rather than treating the decision as automatically obvious in either direction.

## Worked Example

An investor with $1,000 to allocate might consider putting all of it into silver, or splitting it between silver and a dividend-paying stock index fund. Choosing to put the full amount into silver means giving up whatever dividends and potential growth the stock index fund might have generated over that same period — that forgone potential return is the opportunity cost of the all-silver choice. This doesn't make the silver choice wrong; it means the decision should honestly weigh silver's specific benefits (store of value, diversification) against this real, quantifiable tradeoff, rather than ignoring it.
