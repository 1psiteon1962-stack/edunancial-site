# Reviewing and Adjusting a Metals Plan Over Time

## Learning Objectives
- Explain the difference between disciplined plan adherence and stubborn refusal to adjust
- Identify appropriate, deliberate triggers for reviewing a metals investing plan
- Distinguish legitimate plan adjustment from reactive, emotional plan abandonment

## Core Content

A well-constructed personal plan, like the one covered in the previous lesson, isn't meant to be followed blindly forever regardless of changing circumstances — but it also shouldn't be abandoned reactively at the first sign of short-term price volatility. The key distinction is between deliberate, periodic review and reactive, emotional abandonment, similar to the persistence-versus-stubbornness distinction covered in this curriculum's leadership track.

Appropriate triggers for reviewing a plan include: significant changes in personal financial circumstances (a change in income, new financial goals, an approaching major expense), a scheduled periodic review (once a year, for example, rather than constantly), or a genuine, well-reasoned change in understanding about the role precious metals should play in an overall financial picture — not simply because prices moved in an unexpected direction over a short period.

This distinction matters because the whole point of writing a plan down in advance, covered in the previous lesson, is to have a stable reference point specifically for resisting reactive, short-term emotional decisions. A plan that gets abandoned every time short-term volatility occurs defeats its own purpose entirely.

## Worked Example

An investor following their written plan experiences a significant short-term price drop shortly after a purchase. The plan itself anticipated this kind of volatility (connecting to the earlier volatility lesson), so this alone isn't a legitimate trigger to abandon the plan — it's exactly the kind of moment the plan was written in advance to help withstand. Compare that to a genuine life change, like a job loss significantly affecting available monthly budget — that's a legitimate, deliberate reason to review and adjust the plan's specific budget component, without needing to abandon the overall long-term strategy and reasoning behind it.

## Answer Key
1. Deliberate, periodic review versus reactive, emotional abandonment.
2. Any two of: significant personal financial circumstance changes, a scheduled periodic review, or a genuine, well-reasoned change in understanding.
3. No — the plan was specifically written in advance to help withstand exactly this kind of anticipated short-term volatility.
4. Because both distinctions involve staying committed to a sound underlying goal while being genuinely responsive to real, deliberate reasons for change, rather than reacting emotionally to short-term noise.

## Answers and Explanations
1. This distinction is the central concept the lesson establishes for handling plan review appropriately.
2. These triggers represent genuine, deliberate reasons for adjustment, distinct from reactive volatility responses.
3. This reinforces that anticipated volatility is not, by itself, a legitimate reason to abandon a well-reasoned plan.
4. This cross-track connection reinforces a consistent principle applied across different domains within this curriculum.

## Key Takeaways
- A plan should be reviewed deliberately and periodically, not abandoned reactively due to short-term price volatility.
- Legitimate review triggers include real financial circumstance changes, scheduled periodic reviews, or genuine shifts in understanding — not short-term price movement alone.
- This mirrors the persistence-versus-stubbornness distinction: stay committed to a sound plan while remaining genuinely responsive to real, deliberate reasons for change.
