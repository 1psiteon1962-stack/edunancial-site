## GOLD-L2-002
### Net Worth: The Scorecard Behind Every Wealth-Building Decision

**1. Learning Objectives**
By the end of this lesson, the learner will be able to:
- Define net worth at a conceptual level.
- Explain why net worth, not income, is the primary wealth-building scorecard.
- Calculate a simple net worth given basic asset and liability figures.

**2. Core Content**
Net worth is calculated as total assets minus total liabilities — a single number summarizing a person's overall financial position at a specific moment in time. This differs fundamentally from income (developed further in Lesson 4), which measures money flowing in over a period, not the accumulated value of what someone actually owns after subtracting what they owe.

Net worth serves as this level's central scorecard precisely because it captures the net effect of every wealth-building decision covered throughout this curriculum: a mortgage paid down (RED-L2), a business built with real equity value (BLUE-L2), an investment portfolio grown over time (WHITE-L2), and debts avoided or paid off (GREEN-L2's debt discussion) all show up in this single number. Tracking net worth over time, rather than focusing on any single asset or income figure in isolation, provides the clearest overall picture of whether wealth is actually being built.

**3. Worked Example**
Calculating her net worth for the first time, Renata (echoing RED-L2-036) lists her assets — home equity, retirement account balance, and savings — and subtracts her liabilities — remaining mortgage balance, car loan, and credit card balance — arriving at a single net worth figure. She realizes this number, not her income alone, gives her the clearest single measure of her actual financial position, since two people with identical incomes could have very different net worth figures depending on their accumulated assets and debts.

**4. Practice Quiz**
1. How is net worth generally calculated?
   a) Total income minus total expenses
   b) Total assets minus total liabilities
   c) Only the value of a person's home
   d) A type of tax credit
2. Why does this lesson identify net worth, rather than income, as the primary wealth-building scorecard?
   a) Income and net worth are identical concepts
   b) Net worth captures the accumulated net effect of every wealth-building decision, not just money flowing in
   c) Net worth has no relationship to wealth-building decisions
   d) Income always equals net worth
3. True or False: Two people with identical incomes always have identical net worth.
4. What did Renata's net worth calculation reveal that her income alone would not have shown?
   a) Nothing different from her income figure
   b) Her actual accumulated financial position, reflecting her assets and liabilities together
   c) Only her monthly spending habits
   d) Her employer's total revenue

**5. Answer Key**
1. b  2. b  3. False  4. b

**6. Answers and Explanations**
1. Net worth is calculated as total assets minus total liabilities.
2. Net worth captures the accumulated net effect of wealth-building decisions, unlike income, which measures flow over a period.
3. Identical incomes can produce very different net worth figures depending on accumulated assets and debts.
4. Renata's net worth calculation revealed her actual accumulated financial position, distinct from her income alone.

**7. Key Takeaways**
- Net worth is calculated as total assets minus total liabilities, a snapshot at a specific moment.
- Net worth, not income, serves as this level's central wealth-building scorecard.
- Net worth captures the accumulated net effect of decisions across every color in this curriculum.
- Two people with identical incomes can have very different net worth, depending on accumulated assets and debts.
