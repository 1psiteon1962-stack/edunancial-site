## GOLD-L2-007
### Risk and Return: Why They Move Together

**1. Learning Objectives**
By the end of this lesson, the learner will be able to:
- Explain the general relationship between risk and expected return.
- Identify why a "high return, no risk" opportunity should raise immediate skepticism.
- Connect this concept to Lesson 12's warning-signs discussion.

**2. Core Content**
A foundational principle across virtually every asset class discussed in this curriculum — real estate leverage (RED-L2-007), bond credit risk (WHITE-L2-012), and business ownership risk (BLUE-L2) — is that risk and expected return generally move together: investments offering higher potential returns generally carry correspondingly higher risk of loss, while lower-risk investments generally offer more modest expected returns. This is not a coincidence or a market inefficiency; it reflects investors generally requiring greater compensation (higher expected return) to accept greater uncertainty or potential loss.

This principle provides an essential screening tool developed further in Lesson 12: an opportunity claiming unusually high returns with little or no risk should immediately raise skepticism, since it contradicts this foundational relationship — either the risk is being understated or hidden, or the claimed return is not genuine, both patterns explored directly in Lessons 12–13's discussion of red flags and fraud.

**3. Worked Example**
Evaluating two investment opportunities, a learner notices one offers a modest, realistic return consistent with its stated risk level, while another claims an unusually high return with little acknowledged risk. Applying this lesson's foundational principle, she recognizes the second opportunity's claims contradict the basic risk-return relationship observed across every asset class in this curriculum, prompting significant skepticism and further investigation before considering it further, rather than being drawn in purely by its attractive headline return figure.

**4. Practice Quiz**
1. What is the general relationship between risk and expected return?
   a) They have no relationship to each other
   b) Higher potential returns generally carry correspondingly higher risk
   c) Higher returns always mean lower risk
   d) Risk and return move in no predictable pattern whatsoever
2. Why should an opportunity claiming high returns with little or no risk raise skepticism?
   a) Such opportunities are always genuine and should be embraced immediately
   b) This claim contradicts the foundational risk-return relationship observed across asset classes
   c) High returns always require no risk whatsoever
   d) Skepticism is never warranted for any investment claim
3. True or False: The risk-return relationship only applies to real estate, not to other asset classes discussed in this curriculum.
4. Why did the learner apply significant skepticism to the second opportunity in the worked example?
   a) It offered a modest, realistic return
   b) Its claims contradicted the basic risk-return relationship observed across every asset class in this curriculum
   c) She had no interest in investing at all
   d) The first opportunity claimed unusually high returns

**5. Answer Key**
1. b  2. b  3. False  4. b

**6. Answers and Explanations**
1. Higher potential returns generally carry correspondingly higher risk, reflecting investors' need for compensation to accept uncertainty.
2. A claim of high return with little or no risk contradicts this foundational relationship, warranting significant skepticism.
3. This risk-return relationship applies broadly across asset classes, including real estate, bonds, and business ownership.
4. The second opportunity's claims contradicted the foundational risk-return principle, appropriately prompting the learner's skepticism.

**7. Key Takeaways**
- Risk and expected return generally move together across virtually every asset class discussed in this curriculum.
- This reflects investors generally requiring greater compensation to accept greater uncertainty or potential loss.
- An opportunity claiming high return with little or no risk contradicts this principle and warrants immediate skepticism.
- This foundational principle provides an essential screening tool developed further in Lessons 12–13.
