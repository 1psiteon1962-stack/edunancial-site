## GOLD-L2-010
### Correlation: Why Some Assets Move Together and Others Don't

**1. Learning Objectives**
By the end of this lesson, the learner will be able to:
- Define correlation at a conceptual level.
- Explain why low or negative correlation between assets enhances diversification benefits.
- Apply this concept to a hypothetical multi-asset portfolio.

**2. Core Content**
Building directly on Lesson 9, this lesson introduces correlation as the specific mechanism explaining why cross-asset-class diversification actually works. Correlation measures the degree to which two assets' returns tend to move together — highly correlated assets tend to rise and fall together, while low or negatively correlated assets tend to move more independently, or even in opposite directions, from one another.

Diversification's risk-reduction benefit is strongest when combining assets with low or negative correlation, since a decline in one asset is more likely to be offset, at least partially, by stability or gains in another — echoing WHITE-L2-008's stocks-versus-bonds discussion, where these two asset classes have historically shown different behavior patterns in various market conditions. Combining highly correlated assets, even if they are technically different investments, provides much weaker diversification benefit, since they tend to decline together during the same adverse conditions.

**3. Worked Example**
Building his multi-asset wealth strategy, a learner considers not just how many different assets he holds, but how those assets have historically tended to move relative to each other. He recognizes that his real estate holdings and his paper asset portfolio have not historically moved in perfect lockstep, providing genuine diversification benefit, while two of his individual stock holdings, both in the same volatile sector, have tended to move together closely, providing much weaker diversification benefit despite being technically different investments.

**4. Practice Quiz**
1. What does correlation measure?
   a) The absolute size of an investment
   b) The degree to which two assets' returns tend to move together
   c) A type of tax credit
   d) The physical location of an asset
2. When is diversification's risk-reduction benefit strongest, per this lesson?
   a) When combining assets with high correlation
   b) When combining assets with low or negative correlation
   c) Correlation has no effect on diversification benefit
   d) Only when combining identical assets
3. True or False: Two technically different investments always provide strong diversification benefit, regardless of their correlation to each other.
4. Why did the learner's two stock holdings in the same sector provide weaker diversification benefit than his real estate and paper asset combination?
   a) They were identical investments
   b) They had tended to move together closely, reflecting high correlation, unlike his less correlated asset classes
   c) Correlation has no relationship to diversification benefit
   d) Sector had no bearing on his analysis

**5. Answer Key**
1. b  2. b  3. False  4. b

**6. Answers and Explanations**
1. Correlation measures the degree to which two assets' returns tend to move together.
2. Diversification's risk-reduction benefit is strongest when combining assets with low or negative correlation.
3. Technically different investments can still provide weak diversification benefit if they are highly correlated with each other.
4. The stocks' high correlation, both moving together in the same sector, weakened their diversification benefit compared to his less correlated asset classes.

**7. Key Takeaways**
- Correlation measures the degree to which two assets' returns tend to move together.
- Diversification's risk-reduction benefit is strongest when combining assets with low or negative correlation.
- Technically different investments can still provide weak diversification benefit if highly correlated with each other.
- This mechanism explains why the cross-asset-class diversification discussed in Lesson 9 actually reduces risk.
