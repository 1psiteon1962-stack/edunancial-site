## GOLD-L2-017
### Starting Early vs. Starting Later: The Real Cost of Delay

**1. Learning Objectives**
By the end of this lesson, the learner will be able to:
- Apply Lesson 16's compounding discussion to illustrate the cost of delaying investment.
- Explain why an early but smaller invested amount can outperform a later but larger one.
- Connect this concept to Lesson 6's opportunity cost discussion.

**2. Core Content**
Building directly on Lesson 16, this lesson illustrates a specific, powerful application of compounding: delaying the start of investing carries a real, often underappreciated opportunity cost (echoing Lesson 6), since each year of delay is a year of lost compounding growth that cannot be recovered later, even by eventually investing larger amounts. This can produce a counterintuitive result: an investor who starts earlier with smaller amounts can, in some scenarios, accumulate more than an investor who starts later with larger amounts, simply because the earlier investor's money has had more time to compound.

This does not mean it is ever "too late" to begin investing — starting later still provides meaningful benefit compared to not investing at all, and Lesson 5's income-expense gap remains valuable to build and invest at any age — but it does mean that delay carries a real, quantifiable cost, making early action, even with modest amounts, genuinely valuable rather than something to postpone until a more "ideal" future moment that may not meaningfully improve the outcome.

**3. Worked Example**
Comparing two hypothetical investors, one who begins investing a modest amount in her twenties and one who begins investing a larger amount a decade later, a learner models both scenarios at an identical assumed rate of return and finds the earlier, smaller-amount investor accumulates a comparable or even larger final balance than the later, larger-amount investor, purely due to the additional decade of compounding growth the earlier investor's money experienced. This illustrates directly why Lesson 6's opportunity cost concept applies with particular force to the decision of when to begin investing, not just what to invest in.

**4. Practice Quiz**

   b) Each year of delay represents a year of lost compounding growth that cannot be recovered later
   c) Delay always results in a larger final balance regardless of timing
   d) Investment timing has no relationship to compounding
2. Can an investor who starts earlier with smaller amounts sometimes outperform one who starts later with larger amounts?
   a) No, this is never possible under any circumstance
   b) Yes, due to the additional years of compounding growth the earlier investor's money experiences
   c) Later investors always accumulate more regardless of amounts invested
   d) Starting time has no effect on final accumulated balance
3. True or False: This lesson suggests it is ever truly "too late" to begin investing, with no benefit to starting later in life.
4. Why did the earlier, smaller-amount investor in the worked example accumulate a comparable or larger final balance?
   a) She invested a larger amount than the later investor
   b) Her money had an additional decade of compounding growth, illustrating the real cost of delay
   c) The later investor invested for a longer period
   d) Compounding had no effect on either investor's outcome

**5. Answer Key**
1. b  2. b  3. False  4. b

**6. Answers and Explanations**
1. Each year of delay represents a year of lost compounding growth that cannot be recovered later, even with larger future contributions.
2. An earlier investor with smaller amounts can sometimes outperform a later investor with larger amounts, due to additional years of compounding.
3. This lesson explicitly notes that starting later still provides meaningful benefit compared to not investing at all.
4. The earlier investor's additional decade of compounding growth, not a larger invested amount, explained her comparable or larger final balance.

**7. Key Takeaways**
- Delaying investment carries a real, quantifiable opportunity cost, since each year of delay loses irrecoverable compounding growth.
- An earlier, smaller-amount investor can sometimes outperform a later, larger-amount investor due to additional compounding time.
- It is never truly "too late" to begin investing — starting later still provides meaningful benefit over not investing at all.
- This directly connects Lesson 16's compounding mechanics to Lesson 6's opportunity cost framework, applied to timing.
