## GOLD-L2-018
### Alternative Investments: An Overview

**1. Learning Objectives**
By the end of this lesson, the learner will be able to:
- Define "alternative investments" as a general category.
- Identify common examples and their general characteristics.
- Recognize general risk, liquidity, and access considerations for this category.

**2. Core Content**
Alternative investments generally refer to asset categories outside traditional publicly traded stocks and bonds (WHITE-L2) and direct real estate ownership (RED-L2) — commonly including private equity and venture capital (developed further in Lesson 19), commodities, collectibles, cryptocurrency, and certain hedge fund strategies. These investments commonly share certain general characteristics: often lower liquidity (echoing Lesson 14) than publicly traded assets, often less standardized regulatory oversight and public information availability than traditional public markets, and, for some categories, higher minimum investment requirements or specific investor qualification requirements.

This category is deliberately broad and varied — the risk, return, and liquidity profiles across different alternative investment types differ enormously from one another, meaning "alternative investments" functions more as a residual category (everything not fitting the traditional public stock, bond, or direct real estate categories) than a single, coherent asset class with uniform characteristics, making the general evaluation framework from Lesson 11 particularly important to apply carefully to any specific alternative investment opportunity, rather than assuming characteristics from one type transfer to another.

**3. Worked Example**
Considering several different alternative investment opportunities — a venture capital fund, a commodities-focused fund, and a cryptocurrency holding — a learner recognizes these are dramatically different in their underlying risk, liquidity, and return characteristics, despite all technically falling under the broad "alternative investments" label. Rather than assuming general familiarity with one type transfers to genuine understanding of another, she applies Lesson 11's evaluation framework individually and carefully to each specific opportunity, recognizing the label itself provides little useful, specific information about any particular investment's actual characteristics.

**6. Answers and Explanations**
1. Alternative investments generally refer to asset categories outside traditional public stocks, bonds, and direct real estate ownership.
2. The specific types within this broad category differ enormously in actual risk, return, and liquidity characteristics, functioning more as a residual category.
3. Familiarity with one alternative investment type does not automatically transfer to genuine understanding of a dramatically different type.
4. The learner's individual, careful application of the evaluation framework appropriately addressed each opportunity's distinct characteristics.

**7. Key Takeaways**
- Alternative investments generally include private equity, venture capital, commodities, collectibles, cryptocurrency, and certain hedge fund strategies.
- This category commonly features lower liquidity and less standardized oversight than traditional public markets.
- The category functions more as a residual grouping than a coherent asset class, given the enormous variation within it.
- Lesson 11's evaluation framework should be applied individually and carefully to each specific alternative investment opportunity.
