# Anchoring Bias Analysis: Why an Arbitrary Reference Point Can Distort an Investment Decision

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a populous nation's real estate and consumer credit markets, after years of rapid expansion, beginning to show signs of a sharp correction as defaults start rising. As a credit-fueled boom unwinds and asset prices decline meaningfully from their earlier peaks, many investors find themselves unable to stop thinking about what a specific holding was once worth, or what they originally paid for it, even though neither figure has any actual bearing on whether that holding still makes sense to own today. This lesson examines the specific psychological tendency responsible for this fixation.

## Learning Objectives
- Explain the concept of anchoring bias as documented in behavioral finance and broader psychological research.
- Explain why an investor's original purchase price commonly serves as a powerful, but analytically irrelevant, psychological anchor.
- Explain why a specific asset's prior peak price can similarly function as a distorting anchor.
- Connect anchoring bias to the sunk cost fallacy concept covered in the BLUE-L4 track, and explain why a sound investment decision should be based only on forward-looking considerations.

## Core Content
Anchoring bias refers to the well-documented psychological tendency to rely too heavily on an initial piece of information (the "anchor") when making subsequent judgments or decisions, even when that specific initial piece of information has no actual logical or analytical bearing on the decision genuinely at hand. VERIFIED FACT: this bias has been extensively documented across a wide range of psychological and behavioral economics research contexts, extending well beyond investing alone, but its specific effects on investment decision-making have been particularly closely studied and documented.

Here is the "why" an investor's original purchase price commonly serves as a powerful, but analytically irrelevant, psychological anchor: an investor frequently continues to mentally reference what they originally paid for a specific holding when evaluating whether to continue holding or to sell it today, even though this original purchase price has no actual bearing on the forward-looking question of whether that specific holding, evaluated purely on its own current merits and future prospects, still deserves a place in the investor's portfolio today — an investor holding a position that has declined significantly below their original purchase price may resist selling specifically because doing so would require confirming a loss relative to this psychologically anchored reference point, even if the investor's own genuine, forward-looking analysis suggests the position no longer merits continued holding.

This is why a specific asset's prior peak price can similarly function as a distorting anchor: an investor may resist selling a declined position specifically because its current price seems disappointing relative to where it once traded, even though that prior peak price, like the original purchase price, has no actual bearing on the asset's genuine current or future value — this is precisely why a stock trading meaningfully below its own prior all-time high is sometimes described, misleadingly, as "cheap" relative to that anchor, even when the asset's current price may still be entirely appropriate, or even excessive, relative to its actual current underlying fundamentals. This is why anchoring bias connects directly to the sunk cost fallacy concept covered in the BLUE-L4 track's business pivot analysis: both concepts describe the same underlying reasoning error of allowing an irrelevant historical reference point (whether a sunk cost already spent, an original purchase price, or a prior peak value) to improperly influence what should be a purely forward-looking decision, and a sound investment decision, exactly like a sound business pivot decision, should be based only on the specific asset's current, genuine forward-looking merits and prospects — evaluated as if the investor were considering that specific asset for the very first time today, at its current price, with no reference whatsoever to what was originally paid or to any prior historical price level — rather than being distorted by an arbitrary, backward-looking anchor that has no actual bearing on the asset's genuine forward-looking investment case.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: An investor purchased a stock at $100 per share, and it has since declined to $60 per share. The investor's own honest, forward-looking analysis of the company's current fundamentals suggests the stock is no longer an attractive holding at any price, yet the investor resists selling, specifically because doing so would mean formally confirming a loss relative to the original $100 purchase price. A financial advisor reframes the decision by asking the investor: "If you had $60 in cash today and were deciding for the very first time whether to buy this specific stock at its current $60 price, would you choose to buy it?" — when the investor honestly answers no, the advisor points out that continuing to hold the stock is functionally identical to making that same purchase decision today, illustrating precisely how the anchoring bias tied to the original $100 purchase price had been distorting what should have been a purely forward-looking decision.

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** This excessive reliance on an initial, often analytically irrelevant reference point is precisely what anchoring bias describes, and it has been documented across a broad range of decision-making contexts.

**Q2.** This continued psychological reference to a figure with no actual forward-looking analytical relevance is precisely why the original purchase price functions as a distorting anchor.

**Q3.** This same anchoring mechanism, now applied to a prior high price rather than an original purchase price, similarly distorts what should be a purely forward-looking evaluation of the asset's genuine current merits.

**Q4.** This shared underlying mechanism — allowing an irrelevant historical reference point to distort a forward-looking decision — is precisely what connects anchoring bias to the sunk cost fallacy concept covered elsewhere in this curriculum.

**Q5.** This direct reframing exercise, removing the original purchase price from consideration entirely, exemplifies precisely the forward-looking analytical approach this lesson recommends for counteracting anchoring bias.

## Key Takeaways
- Anchoring bias describes relying too heavily on an initial, often analytically irrelevant, reference point when making a subsequent decision.
- An investor's original purchase price commonly functions as a powerful but irrelevant anchor when deciding whether to continue holding an investment.
- A prior peak price can similarly distort judgment, since it has no actual bearing on an asset's genuine current or future value.
- Anchoring bias connects directly to the sunk cost fallacy, since both involve an irrelevant historical reference point improperly influencing what should be a purely forward-looking decision.
