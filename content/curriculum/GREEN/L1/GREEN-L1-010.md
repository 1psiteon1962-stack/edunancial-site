# Tax Brackets Explained

## Learning Objectives

- Read and interpret a simple tax bracket table.
- Calculate tax owed across multiple brackets for a sample income.
- Reinforce the marginal-rate concept with a numeric example.

## Core Content

Building on the previous lesson, let's walk through brackets with actual numbers. Imagine an illustrative, simplified bracket table: income from $0–$10,000 is taxed at 10%; income from $10,001–$40,000 is taxed at 20%; income above $40,000 is taxed at 30%.

If someone earns $50,000, they do not pay 30% on the whole $50,000. Instead: the first $10,000 is taxed at 10% ($1,000), the next $30,000 (from $10,001 to $40,000) is taxed at 20% ($6,000), and the remaining $10,000 (from $40,001 to $50,000) is taxed at 30% ($3,000). Total tax owed: $10,000, which works out to an effective rate of 20% — well below the 30% top bracket.

This step-by-step, bracket-by-bracket calculation is exactly what marginal taxation means in practice. Real-world bracket numbers vary by country and change over time, so the specific dollar figures here are illustrative only — the method of calculation is the durable, transferable skill.

> **Note:** The bracket percentages and dollar thresholds in this lesson are illustrative teaching numbers, not figures for any specific country. Verify actual current brackets for your jurisdiction.

## Worked Example

**Question:** Using the illustrative brackets above, how much tax would someone earning exactly $40,000 owe?

**Student:** Let me work through it in order — first $10,000 at 10% is $1,000.

**Guide:** Good, keep going.

**Student:** The remaining $30,000, from $10,001 to $40,000, is taxed at 20%, which is $6,000.

**Guide:** And is any of it taxed at 30%?

**Student:** No — they didn't earn anything above $40,000, so the 30% bracket never applies. Total is $1,000 + $6,000 = $7,000.

**Guide:** Exactly right — and notice their effective rate is 17.5%, not 20% and not 30%.

## Answer Key

1. B, 2. B, 3. B, 4. B

## Answers and Explanations

**1.** Only the portion of income above $40,000 — which is $10,000 — falls into the 30% bracket.

**2.** Adding $1,000 + $6,000 + $3,000 across the three brackets gives a total of $10,000.

**3.** Dividing total tax ($10,000) by total income ($50,000) gives an effective rate of 20%.

**4.** The dollar figures are a teaching tool; the calculation method is what transfers to any real bracket table.

## Key Takeaways

- Brackets apply step by step, with each rate only touching the income within that band.
- Walking through the math concretely reinforces why the marginal rate concept matters in practice.
- Effective tax rate is always a blend, and always lower than the top bracket touched.
- The specific numbers used here are illustrative — always check your own jurisdiction's actual bracket table.
