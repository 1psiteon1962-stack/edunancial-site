# Self-Employment Tax: What Freelancers and Business Owners Owe

## Learning Objectives

- Recall how payroll tax is split between employer and employee.
- Explain why a self-employed person often owes both halves in some systems.
- Recognise this as a key planning consideration for freelancers and small business owners.

## Core Content

Earlier, you learned that payroll tax is often split between employer and employee — part deducted from wages, part paid separately by the employer. When you're self-employed, you are, in effect, both the employer and the employee. Many systems account for this by requiring self-employed individuals to pay a version of payroll tax that covers both halves — in the US this is called self-employment tax. (The UK works differently: the self-employed pay their own class of National Insurance on their profits through Self Assessment, at rates set specifically for them that are generally lower than the combined employee and employer rates, but they also miss out on things an employer would otherwise fund, such as employer pension contributions and statutory sick pay.)

This is one of the most commonly underestimated costs of self-employment. A new freelancer comparing their invoice total to what an equivalent employee's payslip shows can badly underestimate their true tax burden and costs if they forget that, as a business of one, they're covering contributions and costs an employer would otherwise handle. Understanding this in advance — and setting money aside accordingly — prevents an unpleasant surprise when the tax bill arrives.

## Worked Example

**Question:** A freelancer in a system with a self-employment tax compares her £80,000 in annual invoices to a friend's £80,000 salaried job and assumes their tax situations are basically the same. Are they?

**Student:** Probably not, since her friend's employer is covering part of the payroll-style tax automatically.

**Guide:** What does that mean for the freelancer's true tax burden compared to her friend's?

**Student:** She likely owes more in total payroll-style tax, since she's responsible for both the employer and employee portions herself.

**Guide:** Exactly — same top-line income, meaningfully different tax reality.

B) An unpleasant surprise or shortfall when the bill arrives
C) An automatic refund
D) A lower filing status

A) Ignore it until filing

## Answer Key

1. B, 2. B, 3. B, 4. B

## Answers and Explanations

**1.** Self-employment tax is essentially the mechanism for collecting both halves of payroll-style tax from one person.

**2.** Acting as both employer and employee means the self-employed person is on the hook for both shares.

**3.** Underestimating this cost is a common trap that leads to a larger-than-expected bill.

**4.** Proactive awareness and saving, rather than reacting after the fact, is the practical defence.

## Key Takeaways

- Self-employed individuals are responsible for their own payroll-style contributions, and in some systems they owe both the employer and employee shares.
- This means self-employment income carries a meaningfully different tax burden than equivalent salaried income.
- This is one of the most commonly underestimated costs of self-employment.
- Planning ahead and setting money aside prevents an unpleasant surprise when the bill arrives.
