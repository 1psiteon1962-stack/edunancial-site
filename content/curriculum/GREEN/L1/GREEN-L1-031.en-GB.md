# Capital Gains: Taxes on Investment Profit

## Learning Objectives

- Define a capital gain in plain terms.
- Explain the basic calculation of a capital gain.
- Recognise why gains are only realised (and typically taxed) upon sale, not while simply holding an asset.

## Core Content

A capital gain is the profit made when you sell an asset — like shares or a property — for more than you paid for it. The basic calculation is simple: sale price minus purchase price (often called the 'base cost' or 'basis') equals the gain. If the asset sold for less than its base cost, that's a capital loss instead. (In the UK, for example, gains may be subject to Capital Gains Tax above an annual tax-free allowance, but your main home is usually exempt and investments held inside an ISA are free of Capital Gains Tax. Check HMRC for current rules and allowances.)

An important nuance: in most systems, a gain is only 'realised,' and generally only taxable, when the asset is actually sold or otherwise disposed of. If a share's value doubles but you never sell it, you generally don't owe capital gains tax on that increase yet — it's an unrealised, or 'paper,' gain. This distinction matters for investment planning, because selling an appreciated asset is often a deliberate, timeable decision with tax consequences, not something that happens automatically just because the asset's value went up.

## Worked Example

**Question:** An investor buys shares for £1,000. Two years later they're worth £3,000, but she hasn't sold them. Does she owe capital gains tax right now?

**Student:** Based on this lesson, probably not yet, since the gain hasn't been realised through a sale.

**Guide:** What would trigger the tax, and how would it be calculated if she sold at £3,000?

**Student:** Selling would trigger it — the gain would be £3,000 minus her £1,000 base cost, so a £2,000 capital gain.

**Guide:** Exactly — and that timing is something she has some control over, which is a real planning lever.

## Practice Quiz

**Question 1.** A capital gain is calculated as:

A) Purchase price minus sale price
B) Sale price minus purchase price (base cost)
C) Only the purchase price
D) A fixed government-set amount

**Question 2.** If an asset is sold for less than its base cost, that's called a:

A) Capital gain
B) Capital loss
C) Tax credit
D) Refund

**Question 3.** A capital gain is generally taxed:

A) The moment the asset's value increases, regardless of sale
B) When the gain is realised, typically upon sale
C) Never
D) Only if the asset is property

**Question 4.** An unrealised, or 'paper,' gain refers to:

A) A gain that has already been taxed
B) An increase in value that hasn't yet been locked in through a sale
C) A type of tax credit
D) A capital loss

## Answer Key

1. B, 2. B, 3. B, 4. B

## Answers and Explanations

**1.** The gain calculation compares what you sold the asset for against what you originally paid.

**2.** A loss is simply the mirror image of a gain — selling for less than the original base cost.

**3.** Realisation through sale is the general trigger for capital gains tax in most systems.

**4.** Value can rise on paper without triggering tax, since nothing has actually been sold yet.

## Key Takeaways

- A capital gain is sale price minus purchase price (base cost).
- Selling below base cost produces a capital loss instead.
- Gains are generally taxed upon realisation — typically when the asset is sold.
- Unrealised gains aren't taxed yet, which makes the timing of a sale a real planning decision.
