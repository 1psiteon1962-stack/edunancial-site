# Capital Gains: Taxes on Investment Profit

## Learning Objectives

- Define a capital gain in plain terms.
- Explain the basic calculation of a capital gain.
- Recognize why gains are only realized (and typically taxed) upon sale, not while simply holding an asset.

## Core Content

A capital gain is the profit made when you sell an asset — like a stock or a piece of real estate — for more than you paid for it. The basic calculation is simple: sale price minus purchase price (often called the 'basis') equals the gain. If the asset sold for less than its basis, that's a capital loss instead.

An important nuance: in most systems, a gain is only 'realized,' and generally only taxable, when the asset is actually sold. If a stock's value doubles but you never sell it, you generally don't owe capital gains tax on that increase yet — it's an unrealized, or 'paper,' gain. This distinction matters for investment planning, because selling an appreciated asset is often a deliberate, timeable decision with tax consequences, not something that happens automatically just because the asset's value went up.

## Worked Example

**Question:** An investor buys a stock for $1,000. Two years later it's worth $3,000, but she hasn't sold it. Does she owe capital gains tax right now?

**Student:** Based on this lesson, probably not yet, since the gain hasn't been realized through a sale.

**Guide:** What would trigger the tax, and how would it be calculated if she sold at $3,000?

**Student:** Selling would trigger it — the gain would be $3,000 minus her $1,000 basis, so a $2,000 capital gain.

**Guide:** Exactly — and that timing is something she has some control over, which is a real planning lever.


## Practice Quiz


**Question 1.** A capital gain is calculated as:

A) Purchase price minus sale price
B) Sale price minus purchase price (basis)
C) Only the purchase price
D) A fixed government-set amount


**Question 2.** If an asset is sold for less than its basis, that's called a:

A) Capital gain
B) Capital loss
C) Tax credit
D) Refund


**Question 3.** A capital gain is generally taxed:

A) The moment the asset's value increases, regardless of sale
B) When the gain is realized, typically upon sale
C) Never
D) Only if the asset is real estate


**Question 4.** An unrealized, or 'paper,' gain refers to:

A) A gain that has already been taxed
B) An increase in value that hasn't yet been locked in through a sale
C) A type of tax credit
D) A capital loss


## Answer Key

1. B, 2. B, 3. B, 4. B

## Answers and Explanations

**1.** The gain calculation compares what you sold the asset for against what you originally paid.

**2.** A loss is simply the mirror image of a gain — selling for less than the original basis.

**3.** Realization through sale is the general trigger for capital gains tax in most systems.

**4.** Value can rise on paper without triggering tax, since nothing has actually been sold yet.

## Key Takeaways

- A capital gain is sale price minus purchase price (basis).
- Selling below basis produces a capital loss instead.
- Gains are generally taxed upon realization — typically when the asset is sold.
- Unrealized gains aren't taxed yet, which makes the timing of a sale a real planning decision.
