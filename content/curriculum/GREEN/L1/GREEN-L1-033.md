# Dividends and Interest: How Investment Income Is Taxed

## Learning Objectives

- Distinguish dividends and interest from capital gains.
- Explain that dividend and interest income is generally taxable in the year received.
- Recognize these as ongoing income streams, not one-time sale events.

## Core Content

So far this track has focused heavily on capital gains — profit from selling an asset. But two other common forms of investment income don't require selling anything at all: dividends (a share of a company's profits paid out to shareholders, often periodically) and interest (payments earned on money lent out, such as through a savings account or a bond).

Unlike capital gains, which are typically triggered by a sale, dividend and interest income is generally taxable in the year it's received, regardless of whether the underlying investment is ever sold. An investor holding a dividend-paying stock for decades without selling a single share will still generally owe tax each year on the dividends received during that time. Recognizing these as distinct, ongoing income streams — separate from the sale-triggered mechanics of capital gains — rounds out the basic picture of how investment income gets taxed.

## Worked Example

**Question:** An investor holds a stock for 15 years without ever selling it, but the company pays a dividend every quarter. Does she avoid all tax on that stock by never selling?

**Student:** Based on this lesson, no — the dividends themselves are generally taxable each year she receives them, even without a sale.

**Guide:** So what's actually being taxed each year, versus what would only be taxed if she eventually sold?

**Student:** The dividends are taxed as they're received. The stock's price appreciation would only become a taxable capital gain if and when she actually sells.

**Guide:** Exactly — two different income streams, two different triggers.

## Answers and Explanations

**1.** A dividend is a distribution of company profit to shareholders, distinct from a sale transaction.

**2.** Interest is compensation for lending money, whether through a bank account, bond, or similar instrument.

**3.** These income types are taxed as received, unlike capital gains, which wait for a sale to trigger tax.

**4.** Ongoing income like dividends keeps generating tax obligations year to year, independent of whether the asset is ever sold.

## Key Takeaways

- Dividends and interest are investment income types separate from capital gains.
- They're generally taxed in the year received, not upon sale.
- Holding an asset without selling doesn't avoid tax on the income it generates along the way.
- Recognizing these as distinct, ongoing streams completes the basic investment-income picture.
