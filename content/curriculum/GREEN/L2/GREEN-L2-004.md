## GREEN-L2-004
### Tax Brackets Explained: Why Earning More Doesn't Mean Losing More

**1. Learning Objectives**
By the end of this lesson, the learner will be able to:
- Explain how a progressive tax bracket system actually works.
- Debunk the common "bracket creep" myth directly.
- Apply bracket mechanics to a simple worked calculation.

**2. Core Content**
Building directly on Lesson 3, this lesson works through tax bracket mechanics in more detail. A progressive tax system divides income into segments ("brackets"), with each segment taxed at its own specific rate — as income rises into a new bracket, only the portion of income within that new bracket is taxed at the new, higher rate, while the portions of income in the lower brackets continue to be taxed at those lower rates, exactly as they were before.

This structure directly addresses the common myth introduced in Lesson 3: earning an additional dollar that pushes a person into a new bracket does not cause any of their previously earned income to suddenly be taxed at the higher rate. It also means that a raise or bonus, even one that crosses into a new bracket, will still always result in more total after-tax income than not receiving that raise at all — the widely repeated fear of "losing money by earning more" is not how progressive tax brackets actually function.

**3. Worked Example**
Using a simplified illustrative bracket structure (10% on the first portion of income, 20% on the next portion, and 30% on any amount above that), a taxpayer earning an amount that crosses into the 30% bracket calculates her total tax owed by applying each rate only to the income within that specific bracket — not applying the 30% rate to her entire income. She confirms that even after accounting for the higher rate applied only to her top slice of income, her total after-tax income is still higher than it would have been had she earned less and stayed entirely within the lower brackets, directly disproving the bracket-fear myth in her own numbers.

**4. Practice Quiz**
1. In a progressive tax bracket system, what happens to income within a lower bracket when a person's total income rises into a higher bracket?
   a) It is retroactively taxed at the new, higher rate
   b) It continues to be taxed at the lower rate that applied to that bracket
   c) It becomes entirely tax-free
   d) It is taxed at a randomly assigned rate
2. Does receiving a raise that crosses into a new tax bracket ever result in less total after-tax income than not receiving the raise?
   a) Yes, always
   b) No — more total income, even taxed partly at a higher marginal rate, still results in more after-tax income overall
   c) Only for self-employed individuals
   d) Only in certain states
3. True or False: A progressive tax bracket system taxes a person's entire income at their highest applicable bracket's rate.
4. What did the taxpayer's calculation confirm about crossing into the 30% bracket?
   a) Her entire income was taxed at 30%
   b) Only her top slice of income was taxed at 30%, and her total after-tax income was still higher than earning less
   c) She would have been better off earning less
   d) Tax brackets have no real effect on her income

**5. Answer Key**
1. b  2. b  3. False  4. b

**6. Answers and Explanations**
1. Income within a lower bracket continues to be taxed at that bracket's own rate, unaffected by income rising into a higher bracket.
2. Earning more, even partly taxed at a higher marginal rate, always results in more total after-tax income than earning less.
3. A progressive system taxes each bracket's portion of income at that bracket's own rate, not the entire income at the top rate.
4. The taxpayer's calculation confirmed only her top slice was taxed at 30%, and her overall after-tax income still increased from earning more.

**7. Key Takeaways**
- Progressive tax brackets tax each portion of income at that bracket's own rate, not the entire income at the highest applicable rate.
- Crossing into a higher bracket never reduces previously taxed income's rate retroactively.
- A raise or bonus, even one crossing into a new bracket, always increases total after-tax income compared to not receiving it.
- This directly debunks a common, costly misunderstanding about how progressive tax systems function.
