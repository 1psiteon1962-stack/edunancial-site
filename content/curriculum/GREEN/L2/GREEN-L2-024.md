## GREEN-L2-024
### Payroll Taxes for the Business Owner: The Employer Side

**1. Learning Objectives**
By the end of this lesson, the learner will be able to:
- Explain the employer's payroll tax responsibilities, distinct from employee withholding.
- Reinforce the BLUE-L2-022 payroll discussion from the employer's specific tax-liability perspective.
- Recognize employer payroll tax as an actual business cost, not merely a withholding pass-through.

**2. Core Content**
Building on BLUE-L2-022's introduction of payroll compliance, this lesson focuses specifically on the employer's own tax liability, distinct from the employee withholding discussed in Lesson 9. Beyond withholding and remitting the employee's own tax obligations, employers generally must also pay their own matching share of FICA taxes (Lesson 11) and federal and state unemployment taxes, representing genuine additional costs to the business beyond the employee's gross wages themselves.

This means the true cost of an employee to a business is generally higher than the employee's stated gross wage alone — echoing BLUE-L2-021's discussion of mapping the full financial picture before hiring a first employee, this lesson reinforces that employer-side payroll taxes are a real, budgetable cost of employment, not merely an administrative pass-through of the employee's own withholding.

**3. Worked Example**
Before hiring her first employee (echoing BLUE-L2-021), a business owner works with her accountant to calculate the true total cost of employment, including not just the stated wage, but her employer-matching FICA contribution and applicable unemployment tax obligations — finding the true total cost meaningfully exceeds the stated wage alone. This more complete calculation, rather than the wage figure in isolation, informs her actual budgeting and pricing decisions, ensuring she has genuinely accounted for the full cost of bringing on this new team member.

**4. Practice Quiz**
1. What must an employer generally pay, beyond withholding and remitting the employee's own tax obligations?
   a) Nothing further is required
   b) Their own matching share of FICA taxes and unemployment taxes
   c) Only the employee's income tax
   d) A one-time government fee with no ongoing obligation
2. Why is the true cost of an employee generally higher than their stated gross wage?
   a) It is not — the stated wage is always the complete cost
   b) Employer-side payroll taxes represent genuine additional costs beyond the gross wage
   c) Employees are never actually paid their stated wage
   d) Payroll taxes have no relationship to employment cost
3. True or False: Employer payroll tax obligations are merely an administrative pass-through with no actual cost to the business.
4. Why did the business owner calculate the true total cost of employment before hiring?
   a) She assumed the stated wage was the complete cost
   b) She recognized employer-side payroll taxes as a real, additional cost requiring accurate budgeting
   c) Payroll taxes do not apply to employers
   d) She had no interest in accurate budgeting

**5. Answer Key**
1. b  2. b  3. False  4. b

**6. Answers and Explanations**
1. Employers generally must pay their own matching FICA share and unemployment taxes, beyond withholding the employee's own obligations.
2. Employer-side payroll taxes represent genuine additional costs beyond the employee's stated gross wage.
3. Employer payroll taxes are a real, budgetable cost to the business, not merely an administrative pass-through.
4. The business owner's complete cost calculation ensured accurate budgeting that accounted for the full cost of employment.

**7. Key Takeaways**
- Employers generally must pay their own matching FICA share and unemployment taxes, beyond employee withholding.
- The true cost of an employee generally exceeds their stated gross wage due to these employer-side obligations.
- This reinforces BLUE-L2-021's full-financial-picture discussion from the specific employer payroll tax perspective.
- Accurate budgeting for hiring requires accounting for this full cost, not the wage figure alone.
