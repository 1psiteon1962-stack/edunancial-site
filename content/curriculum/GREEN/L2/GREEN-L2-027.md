## GREEN-L2-027
### Flexible Spending Accounts vs. HSAs: A Comparison

**1. Learning Objectives**
By the end of this lesson, the learner will be able to:
- Compare Flexible Spending Accounts (FSAs) and HSAs directly.
- Identify the key structural differences between the two.
- Apply this comparison to a hypothetical benefits selection scenario.

**2. Core Content**
Building directly on Lesson 26, this lesson compares HSAs to Flexible Spending Accounts (FSAs), another tax-advantaged account used for medical (or in some cases, dependent care) expenses. Unlike HSAs, FSAs are generally not tied to a high-deductible health plan requirement, but FSA funds generally must be used within the plan year (subject to limited rollover or grace period provisions in some plans) or are generally forfeited — a meaningfully different structure from the HSA's non-expiring, potentially investable balance discussed in Lesson 26.

This "use it or lose it" structure means FSA elections require more careful, deliberate estimation of expected annual medical expenses than an HSA contribution does, since overestimating could result in forfeited funds, while underestimating means missing out on some potential tax-advantaged savings. Some employers offer both account types for different purposes, though an individual generally cannot contribute to both an HSA and a general-purpose FSA in the same year under common plan structures, making this a genuine either-or decision for many employees during benefits enrollment.

**3. Worked Example**
During benefits enrollment, Amara (from Lesson 2) compares her employer's HSA-eligible high-deductible health plan option against a more traditional health plan paired with an FSA. Recognizing the FSA's "use it or lose it" structure would require her to estimate her annual medical expenses carefully, while the HSA's non-expiring balance would provide more flexibility if her actual expenses turned out lower than expected, she considers her own typically unpredictable annual medical costs as a specific factor favoring the HSA's more forgiving structure for her particular situation.

**4. Practice Quiz**
1. What is a key structural difference between an FSA and an HSA?
   a) They are functionally identical in every respect
   b) FSA funds generally must be used within the plan year or are forfeited, unlike an HSA's non-expiring balance
   c) HSAs always expire at year-end, unlike FSAs
   d) FSAs require a high-deductible health plan, unlike HSAs
2. Why does an FSA's "use it or lose it" structure require careful estimation?
   a) It does not require any estimation at all
   b) Overestimating expected expenses could result in forfeited funds
   c) FSA funds never expire, eliminating any need for estimation
   d) FSAs have no relationship to medical expenses
3. True or False: An individual can generally contribute to both an HSA and a general-purpose FSA in the same year under common plan structures.
4. Why did Amara consider the HSA's structure more favorable for her specific situation?
   a) She had perfectly predictable annual medical expenses
   b) Her unpredictable annual medical costs favored the HSA's more forgiving, non-expiring structure
   c) FSAs offer more flexibility than HSAs in every case
   d) She had no medical expenses at all

**5. Answer Key**
1. b  2. b  3. False  4. b

**6. Answers and Explanations**
1. FSA funds generally must be used within the plan year or are forfeited, unlike an HSA's non-expiring balance.
2. Overestimating expected expenses under an FSA's "use it or lose it" structure risks forfeiting unused funds.
3. Under common plan structures, an individual generally cannot contribute to both an HSA and a general-purpose FSA in the same year.
4. Amara's unpredictable medical expenses made the HSA's more forgiving, non-expiring structure a better fit for her situation.

**7. Key Takeaways**
- FSAs generally do not require a high-deductible health plan but generally follow a "use it or lose it" annual structure.
- HSAs generally require a high-deductible health plan but offer a non-expiring, potentially investable balance.
- FSA elections require careful estimation of annual expenses, given the forfeiture risk of overestimating.
- An individual generally cannot contribute to both an HSA and a general-purpose FSA simultaneously under common plan structures.
