## GREEN-L2-032
### Sales Tax vs. Income Tax vs. Property Tax: Three Different Systems

**1. Learning Objectives**
By the end of this lesson, the learner will be able to:
- Distinguish sales tax, income tax, and property tax at a conceptual level.
- Explain what triggers each type of tax.
- Reinforce the RED-L2-014 property tax discussion from a comparative perspective.

**2. Core Content**
Building on Lesson 31, this lesson compares three distinct tax systems more directly. Income tax (the primary focus of most of this level so far) is generally triggered by earning income. Sales tax is generally triggered by purchasing goods or services, calculated as a percentage of the transaction price and generally collected by the seller at the point of sale. Property tax (echoing RED-L2-014) is generally triggered by owning property, calculated based on the property's assessed value, and paid on a recurring basis regardless of whether the property generates any income.

Each system taxes a fundamentally different event — earning, spending, or owning — meaning a person's overall tax burden reflects the combined effect of all three (plus others, such as FICA, Lesson 11) operating simultaneously on different aspects of their financial life, not a single unified tax applied once. Understanding which system applies to a given situation helps clarify why, for example, a large purchase triggers sales tax regardless of a person's income level, while property tax continues regardless of whether that specific property is currently generating any income at all.

**3. Worked Example**
Making a major purchase, Julio (from Lesson 18) pays sales tax at the point of sale, a tax triggered by his spending act, entirely separate from the income tax he already paid on the earnings he used to make that purchase, and separate again from the property tax he pays annually on his home regardless of his income or spending in any given year. Recognizing these as three genuinely distinct systems, each triggered by a different event, helps Julio understand his overall tax burden as the sum of several different, independently operating tax categories, rather than a single combined tax.

**4. Practice Quiz**
1. What generally triggers sales tax?
   a) Owning property
   b) Purchasing goods or services
   c) Earning income
   d) Filing an annual tax return
2. What generally triggers property tax, echoing RED-L2-014?
   a) Purchasing goods or services
   b) Owning property, based on its assessed value, regardless of income generated
   c) Earning wages from an employer
   d) Filing a specific application each year
3. True or False: Income tax, sales tax, and property tax are all triggered by the exact same underlying event.
4. Why did Julio recognize his overall tax burden as the sum of several independently operating categories?
   a) All three taxes are triggered by the identical event
   b) Each tax type — income, sales, and property — is triggered by a genuinely different event in his financial life
   c) He only ever pays one type of tax
   d) Property tax has no relationship to owning property

**5. Answer Key**
1. b  2. b  3. False  4. b

**6. Answers and Explanations**
1. Sales tax is generally triggered by purchasing goods or services.
2. Property tax is generally triggered by owning property, based on assessed value, regardless of income generated.
3. Each tax type is triggered by a genuinely different underlying event — earning, spending, or owning.
4. Julio's recognition reflected the genuinely distinct triggering events behind income, sales, and property tax.

**7. Key Takeaways**
- Income tax is triggered by earning; sales tax is triggered by spending; property tax is triggered by owning property.
- Each system operates independently, taxing a different aspect of a person's financial life.
- Overall tax burden reflects the combined effect of all applicable systems operating simultaneously.
- This directly reinforces the RED-L2-014 property tax discussion from a comparative, multi-system perspective.
