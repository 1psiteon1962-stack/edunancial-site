## GREEN-L2-036
### Recordkeeping: What to Keep and for How Long

**1. Learning Objectives**
By the end of this lesson, the learner will be able to:
- Identify general categories of tax records worth retaining.
- Explain general guidelines for how long to retain different record types.
- Connect this concept directly to Lesson 35's audit discussion.

**2. Core Content**
Building directly on Lesson 35, good recordkeeping generally includes retaining income documentation (pay stubs, 1099s), receipts and documentation supporting claimed deductions or credits, and records of significant financial transactions relevant to future tax calculations (such as home purchase records relevant to a future sale, echoing RED-L2). General guidelines commonly suggest retaining tax returns and supporting documentation for a specific number of years after filing (commonly cited periods range from three to seven years, depending on the specific situation, such as whether income was underreported), though certain records — particularly those related to property purchases or long-term investments — may warrant retention for as long as the asset is held, plus the applicable retention period after its eventual sale.

Organized, accessible recordkeeping serves multiple purposes beyond audit preparedness (Lesson 35): it supports accurate tax preparation each year, helps substantiate deductions and credits actually claimed, and provides documentation needed for future transactions, such as calculating the correct tax basis when eventually selling a long-held asset.

**3. Worked Example**
Maintaining organized digital records throughout the year, a taxpayer keeps not only his annual tax returns and supporting documents for the commonly recommended multi-year period, but also retains all purchase records and improvement receipts for his home indefinitely, for as long as he owns it, recognizing that these records will be needed to accurately calculate his tax basis whenever he eventually sells the property. This proactive, categorized approach — separating routine annual records from long-term asset records requiring extended retention — reflects the practical application of this lesson's general guidelines.

**4. Practice Quiz**
1. What is a general category of records worth retaining for tax purposes?
   a) Only receipts for entertainment with no tax relevance
   b) Income documentation and receipts supporting claimed deductions or credits
   c) Records with no relationship to any tax filing
   d) Only records from the current calendar month
2. Why might property-related records warrant retention for longer than the standard multi-year guideline?
   a) Property records have no ongoing relevance
   b) They may be needed to calculate the correct tax basis whenever the asset is eventually sold
   c) Property records should be discarded immediately after purchase
   d) Property has no relationship to future tax calculations
3. True or False: All tax records should be retained for the exact same length of time, regardless of the type of record or asset involved.
4. Why did the taxpayer retain his home purchase and improvement records indefinitely, beyond the standard guideline?
   a) He had no plans to ever sell his home
   b) These records would be needed to calculate his correct tax basis whenever he eventually sells the property
   c) Property records have no tax relevance
   d) He was required to discard them after a fixed period regardless of ownership status

**5. Answer Key**
1. b  2. b  3. False  4. b

**6. Answers and Explanations**
1. Income documentation and receipts supporting claimed deductions or credits are general categories worth retaining.
2. Property-related records may be needed to calculate the correct tax basis whenever the asset is eventually sold.
3. Retention periods vary by record type, with property and long-term investment records often warranting extended retention.
4. The taxpayer's extended retention of home-related records reflected the need to accurately calculate tax basis at eventual sale.

**7. Key Takeaways**
- Good recordkeeping includes income documentation, deduction/credit support, and significant transaction records.
- General guidelines suggest retaining most tax records for three to seven years, depending on the specific situation.
- Property and long-term investment records often warrant retention for as long as the asset is held, plus the standard period after sale.
- Organized recordkeeping supports accurate preparation, audit readiness (Lesson 35), and future transaction calculations.
