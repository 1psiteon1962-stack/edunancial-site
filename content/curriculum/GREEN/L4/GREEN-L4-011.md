# Entity Choice Tax Analysis: Why Sole Prop, LLC, S-Corp, and C-Corp Are Taxed Differently

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a central bank raising its benchmark interest rate sharply and repeatedly over a short stretch of months to fight rising inflation. As borrowing costs rise and self-employed business owners look for every available way to manage their overall tax burden, some discover that simply changing their business's legal and tax structure, without changing anything about the underlying business itself, can meaningfully affect their total tax bill. This lesson examines why that is true.

## Learning Objectives
- Explain the basic tax treatment of a sole proprietorship.
- Explain how an S-corporation election can affect the self-employment tax treatment of business income.
- Explain the double taxation concept associated with a traditional C-corporation.
- Explain why entity choice is a genuine, business-specific tax planning decision rather than a one-size-fits-all answer.

## Core Content
A sole proprietorship is the simplest, default business structure for an individual owner with no formal entity election, in which all business income and expenses are reported directly on the owner's personal tax return, and the owner pays self-employment tax (Lesson 13) on the full net business income, in addition to ordinary income tax. VERIFIED FACT: an LLC (limited liability company) is a state-law legal entity that, for federal tax purposes, is generally taxed as a sole proprietorship (if it has a single owner) or a partnership (if it has multiple owners) by default, though an LLC can elect to be taxed as an S-corporation or C-corporation instead, meaning "LLC" describes a legal liability structure, not automatically a distinct federal tax treatment.

Here is the "why" an S-corporation election can meaningfully affect the self-employment tax treatment of business income, connecting directly to Lesson 13's self-employment tax analysis: an S-corporation owner who actively works in the business must pay themselves a "reasonable salary" (subject to payroll taxes, similar to any employee), but any additional business profit distributed beyond that reasonable salary is generally not subject to self-employment tax in the same way it would be for a sole proprietorship or a default-taxed LLC — this specific structural difference is why many actively working business owners with meaningful profit above a reasonable salary level consider an S-corporation election specifically to reduce their overall self-employment tax burden, REGION-SPECIFIC RULE and important caution, since the IRS requires the salary paid to genuinely be "reasonable" for the work performed, and aggressively low salaries specifically designed to minimize payroll tax exposure can draw scrutiny and be challenged.

This is why a traditional C-corporation faces the distinct "double taxation" issue: a C-corporation pays corporate-level income tax on its profits, and then, if those after-tax profits are distributed to shareholders as dividends, the shareholders pay a second layer of tax on those dividends on their own individual returns (connecting to the dividend tax treatment covered in Lesson 17), meaning C-corporation profits that are ultimately distributed to owners can face two separate layers of taxation, unlike the single layer of tax that applies to pass-through entities' income (Lesson 10's QBI deduction discussion). This is why entity choice is a genuine, business-specific tax planning decision, not a single universally correct answer for every business: the right choice depends on factors including the business's profit level, whether profits will be retained in the business or distributed to owners, the owner's need for certain fringe benefits more readily available through a C-corporation structure, and the specific tax rates and rules applicable in a given year, meaning this decision generally warrants guidance from a qualified tax professional familiar with the specific business's circumstances.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: A business owner operating as a sole proprietorship with $150,000 in net business income pays self-employment tax on the entire $150,000. The same owner, after electing S-corporation tax treatment for an LLC with identical business economics, pays themselves a reasonable salary of $80,000 (subject to payroll tax) and takes the remaining $70,000 as a distribution not subject to self-employment tax, producing a meaningful reduction in the owner's total self-employment and payroll tax burden compared to the sole proprietorship structure, even though the underlying business generated the exact same $150,000 in profit under both structures.

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** This direct pass-through to the owner's personal return, combined with full self-employment tax exposure on net income, is the defining tax treatment of a sole proprietorship.

**Q2.** This specific salary-versus-distribution structural distinction is precisely why an S-corporation election can reduce an actively working owner's total self-employment tax exposure.

**Q3.** This two-layer taxation of corporate profits ultimately distributed to shareholders is the specific issue the term 'double taxation' describes.

**Q4.** The genuine variation in relevant factors across different businesses is precisely why this decision requires case-by-case, professionally guided analysis rather than a one-size-fits-all rule.

**Q5.** This direct contrast in how the identical $150,000 of business income was taxed under each structure illustrates precisely the self-employment tax planning benefit an S-corporation election can provide for an actively working owner.

## Key Takeaways
- A sole proprietorship's income passes directly to the owner's personal return, with the owner paying self-employment tax on the full net income.
- An S-corporation election can reduce self-employment tax exposure, since only a reasonable salary is subject to payroll tax, while additional distributed profit generally is not.
- A traditional C-corporation faces double taxation: corporate-level tax on profits, plus individual-level tax on any dividends distributed from those profits.
- Entity choice is a genuine, business-specific tax planning decision depending on profit level, distribution plans, and other factors, generally warranting professional guidance.
