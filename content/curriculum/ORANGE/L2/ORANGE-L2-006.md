## ORANGE-L2-006
### CAC vs. LTV: The Ratio That Determines Business Viability

**1. Learning Objectives**
By the end of this lesson, the learner will be able to:
- Explain the general relationship between CAC and LTV.
- Apply a commonly referenced CAC-to-LTV ratio guideline.
- Connect this concept to BLUE-L2-012's break-even discussion.

**2. Core Content**
Building directly on Lessons 4–5, this lesson examines the relationship between CAC and LTV as a core determinant of a customer-acquisition strategy's actual viability. A business spending more to acquire a customer (CAC) than that customer will ever generate in value (LTV) is engaged in an unsustainable pattern, regardless of how much sales volume that spending produces — echoing BLUE-L2-012's break-even discussion, now applied specifically to the economics of customer acquisition rather than overall business operations.

A commonly referenced general guideline suggests an LTV-to-CAC ratio of at least 3:1 (LTV at least three times CAC) as a rough indicator of a reasonably healthy, sustainable acquisition strategy, though this is a general guideline, not a universal rule, and the appropriate ratio can vary by industry, business model, and specific growth stage — a business intentionally acquiring customers at a lower near-term ratio while investing heavily in early growth, for example, might have valid strategic reasons for temporarily accepting a less favorable ratio.

**3. Worked Example**
Comparing her calculated CAC (Lesson 4) and LTV (Lesson 5), a business owner finds her ratio falls well below the commonly referenced 3:1 guideline, indicating her current acquisition strategy may not be sustainable at scale, even though she is successfully generating sales volume. Rather than continuing to scale her current marketing spend without addressing this imbalance, she works to either reduce her CAC (through more efficient marketing channels, Lesson 21) or increase her LTV (through improved retention, Lesson 33, or upselling, Lesson 35), recognizing that sales volume alone, without a sustainable CAC-to-LTV relationship, does not indicate genuine business health.

**4. Practice Quiz**
1. What does an unsustainable CAC-to-LTV relationship generally involve?
   a) Spending less to acquire a customer than that customer will ever generate in value
   b) Spending more to acquire a customer than that customer will ever generate in value
   c) A relationship with no bearing on business sustainability
   d) A guaranteed profitable outcome regardless of the specific figures
2. What is a commonly referenced general LTV-to-CAC ratio guideline?
   a) 1:1
   b) At least 3:1 (LTV at least three times CAC)
   c) 100:1
   d) There is no commonly referenced guideline at all
3. True or False: Every business must always maintain the exact same LTV-to-CAC ratio, regardless of industry, business model, or growth stage.
4. Why did the business owner reconsider her acquisition strategy despite successfully generating sales volume?
   a) Sales volume alone was sufficient evidence of genuine business health
   b) Her CAC-to-LTV ratio fell well below the general guideline, indicating a potentially unsustainable pattern despite the volume
   c) She had no interest in her business's long-term sustainability
   d) Her ratio already exceeded the general guideline significantly

**5. Answer Key**
1. b  2. b  3. False  4. b

**6. Answers and Explanations**
1. An unsustainable relationship involves spending more to acquire a customer than that customer will ever generate in value.
2. A commonly referenced general guideline suggests an LTV-to-CAC ratio of at least 3:1.
3. The appropriate ratio can vary by industry, business model, and specific growth stage, rather than being universal.
4. The business owner's below-guideline ratio indicated a potentially unsustainable acquisition pattern, despite her sales volume.

**7. Key Takeaways**
- A sustainable acquisition strategy generally requires LTV to exceed CAC by a meaningful margin, not merely generate sales volume.
- A commonly referenced guideline suggests an LTV-to-CAC ratio of at least 3:1, though this varies by context.
- This echoes BLUE-L2-012's break-even discussion, now applied specifically to customer acquisition economics.
- Addressing an unfavorable ratio can involve reducing CAC or increasing LTV, not simply continuing to scale spending regardless.
