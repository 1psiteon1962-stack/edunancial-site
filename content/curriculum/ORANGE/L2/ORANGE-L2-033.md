## ORANGE-L2-033
### Customer Retention vs. Customer Acquisition: Where to Invest

**1. Learning Objectives**
By the end of this lesson, the learner will be able to:
- Compare customer retention and customer acquisition investment.
- Connect this concept directly to Lesson 6's CAC-to-LTV discussion.
- Apply this comparison to a hypothetical budget allocation scenario.

**2. Core Content**
Building directly on Lessons 4–6, this lesson examines the relative value of investing in customer retention (keeping existing customers engaged and purchasing) versus customer acquisition (attracting new customers). Retaining an existing customer is commonly observed to be less expensive than acquiring a new one, since an existing customer already trusts the business (echoing Lesson 32) and does not require the full awareness-to-decision journey (Lesson 2) a new prospect requires — directly connecting to Lesson 5's LTV discussion, since improving retention directly increases LTV, improving the CAC-to-LTV ratio (Lesson 6) without necessarily changing CAC at all.

This does not mean acquisition investment is unimportant — a business needs new customers to grow beyond its existing base — but it does mean a business allocating its entire marketing budget toward acquisition while neglecting retention may be pursuing a less efficient growth strategy than one that balances both, particularly if the business's retention has room for improvement.

**3. Worked Example**
Reviewing her marketing budget allocation, a business owner discovers she has been investing almost exclusively in customer acquisition while allocating minimal resources toward retention efforts (customer support quality, ongoing engagement, loyalty programs). Recognizing that improving her retention would directly increase her LTV (Lesson 5) and improve her CAC-to-LTV ratio (Lesson 6) without requiring any change to her acquisition spending, she reallocates a portion of her budget toward retention-focused initiatives, recognizing this as a more balanced, potentially more efficient growth strategy than her previous acquisition-exclusive approach.

**4. Practice Quiz**
1. What is commonly observed about the relative cost of retaining an existing customer versus acquiring a new one?
   a) Retention is always more expensive than acquisition
   b) Retaining an existing customer is commonly less expensive than acquiring a new one
   c) There is no cost difference between the two
   d) Acquisition costs nothing at all
2. How does improving retention connect directly to Lesson 6's CAC-to-LTV discussion?
   a) Retention has no relationship to LTV or CAC
   b) Improving retention directly increases LTV, improving the CAC-to-LTV ratio without necessarily changing CAC
   c) Retention only affects CAC, never LTV
   d) Retention and CAC-to-LTV are unrelated concepts
3. True or False: A business should always invest exclusively in acquisition, with no attention to retention, regardless of its current retention performance.
4. Why did the business owner reallocate a portion of her budget toward retention?
   a) She wanted to eliminate all acquisition spending entirely
   b) Improving retention would increase her LTV and improve her CAC-to-LTV ratio without requiring changes to her acquisition spending
   c) Retention has no relationship to her business's overall growth efficiency
   d) She had no interest in balancing her marketing budget

**5. Answer Key**
1. b  2. b  3. False  4. b

**6. Answers and Explanations**
1. Retaining an existing customer is commonly observed to be less expensive than acquiring a new one.
2. Improving retention directly increases LTV, improving the CAC-to-LTV ratio without necessarily requiring any change to CAC.
3. A balanced approach considering both acquisition and retention is generally described as more efficient than an acquisition-exclusive strategy.
4. The business owner's reallocation appropriately recognized retention's direct positive effect on her overall growth efficiency.

**7. Key Takeaways**
- Retaining an existing customer is commonly less expensive than acquiring a new one.
- Improving retention directly increases LTV, improving the CAC-to-LTV ratio without necessarily changing CAC.
- A balanced investment in both acquisition and retention is generally more efficient than an acquisition-exclusive approach.
- This connects directly to Lessons 4–6's CAC and LTV discussions, applying them to budget allocation decisions.
