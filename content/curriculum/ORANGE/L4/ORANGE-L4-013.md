# Net Promoter Score Analysis: Why a Single Number Provides a Useful Signal but an Incomplete Picture

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a widespread public health disruption halting global shipping and manufacturing for an extended stretch, snarling supply chains across many industries at once. As a widespread disruption disrupts customer experience across many industries, businesses tracking a single customer satisfaction number discover that this one figure, while useful, does not by itself explain why customers feel the way they do or what specifically should be done about it.

## Learning Objectives
- Explain how Net Promoter Score (NPS) is calculated, based on customers classified as promoters, passives, and detractors.
- Explain what a specific NPS score does and does not directly reveal about genuine customer sentiment.
- Explain why NPS should generally be paired with qualitative follow-up, such as an open-ended 'why' question, to be genuinely useful for improvement.
- Explain why comparing NPS scores across different industries without adjustment can be misleading, since typical baseline scores vary considerably by industry.

## Core Content
Net Promoter Score (NPS) is a customer sentiment metric based on a single survey question asking customers how likely they are, on a scale of 0 to 10, to recommend a business to others, with respondents classified into three groups: promoters (scoring 9 or 10, generally considered genuinely enthusiastic, loyal customers), passives (scoring 7 or 8, generally considered satisfied but not particularly enthusiastic), and detractors (scoring 0 through 6, generally considered dissatisfied customers at meaningful risk of churning or actively discouraging others). VERIFIED FACT: NPS is calculated by subtracting the percentage of detractors from the percentage of promoters, producing a single score ranging from negative 100 to positive 100.

Here is the "why" a specific NPS score does and does not directly reveal about genuine customer sentiment: NPS provides a useful, simple, and easily trackable directional signal of overall customer sentiment over time, and a business can meaningfully track whether its own score is improving or declining across successive measurement periods, but the single number itself does not directly reveal why customers feel the way they do, meaning a declining score signals that something has changed, without independently identifying what that something actually is.

This is why NPS should generally be paired with qualitative follow-up, such as an open-ended "why" question, to be genuinely useful for actual improvement: asking respondents to briefly explain the specific reason behind their given score provides the qualitative context the numeric score alone cannot supply, allowing a business to identify specific, actionable themes behind a declining score (a specific product issue, a specific service concern, a specific competitive comparison) rather than simply knowing that sentiment has broadly declined without any further insight into the actual underlying cause — this is precisely why a business relying on the bare NPS number alone, without this kind of accompanying qualitative context, generally has limited ability to translate the score into any specific, targeted improvement action. This is why comparing NPS scores across different industries without adjustment can be misleading: OPINION and a widely recognized limitation, typical baseline NPS scores vary considerably by industry, reflecting genuine differences in typical customer expectations and the inherent nature of different categories of products and services, meaning a specific NPS score considered quite strong within one particular industry might be considered comparatively weak within a different industry with a higher typical baseline, meaning a business should generally benchmark its own NPS primarily against its own historical trend and genuinely comparable industry peers, rather than against an arbitrary, universal numeric standard applied identically across every possible industry and business type.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: A business notices its NPS score has declined significantly over the past two quarters but, relying only on the bare numeric score, has no specific insight into why. After adding an open-ended "why did you give this score" follow-up question to its survey, the business discovers a recurring, specific theme among detractors: dissatisfaction with a recently changed shipping policy, a specific, actionable finding the numeric score alone had never revealed. Reverting the shipping policy change directly and measurably improves the subsequent NPS score, illustrating precisely why pairing the numeric metric with qualitative follow-up enabled a targeted, effective improvement the bare number alone could not have identified.

## Key Takeaways
- NPS is calculated by subtracting the percentage of detractors from the percentage of promoters, based on a 0-10 recommendation likelihood question.
- The numeric score provides a useful directional sentiment signal but does not, by itself, reveal why customers feel the way they do.
- Pairing NPS with an open-ended 'why' follow-up question provides the qualitative context needed to identify specific, actionable improvement themes.
- Typical NPS baselines vary considerably by industry, making cross-industry comparison potentially misleading without adjustment for this variation.
