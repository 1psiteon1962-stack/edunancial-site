# Paid Advertising Return Analysis: Why Return on Ad Spend Requires Careful, Honest Attribution

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a widespread public health disruption halting global shipping and manufacturing for an extended stretch, snarling supply chains across many industries at once. As a widespread disruption tightens marketing budgets and forces every advertising dollar to be justified with hard numbers, businesses relying on a specific paid channel's reported return on ad spend discover that this figure can be meaningfully inflated by attribution methodology, prompting a closer look at what ROAS actually measures and where it can mislead.

## Learning Objectives
- Explain how return on ad spend (ROAS) is calculated, and what a specific ROAS figure is intended to represent.
- Explain the difference between attributed revenue and genuinely incremental revenue, and why this distinction matters for interpreting ROAS correctly.
- Explain why a channel's reported ROAS can be inflated if it receives credit for sales that would have occurred anyway, without that specific advertising.
- Explain why testing a channel's genuine incremental contribution, such as through a controlled holdout test, provides a more reliable efficiency measure than reported ROAS alone.

## Core Content
Return on ad spend (ROAS) is a marketing metric calculated by dividing the revenue attributed to a specific advertising channel or campaign by the total amount spent on that same channel or campaign, generally expressed as a ratio (a ROAS of 4, for example, meaning $4 in attributed revenue for every $1 spent). VERIFIED FACT: ROAS is intended to represent how efficiently a specific advertising investment converts into revenue, but the specific way "attributed revenue" is actually calculated significantly affects how meaningful and reliable this figure genuinely is.

Here is the "why" the distinction between attributed revenue and genuinely incremental revenue matters for correctly interpreting ROAS: attributed revenue refers to the specific dollar amount a given attribution model (per this level's Lesson 6 discussion) assigns credit for to a specific channel or campaign, while genuinely incremental revenue refers to the revenue that would not have occurred at all in the absence of that specific advertising — these two figures can differ meaningfully, since a specific ad might receive full attribution credit for a sale from a customer who would have made that same purchase anyway, through some other channel or on their own initiative, even if that specific ad had never actually been shown to them.

This is why a channel's reported ROAS can be inflated if it receives credit for sales that would have occurred anyway, without that specific advertising: this is particularly likely to occur with advertising specifically targeting customers who already have strong existing intent to purchase (such as an ad targeting someone who has already searched directly for the business's own specific brand name), since this kind of advertising frequently receives attribution credit for a sale the customer was already quite likely to complete regardless of whether that specific ad had actually been shown, meaning the reported ROAS for this kind of already-high-intent targeting can appear considerably stronger than the advertising's genuine, incremental contribution actually justifies. This is why testing a channel's genuine incremental contribution, such as through a controlled holdout test (withholding the specific advertising from a randomly selected portion of the otherwise eligible audience and comparing that group's actual resulting revenue against a comparable group that did receive the advertising), provides a more reliable efficiency measure than reported ROAS alone: this kind of controlled comparison directly measures the genuine incremental difference in revenue the advertising actually caused, isolating that specific effect from revenue that would have occurred anyway regardless of the specific advertising, providing considerably more reliable insight into a channel's actual, genuine marginal contribution than an attribution-based ROAS figure that may be significantly inflated by crediting sales the advertising did not actually, incrementally cause.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: A business's advertising specifically targeting people who have already searched for its own brand name by name reports an impressively high ROAS of 8, based on standard attribution crediting. A controlled holdout test, withholding this specific advertising from a randomly selected portion of this already-high-intent audience, reveals that this group converted at nearly the same rate as the group that did see the ads, meaning most of the attributed revenue would have occurred anyway even without the advertising — the genuine incremental ROAS, once properly isolated, turns out to be considerably lower than the originally reported figure, illustrating precisely why attribution-based ROAS alone can meaningfully overstate a channel's genuine efficiency.

## Key Takeaways
- ROAS is calculated by dividing attributed revenue by advertising spend, intended to measure advertising efficiency.
- Attributed revenue and genuinely incremental revenue can differ meaningfully, since attribution can credit sales that would have occurred anyway.
- Advertising targeting already-high-intent customers is particularly prone to inflated ROAS, since it often receives credit for sales it did not actually cause.
- A controlled holdout test provides a more reliable measure of genuine incremental contribution than attribution-based ROAS alone, by directly isolating the advertising's actual causal effect.
