# Loyalty Programme Analysis: Why the Right Reward Structure Depends on What Actually Drives Repeat Behaviour

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a populous nation's real estate and consumer credit markets, after years of rapid expansion, beginning to show signs of a sharp correction as defaults start rising. As a credit-fueled boom unwinds and businesses look for cost-effective ways to encourage repeat business from increasingly price-conscious customers, many discover that a generic, one-size-fits-all loyalty programme structure fails to genuinely motivate their own specific customer base's actual repeat purchasing behaviour.

## Learning Objectives
- Explain the general purpose of a customer loyalty programme, and why it specifically targets repeat purchasing behaviour rather than initial acquisition.
- Explain the difference between a simple points-based programme, a tiered status programme, and a paid membership programme, and the genuinely different customer motivations each structure appeals to.
- Explain why a loyalty programme's genuine effectiveness depends on understanding what actually motivates a specific customer base's repeat behaviour, rather than defaulting to a generic, commonly used structure.
- Explain why a poorly designed loyalty programme can become a pure cost centre providing discounts to customers who would have repurchased anyway, echoing this level's Lesson 22 incremental-versus-attributed revenue discussion.

## Core Content
A customer loyalty programme is a structured incentive system specifically designed to encourage and reward repeat purchasing behaviour from existing customers. VERIFIED FACT: this distinguishes loyalty programmes from customer acquisition-focused marketing efforts (per this level's earlier lessons), since a loyalty programme's specific purpose is retaining and increasing engagement from customers a business has already acquired, rather than attracting entirely new customers.

Here is the "why" points-based, tiered status, and paid membership programmes appeal to genuinely different customer motivations: a simple points-based programme (earning points redeemable for future discounts or rewards) appeals primarily to price-sensitive customers motivated by tangible financial value, a tiered status programme (unlocking increasingly exclusive benefits or recognition at higher engagement levels) appeals additionally to customers motivated by status, exclusivity, and recognition beyond pure financial value, and a paid membership programme (charging an upfront fee in exchange for ongoing specific benefits) appeals specifically to customers who value convenience and are willing to commit financially upfront in exchange for guaranteed ongoing benefits, meaning these three structures are not simply different variations of the same underlying appeal, but rather appeal to genuinely different underlying customer motivations.

This is why a loyalty programme's genuine effectiveness depends on understanding what actually motivates a specific customer base's repeat behaviour, rather than defaulting to a generic, commonly used structure: a business whose specific customer base is genuinely motivated primarily by status and recognition may find a simple points-based programme insufficiently compelling, while a business whose customer base is genuinely motivated primarily by straightforward financial value may find an elaborate tiered status programme's non-financial benefits comparatively unappealing, meaning sound loyalty programme design generally requires genuine research into what actually motivates that specific business's own particular customer base, rather than simply adopting whatever loyalty programme structure happens to be commonly used within that general industry. This is why a poorly designed loyalty programme can become a pure cost centre providing discounts to customers who would have repurchased anyway, echoing this level's Lesson 22 discussion: similar to the concern Lesson 22 raised regarding advertising receiving credit for sales that would have occurred anyway, a loyalty programme that simply provides ongoing discounts to a business's most loyal, already-repeat-purchasing customers, without genuinely influencing any incremental behaviour beyond what those customers would have done regardless, functions as a pure cost with no genuine incremental behavioural benefit, meaning a well-designed loyalty programme should be evaluated, similar to advertising ROI, based on its genuine incremental effect on customer behaviour, not merely on how much loyalty programme activity or point redemption it happens to generate among customers who may well have remained loyal regardless of the programme's existence.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: A business initially adopts a generic, industry-standard points-based loyalty programme, but discovers through direct customer research that its own specific customer base is actually motivated considerably more by exclusive access and status recognition than by the modest financial discounts the points programme provides. Redesigning the programme around a tiered status structure, offering increasingly exclusive access and recognition at higher engagement levels, produces meaningfully stronger genuine engagement than the original generic points-based structure had, illustrating precisely why loyalty programme design should reflect genuine insight into what actually motivates a specific customer base, rather than defaulting to a commonly used, generic structure.

**Q2.** This genuine distinction in underlying customer motivation across these three programme types is precisely why they are not simply interchangeable variations of the same basic appeal.

## Key Takeaways
- A loyalty programme specifically encourages and rewards repeat purchasing behaviour from existing customers, distinct from acquisition-focused marketing.
- Points-based, tiered status, and paid membership programmes appeal to genuinely different underlying customer motivations, from financial value to status recognition to convenience.
- Loyalty programme effectiveness depends on understanding what genuinely motivates a specific customer base, rather than defaulting to a generic, commonly used structure.
- A poorly designed programme can become a pure cost centre by rewarding customers who would have repurchased anyway, echoing the earlier concern about attributing credit for behaviour that would have occurred regardless.
