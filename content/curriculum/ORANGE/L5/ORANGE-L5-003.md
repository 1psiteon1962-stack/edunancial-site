# Customer Acquisition Cost, Lifetime Value, and Financing

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a systemically important financial institution suddenly revealing a large, previously undisclosed loss, raising fears about counterparty risk rippling through the broader banking system.

Apply it here: After a major bank's losses shake confidence, investors stop funding fast-growing startups that spend heavily on marketing. Ask across colors: How long does it take this company to earn back what it spends to win a customer (ORANGE/BLUE)? Can it survive without new investment (WHITE)? What happens if customers leave faster than expected?

## Learning Objectives
- Calculate customer acquisition cost and lifetime value
- Explain payback period and why it matters for cash
- Connect marketing spending to financing risk

## Core Content
Two numbers help decide whether marketing spending builds a business or drains it:

- Customer Acquisition Cost (CAC): Total sales and marketing spending in a period divided by the number of new customers gained.
- Customer Lifetime Value (LTV): The total gross profit a customer is expected to generate over the time they stay. A simple version: average monthly gross profit per customer x average number of months a customer stays.

ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE of the ratio: If CAC is $200 and LTV is $800, the LTV:CAC ratio is 4:1. Many businesses consider a ratio well above 1:1 necessary, though the right target depends on the industry. ASSUMPTION/OPINION.

But the ratio alone is not enough. Payback period matters too: how many months until a customer's gross profit repays their CAC. A business with a great LTV:CAC ratio but a 24-month payback needs a lot of cash to grow.

How colors connect:
- BLUE: Every new customer costs cash upfront. Faster growth means more cash out before cash comes back. Businesses often finance this gap with investor money or loans.
- WHITE: When investors pull back, as in the scenario, businesses that depend on outside money to cover long payback periods are at risk.
- GREEN: REGION-SPECIFIC RULE: Marketing costs are often deductible when incurred, which can help cash flow if the business is profitable.
- PURPLE: Subscription terms, cancellation rules, and auto-renewal laws affect how long customers actually stay.
- BLACK: Leaders must decide how fast to grow based on available cash, not just opportunity.

Churn (the rate at which customers leave) is the hidden variable. If churn rises from 3% to 6% per month, average customer life can fall by about half, cutting LTV sharply.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: In tight funding markets, businesses with shorter payback periods and lower churn tend to survive. Growth that depends on constant new investment is fragile.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: Two meal-kit companies each spend $100,000 per month on marketing.

Company A: CAC = $150. Monthly gross profit per customer = $25. Average customer stays 12 months. LTV = $300. Payback period = 6 months. LTV:CAC = 2:1.

Company B: CAC = $150. Monthly gross profit per customer = $15. Average customer stays 30 months. LTV = $450. Payback period = 10 months. LTV:CAC = 3:1.

Company B looks better on the ratio. But when investors stop funding growth, Company B needs 10 months to recover each customer's cost, and its customers stay long only if churn stays low. Company A recovers costs in 6 months.

Then churn rises for both. Company B's average customer life falls from 30 months to 15, cutting LTV to $225 (1.5:1). Company A's falls from 12 to 8 months, cutting LTV to $200 (1.3:1), but it still pays back its CAC within 6 months.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Company A reduces marketing spending and survives on its own cash. Company B must raise money on poor terms. Payback period and churn mattered as much as the headline ratio.

Q2: C
Q3: A
Q4: A
Q5: B

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** CAC measures what it costs to win each customer.

**Q2.** Payback period shows how long cash is tied up.

**Q3.** Customers who leave faster produce less total profit.

**Q4.** Short payback reduced dependence on outside money.

**Q5.** Dependence on outside money creates vulnerability.

## Key Takeaways
- Compare CAC with LTV, but also check payback period
- Churn can cut lifetime value quickly
- Long payback periods require financing that may disappear
- Grow at a pace your cash can support
