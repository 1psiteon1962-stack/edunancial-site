# Negotiating a Business Acquisition with the Whole Board in View

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a large trading bloc suddenly imposing steep tariffs on a broad category of imported manufactured goods, raising costs for companies that depend on those imports as inputs.

Apply it here: New tariffs make a small import business less profitable, and its owner wants to sell. A buyer sees opportunity. Ask across colors: What is each side's best alternative (ORANGE)? How should price be split between cash, seller financing, and an earnout (BLUE)? Which legal protections matter most (PURPLE)? Which tax structure benefits both sides (GREEN)?

## Learning Objectives
- Apply BATNA and anchoring to an acquisition negotiation
- Trade across price, terms, protections, and structure
- Negotiate in a way that preserves the post-closing relationship

## Core Content
At Level 4, ORANGE covered negotiation fundamentals: BATNA (Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement), anchoring, reservation price, and creating value through trade-offs. At Level 5, you use them in one of the most complex negotiations in business: buying a company.

What can be negotiated in an acquisition:
- Price (ORANGE/WHITE): Headline value.
- Payment form (BLUE): Cash at closing, seller financing, earnouts tied to future performance, or equity in the buyer's company.
- Structure (GREEN/PURPLE): Asset or share purchase (PURPLE-L5-023 in the law track), which affects both sides' taxes.
- Protections (PURPLE): Reps, warranties, indemnities, escrows, and caps (PURPLE-L5-021).
- Transition (BLACK): How long the seller stays to help, and in what role.
- Non-compete (PURPLE): Whether the seller can compete afterward.
- Employees (BLACK): Whether key employees stay and on what terms.

Value creation through trade-offs: Because buyers and sellers value different terms differently, a deal can be better for both than a simple price haggle. A seller who cares most about total price may accept an earnout. A buyer who cares most about limiting risk may pay a higher price in exchange for a larger escrow.

BATNA in an acquisition:
- Seller's BATNA: Another buyer, continuing to run the business, or winding it down.
- Buyer's BATNA: Another acquisition target, starting from scratch, or not buying.

A stronger BATNA gives more leverage. The scenario's tariffs may weaken the seller's BATNA if continuing to operate is less attractive.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Squeezing a seller too hard can backfire. If the seller stays on for a transition or holds a seller note, a resentful seller can make the transition harder. Fair deals often produce better outcomes after closing.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: Tomas wants to buy an import business. The seller asks $1.2 million. Tariffs have cut profits.

BATNA analysis:
- Seller: No other serious buyers. Continuing means lower profits under tariffs. BATNA is weak.
- Tomas: Two other businesses under consideration, though less attractive. BATNA is moderate.

Tomas's offer: $950,000, structured as:
- $500,000 cash at closing.
- $250,000 seller note over 5 years at a fair rate.
- Up to $250,000 earnout if revenue recovers to pre-tariff levels within 2 years.
- Asset purchase for Tomas's tax benefit. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE.
- 10% escrow for 18 months.
- Seller stays 6 months as a paid consultant.

The seller counters at $1.1 million total. They settle at $1.05 million: $500,000 cash, $300,000 note, and up to $250,000 earnout. The seller receives more total value if the business recovers. Tomas limits his upfront cash and shares risk with the seller.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: The earnout bridged their disagreement about the future. The seller believed recovery was likely; Tomas was unsure. The earnout let both be right.

## Practice Quiz
**Q1. What does BATNA stand for?**
A) Best Available Tax And Note Agreement
B) Basic Agreement To Negotiate Always
C) Business Asset Transfer And Negotiation Act
D) Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement

**Q2. How can a deal be better for both sides than a simple price haggle?**
A) By trading terms that each side values differently
B) By lying
C) By ignoring taxes
D) By avoiding lawyers

**Q3. How did the tariffs affect the seller's BATNA?**
A) Strengthened it
B) Eliminated the buyer's options
C) Had no effect
D) Weakened it, because continuing to operate became less attractive

**Q4. What role did the earnout play?**
A) It lowered taxes
B) It replaced the escrow
C) It bridged disagreement about whether the business would recover
D) It paid the buyer

**Q5. Why can squeezing a seller too hard backfire?**
A) Sellers cannot be squeezed
B) It raises taxes
C) A resentful seller holding a note or helping with transition can make things harder
D) It is illegal

## Answer Key
Q1: D
Q2: A
Q3: D
Q4: C
Q5: C

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** BATNA is your best option if the deal fails.

**Q2.** Value creation comes from differing priorities.

**Q3.** A weaker alternative reduces leverage.

**Q4.** Earnouts let buyers and sellers share uncertainty.

**Q5.** Post-closing relationships matter.

## Key Takeaways
- Acquisitions involve price, payment form, structure, protections, and transition
- BATNA determines leverage
- Trading differently valued terms creates value for both sides
- Fair deals often work better after closing
