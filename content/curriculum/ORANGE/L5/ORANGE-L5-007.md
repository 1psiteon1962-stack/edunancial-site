# Negotiating Real Estate with Seller Financing

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a central bank raising its benchmark interest rate sharply and repeatedly over a short stretch of months to fight rising inflation.

Apply it here: With bank rates high, a buyer cannot afford a conventional loan on a small apartment building, and the seller cannot find other buyers. Ask across colors: What does each side really need (ORANGE)? Could seller financing at a lower rate make the deal work (BLUE/RED)? What documents protect the seller (PURPLE)? How might installment treatment affect the seller's taxes (GREEN)?

## Learning Objectives
- Identify each side's priorities in a seller-financed deal
- Trade price against rate, down payment, and term
- Structure a deal that protects the seller and works for the buyer

## Core Content
At Level 2, RED introduced creative financing. At Level 3, BLUE covered seller financing for business acquisitions. At Level 5, ORANGE shows how to negotiate these deals so both sides win.

In seller financing, the seller acts as the lender, receiving payments over time instead of all cash at closing.

What each side often wants:
- Buyer: Lower down payment, lower interest rate, longer term, flexibility.
- Seller: Higher total price, reliable payments, security, possible tax advantages from spreading the gain over time (REGION-SPECIFIC RULE), and sometimes a steady income stream in retirement.

Key negotiating variables:
- Price
- Down payment
- Interest rate
- Term and amortization (including balloon payments)
- Prepayment rights
- Security and personal guarantees

The trade-off principle: A seller may accept a lower interest rate in exchange for a higher price, or a lower price in exchange for a larger down payment. A buyer may accept a higher price in exchange for a lower rate that makes monthly payments affordable.

How colors connect:
- RED: The property's income must support the payments.
- BLUE: Compare the seller's rate with bank rates. When bank rates are high, seller financing can be a major advantage for buyers.
- PURPLE: Promissory note, mortgage or deed of trust, and default terms (PURPLE-L5-024). Check whether the seller has an existing mortgage with a due-on-sale clause.
- GREEN: Sellers may be able to spread the tax on the gain over the payment period. Interest must often meet minimum rates. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE.
- WHITE: For the seller, the note becomes an investment. Compare its expected return and risk with other options.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: In high-rate markets, seller financing can turn a stalled sale into a deal. The key is focusing on what each side truly needs, not just the headline price.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: Rosa, 68, owns a 6-unit building free and clear. She wants to retire and has listed it at $900,000 for months with no acceptable offers. Bank rates are high.

Ben wants to buy but can only afford payments if the rate is lower than banks offer.

Negotiation:
- Rosa's priorities: Total value, reliable monthly income, and spreading taxes over time.
- Ben's priorities: Affordable monthly payments and a manageable down payment.

Deal:
- Price: $920,000 (higher than her asking price).
- Down payment: 20% ($184,000).
- Seller note: $736,000 at 5.5% (below bank rates), 25-year amortization with a 10-year balloon.
- Security: Recorded mortgage on the property. Ben provides a limited personal guarantee.
- Default: 30-day cure period.

Rosa receives more total value and steady monthly income. Ben gets affordable payments. Her CPA explains how installment treatment may spread her taxes. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: A price of $920,000 with a 5.5% rate worked better for both than $880,000 with bank financing Ben could not afford.

## Key Takeaways
- Seller financing makes the seller a lender
- Negotiate price, down payment, rate, term, and security together
- In high-rate markets, seller financing can unlock deals
- Focus on what each side truly needs
