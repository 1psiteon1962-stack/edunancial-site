# Communicating Through a Supply Shock

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a widespread public health disruption halting global shipping and manufacturing for an extended stretch, snarling supply chains across many industries at once.

Apply it here: A widespread disruption halts shipping, and a company cannot fill orders on time. Ask across colors: What should customers be told, and when (ORANGE)? What do contracts promise about delivery (PURPLE)? Should limited inventory go to the most profitable customers or the most loyal ones (BLUE/BLACK)? Should prices change?

## Learning Objectives
- Communicate honestly and early during supply disruptions
- Coordinate messaging with contract obligations
- Make fair allocation and pricing decisions under scarcity

## Core Content
When supply is disrupted, customers experience it as broken promises: late deliveries, missing products, or sudden price changes. How a business communicates can decide whether customers stay loyal or leave permanently.

Communication principles:
- Early: Tell customers as soon as you know about a problem, not when they discover it.
- Honest: Explain what happened, what you know, and what you do not know.
- Specific: Give realistic timelines, even if they are ranges.
- Frequent: Update regularly, even when there is no new news.
- Options: Offer alternatives, such as substitute products, partial shipments, or refunds.

Allocation under scarcity: When there is not enough product for everyone, businesses must decide who gets what. Options include:
- First come, first served.
- Prioritizing long-term or contract customers.
- Proportional allocation (each customer receives the same percentage of their order).
- Prioritizing essential uses.

Each choice sends a message about company values.

How colors connect:
- PURPLE: Contracts may require delivery by certain dates. Force majeure clauses (PURPLE-L5-039 in the law track) may excuse delays if the event is covered and notice rules are followed. Notice deadlines matter.
- BLUE: Inventory, cash flow, and supplier relationships determine what can be delivered.
- ORANGE: Pricing during shortages must be handled carefully. Large increases during emergencies may be restricted in some places and can damage trust. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE.
- BLACK: Leadership decides allocation principles and communicates them consistently.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Customers often remember how a business treated them during a crisis long after they forget the crisis itself.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: Hugo's company supplies packaging to food producers. A shipping disruption cuts his supply by 40% for three months.

His response:
- Day 1: Sends customers a clear message: "We expect a 40% supply shortfall for about 3 months. Here is what we know, what we are doing, and what to expect."
- Contracts (PURPLE): His attorney confirms the disruption is covered by the force majeure clause in most contracts and helps send required notices within the deadline.
- Allocation: Hugo allocates supply proportionally, so each customer receives 60% of their usual order. Customers producing essential foods receive priority for a small reserve.
- Options: He offers substitute packaging from a second supplier at a small price increase, clearly explained.
- Updates: Weekly emails with new information.
- Pricing: He passes on only his actual extra costs for substitute materials, not a crisis premium.

After the disruption, most customers stay. Several praise his communication. A competitor who stayed silent and raised prices sharply loses several accounts to Hugo.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Hugo could not prevent the disruption. He could control how he treated customers during it.

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** Early communication builds trust.

**Q2.** Proportional allocation spreads shortages evenly.

**Q3.** Notice requirements must be followed.

**Q4.** Fair pricing preserved relationships. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE.

**Q5.** Crisis behavior shapes long-term loyalty.

## Key Takeaways
- Communicate early, honestly, specifically, and frequently
- Allocation choices send messages about company values
- Follow contract notice rules, including force majeure
- Customers remember how you treated them in a crisis
