# Currency Risk in International Pricing

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a mid-sized economy's currency losing a large share of its value against major reserve currencies over a few months, driven by capital fleeing the country and eroding investor confidence.

Apply it here: A company sells abroad and prices in its customers' currency. Then its customers' currency loses a third of its value. Ask across colors: What happens to the company's revenue when converted back home (WHITE)? Should prices be raised in the foreign market (ORANGE)? Do contracts allow price changes (PURPLE)? Can financial tools reduce this risk (BLUE)?

## Learning Objectives
- Explain how exchange-rate changes affect international sales
- Compare pricing in home currency vs. customer currency
- Use contract and financial tools to manage currency risk

## Core Content
When a business sells in another country, it faces currency risk: the chance that exchange-rate changes will reduce the value of its sales.

Pricing currency choices:
- Price in your home currency: You know exactly what you will receive. But customers bear the currency risk, and your prices may become unaffordable if their currency weakens.
- Price in the customer's currency: Easier for customers, often more competitive. But you bear the currency risk.
- Price in a third, stable reserve currency: Common in some international trade. Shifts risk to both sides in different ways.

What happens when the customer's currency falls (as in the scenario):
- If you price in their currency: Your revenue in home-currency terms drops.
- If you price in your currency: Your prices rise sharply for customers, and demand may fall.

Either way, someone absorbs the impact.

Tools for managing currency risk:
- Contract clauses (PURPLE): Currency adjustment clauses and shared bands (PURPLE-L5-039 in the law track).
- Pricing reviews (ORANGE): Regular price updates for foreign markets.
- Natural hedging (BLUE): Matching costs and revenues in the same currency, such as buying supplies or paying local staff in the currency you earn.
- Financial hedging (WHITE/BLUE): Tools such as forward contracts that lock in exchange rates for future transactions. These have costs and complexity and are often used with professional guidance.
- Payment timing: Collecting payment faster reduces exposure time.

GREEN connection: REGION-SPECIFIC RULE: Currency gains and losses may have tax consequences.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Small exporters often ignore currency risk until a large move hurts them. A simple policy, such as reviewing foreign prices quarterly and using contract bands, can prevent most painful surprises.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: Pedro's company exports specialty tools. He sells to customers in a foreign market, priced in their currency. At the start, 10 units of their currency equal 1 unit of his.

He sells $100,000 worth (in his currency) per quarter.

The foreign currency falls 33%, so 15 units now equal 1 unit of his currency. The same foreign-currency revenue now converts to about $67,000.

His options:
- Raise foreign prices 50% to restore his revenue. Customers may buy less.
- Keep prices and accept lower revenue.
- Mix: raise prices 20%, reduce costs, and accept some margin loss.

He chooses the mix. For the future, he:
- Adds contract clauses sharing currency moves beyond 10%.
- Opens a small local warehouse and pays some costs in the foreign currency (natural hedge).
- Uses forward contracts for large known orders, with guidance from his bank.
- Reviews foreign pricing quarterly.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Pedro could not prevent the currency move. But with these tools in place, the next move will hurt far less.

## Key Takeaways
- International sales carry currency risk
- The pricing currency decides who bears the risk
- Use contract clauses, natural hedges, financial hedges, and faster payment
- Review foreign pricing regularly
