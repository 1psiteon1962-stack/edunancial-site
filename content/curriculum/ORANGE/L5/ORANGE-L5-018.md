# Customer Concentration Risk

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a systemically important financial institution suddenly revealing a large, previously undisclosed loss, raising fears about counterparty risk rippling through the broader banking system.

Apply it here: A major financial institution's losses cause its business clients to cut spending. A supplier gets 60% of its revenue from one of those clients. Ask across colors: What happens to cash flow if that client cuts orders (BLUE)? How much negotiating power does the supplier really have (ORANGE)? How do lenders and buyers view this dependence (WHITE)? What do the contracts guarantee (PURPLE)?

## Learning Objectives
- Measure customer concentration
- Explain how concentration affects negotiating power, financing, and valuation
- Build a plan to diversify while protecting key accounts

## Core Content
Customer concentration means a large share of revenue comes from a few customers. It can feel like success: big customers bring big orders. But it creates serious risks.

Risks of concentration:
- Cash flow shock: If a major customer leaves, delays payments, or cuts orders, revenue can fall sharply overnight.
- Negotiating power: Large customers know you depend on them. They may demand lower prices, longer payment terms, or special terms.
- Payment risk: If a major customer gets into financial trouble, your receivables may become hard to collect.
- Financing: Lenders may limit credit or require tighter terms for concentrated businesses. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE: Some lenders limit how much of a borrowing base can come from a single customer's receivables.
- Valuation: Buyers and investors often discount businesses with high concentration because of the risk (WHITE).

Measuring concentration: What percentage of revenue comes from your largest customer? Your top 5? ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Many buyers become cautious when a single customer exceeds roughly 20% to 25% of revenue, though standards vary by industry.

How colors connect:
- ORANGE: Diversifying requires new marketing and sales efforts.
- BLUE: Cash reserves should reflect concentration risk.
- PURPLE: Longer-term contracts, minimum purchase commitments, and termination notice periods can reduce risk from key customers.
- WHITE: Lower concentration generally supports higher valuations.
- BLACK: Leadership must balance serving key accounts well with investing in growth elsewhere.

Diversifying does not mean abandoning big customers. It means growing other revenue so no single customer can sink the business.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: The best time to diversify is while the big customer is still happy. Diversifying in a panic after losing them is much harder.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: Elena's printing company earns $2 million per year. Her largest client, a regional bank, provides $1.2 million (60%).

The scenario hits. The bank cuts its marketing budget and reduces orders by half. Elena's revenue drops $600,000.

Her lender notices the drop and tightens her credit line, citing concentration.

Elena's plan (which she wishes she had started earlier):
- Protect the key account: She meets with the bank, offers a cost-saving package, and negotiates a 12-month contract with minimum order commitments.
- Diversify: She targets healthcare and education clients, which are less affected by financial sector cuts.
- Cash: She builds reserves to cover 6 months of fixed costs.
- Contracts (PURPLE): New client contracts include 90-day termination notice periods.
- Goal: No single client above 25% within three years.

Three years later, her largest client is 22% of revenue. When she explores selling the company, buyers value it more highly than they would have before.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Elena's concentration felt like success until the bank cut back. Diversification turned her business from fragile to resilient.

## Key Takeaways
- Concentration creates cash flow, negotiating, payment, financing, and valuation risk
- Measure revenue share from top customers
- Use contracts to add stability with key accounts
- Diversify before you need to
