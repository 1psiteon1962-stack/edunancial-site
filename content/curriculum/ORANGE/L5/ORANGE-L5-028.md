# Enterprise Sales Cycles and Cash Flow

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a systemically important financial institution suddenly revealing a large, previously undisclosed loss, raising fears about counterparty risk rippling through the broader banking system.

Apply it here: A banking scare makes large companies delay purchasing decisions. A supplier's pipeline of big deals suddenly stalls. Ask across colors: How long can the supplier survive while deals are delayed (BLUE)? How should forecasts be adjusted (ORANGE)? What contract and payment terms reduce risk (PURPLE)? How much should the supplier depend on a few big deals (WHITE)?

## Learning Objectives
- Explain how long sales cycles affect cash flow
- Forecast sales using weighted pipelines
- Negotiate contract and payment terms that protect cash

## Core Content
Selling to large organizations (enterprise or B2B sales) often involves long cycles: many decision-makers, formal procurement processes, legal review, and budget approval. A single deal can take months or even more than a year.

Cash flow effects:
- Sales costs are paid long before revenue arrives.
- Large customers may demand long payment terms (60, 90, or more days).
- Delays in a few big deals can create major cash shortfalls.

Forecasting: A weighted pipeline estimates expected revenue by multiplying each deal's value by its probability of closing, based on its stage.

ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: A $500,000 deal at the proposal stage with a 30% historical close rate contributes $150,000 to the weighted forecast.

During uncertain times, probabilities and timelines should be adjusted downward.

Contract and payment terms:
- Deposits or upfront payments for large projects.
- Milestone payments tied to deliverables.
- Late payment interest or fees.
- Clear acceptance criteria to prevent disputes about whether work is complete.
- Limitation of liability and indemnity terms (PURPLE-L5-017 and -021 in the law track).

How colors connect:
- BLUE: Cash reserves and credit lines must cover long gaps. Some businesses use receivables financing. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE.
- PURPLE: Enterprise contracts often come with the customer's standard terms, which may be one-sided. Negotiating key terms matters.
- WHITE: Concentration in a few big deals increases risk (ORANGE-L5-018).
- BLACK: Leaders must plan for delays, not just successes.
- GREEN: REGION-SPECIFIC RULE: Revenue recognition for long contracts affects reported profits and taxes.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: A signed enterprise contract is good news. Cash in the bank is better news. Plan around the second.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: Yuki's software company sells to large hospitals. Her pipeline has 10 deals worth $3 million total.

Before the banking scare, her weighted forecast was $1.2 million for the next two quarters.

After the scare, hospitals delay decisions. Yuki adjusts:
- Reduces probabilities by one-third and pushes expected close dates back one quarter. New weighted forecast: $650,000 over three quarters.
- Cash plan (BLUE): Her reserves cover 5 months of costs. She draws a credit line as backup and slows hiring.
- Contracts (PURPLE): For new deals, she negotiates 30% upfront payments, milestone billing, and 45-day payment terms instead of the hospitals' standard 90 days. For one hospital that insists on 90 days, she adds a small price increase to cover the financing cost.
- Pipeline (ORANGE): She adds smaller clinics with shorter sales cycles to balance the big deals.

Several hospital deals eventually close, later than originally hoped. Yuki's company survives because she planned for delays.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Yuki's adjustments were not pessimistic. They were realistic, and realism kept her company alive.

**Q5.** Cash timing determines survival.

## Key Takeaways
- Enterprise sales cycles are long and delay cash
- Use weighted pipelines and adjust them for conditions
- Negotiate deposits, milestones, and shorter payment terms
- Plan for delays; cash in the bank matters most
