# Payment Terms and Trade Credit as Sales Tools

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a mid-sized sovereign government signaling it may be unable to make an upcoming bond payment, raising default risk on its debt.

Apply it here: A government debt crisis makes many businesses short on cash. Customers ask a supplier for longer payment terms. Ask across colors: Will longer terms win more sales (ORANGE)? Can the supplier afford to wait for payment (BLUE)? What happens if customers cannot pay (PURPLE)? How should credit decisions be made (BLACK)?

## Learning Objectives
- Explain how trade credit supports sales
- Evaluate the cash flow and collection risks of offering credit
- Set credit policies, check customers, and document terms

## Core Content
Trade credit means letting business customers buy now and pay later, such as "Net 30" (payment due in 30 days). It is one of the most common forms of business financing, and it is also a sales tool.

Why offer credit:
- Customers may buy more or choose you over competitors.
- Many large customers expect it.
- Longer terms can help customers during tight times, building loyalty.

Risks:
- Cash flow (BLUE): You pay your costs before customers pay you. Longer terms mean more cash tied up in receivables.
- Collection: Some customers pay late or not at all, especially during crises.
- Concentration: One large unpaid customer can cause serious problems.

Credit policy elements:
- Credit checks: Review references, payment history, and financial information before offering credit. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE on what credit information is available and how it may be used.
- Credit limits: Maximum amount a customer can owe at once.
- Terms: Due dates, early payment discounts (such as 2% if paid within 10 days), and late fees.
- Written agreements: Credit applications and terms signed by the customer, possibly with personal guarantees from owners of small customers (PURPLE-L5-009 in the law track).
- Security: For large amounts, a security interest in goods sold. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE.
- Monitoring: Track aging receivables and act early on late payments.

How colors connect:
- ORANGE: Credit terms can win sales, but should not replace value.
- BLUE: Receivables must be financed by cash or credit lines.
- PURPLE: Clear terms support collection (PURPLE-L5-041).
- GREEN: REGION-SPECIFIC RULE: Bad debts may be deductible when written off.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: A sale to a customer who never pays is worse than no sale. Credit policy protects the business from selling itself into a cash crisis.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: Andre's company distributes building supplies. During the debt crisis, customers ask for 60-day terms instead of 30.

Andre's analysis:
- Cash: Moving all customers from 30 to 60 days would tie up an extra $400,000 in receivables. His credit line cannot cover that.
- Risk: Some customers show slower payments already.

His credit policy:
- Existing customers with strong payment histories: Offered 45-day terms with a 2% discount for payment within 10 days.
- Customers with weaker histories: Keep 30-day terms, with lower credit limits.
- New customers: Credit application, references, and a personal guarantee from owners of small businesses.
- Large orders: A security interest in delivered materials until paid. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE.
- Monitoring: Weekly review of overdue accounts. Calls at 5 days late, holds on new orders at 30 days late.

Results: Several strong customers pay early to get the discount, improving cash. Two weaker customers default, but losses are limited by credit limits and guarantees.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Andre used credit terms to help good customers without letting risky customers put his business in danger.

## Key Takeaways
- Trade credit can win sales but ties up cash and adds collection risk
- Use credit checks, limits, written terms, guarantees, and security
- Match terms to customer risk
- Monitor receivables and act early on late payments
