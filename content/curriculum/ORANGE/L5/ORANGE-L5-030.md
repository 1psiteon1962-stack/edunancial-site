# Winning Government and Institutional Contracts

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a mid-sized sovereign government signaling it may be unable to make an upcoming bond payment, raising default risk on its debt.

Apply it here: A government facing a debt crisis delays payments to its suppliers. A small company that depends on government contracts faces a cash crunch. Ask across colors: What do the contract's payment terms actually say (PURPLE)? How much cash reserve is needed for delayed government payments (BLUE)? How should the company balance public and private customers (ORANGE/WHITE)?

## Learning Objectives
- Describe how public procurement generally works
- Identify compliance and contract obligations in government sales
- Evaluate the risks and benefits of government and institutional customers

## Core Content
Governments, schools, hospitals, and other institutions buy large amounts of goods and services. Selling to them can bring large, stable contracts. But the process and obligations differ from private sales.

How public procurement generally works (REGION-SPECIFIC RULE):
- Opportunities are often posted publicly.
- Buyers issue requests for proposals or bids with detailed requirements.
- Suppliers submit formal proposals, often with strict formats and deadlines.
- Evaluation may be based on price, quality, experience, and compliance.
- Some programs set aside contracts for small or disadvantaged businesses.

Obligations:
- Compliance: Registration, certifications, insurance, labor standards, and reporting requirements.
- Contract terms: Often standard and non-negotiable. May include audit rights, termination for convenience (the buyer can end the contract without fault), and strict performance requirements.
- Ethics: Strict rules against gifts, conflicts of interest, and improper contact during bidding. Violations can bring serious penalties.

How colors connect:
- PURPLE: Contract terms, compliance, and ethics rules.
- BLUE: Government payments can be reliable but slow, and in fiscal crises, delayed. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE: Some jurisdictions have prompt payment rules with interest for late payment.
- ORANGE: Winning bids requires understanding evaluation criteria and presenting value clearly.
- WHITE: Government contracts can reduce some risks (steady demand) but add others (political and fiscal risk).
- BLACK: Bidding takes significant time. Leaders must choose opportunities carefully.
- GREEN: REGION-SPECIFIC RULE: Some government contracts have specific tax or reporting requirements.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Government contracts can be excellent anchors for a business, but concentration risk (ORANGE-L5-018) applies here too. A government in fiscal trouble may pay late, reduce budgets, or cancel programs.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: Musa's company provides IT support. It wins a 3-year contract with a regional government agency worth $1.5 million per year, now 60% of revenue.

The debt crisis hits. The agency delays payments by 90 days, then 120 days.

Musa's position:
- Contract (PURPLE): The contract allows termination for convenience and has no late payment interest. But his jurisdiction has a prompt payment law that may require interest on late government payments. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE. His attorney files the required claims.
- Cash (BLUE): His reserves cover 3 months. He draws on a credit line and negotiates with his own suppliers.
- Diversification (ORANGE): He accelerates marketing to private clients and a hospital system.

Lessons he applies afterward:
- Keeps government contracts below 35% of revenue.
- Builds reserves covering at least 6 months of costs.
- Reviews payment terms and termination rights before bidding.
- Follows strict ethics rules in all bidding contacts.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: The government contract was valuable, but relying on it too heavily turned a payment delay into a threat to Musa's business.

## Key Takeaways
- Government and institutional sales involve formal bids and strict compliance
- Contracts often include audit rights and termination for convenience
- Payment can be reliable but slow, and fiscal crises add risk
- Avoid over-dependence; build reserves and diversify
