# Pricing Power and Competitive Moats

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a central bank raising its benchmark interest rate sharply and repeatedly over a short stretch of months to fight rising inflation.

Apply it here: Inflation rises, and every business faces higher costs. Some can raise prices easily; others lose customers when they try. Ask across colors: What makes one company's customers stay (ORANGE)? Are its advantages legally protected (PURPLE)? How do investors value companies with strong moats (WHITE)? How does pricing power protect cash flow (BLUE)?

## Learning Objectives
- Define pricing power and competitive moats
- Identify common sources of durable advantage
- Connect moats to valuation, strategy, and inflation resilience

## Core Content
Pricing power is a business's ability to raise prices without losing many customers. It is one of the clearest signs of a strong business, especially during inflation.

Pricing power usually comes from a competitive moat: a durable advantage that protects a business from competitors, like a moat around a castle.

Common sources of moats:
- Brand: Customers trust and prefer the brand (ORANGE-L5-010).
- Switching costs: Changing to a competitor is costly, time-consuming, or risky for customers (for example, business software deeply built into operations).
- Network effects: The product becomes more valuable as more people use it (marketplaces, communication platforms).
- Cost advantages: The business can produce at lower cost due to scale, location, or processes.
- Legal protection: Patents, trademarks, licenses, or regulatory approvals (PURPLE).
- Unique assets: Locations, relationships, or data that competitors cannot easily copy.

How colors connect:
- WHITE: Investors often pay higher valuations for businesses with durable moats because future profits are more predictable. WHITE-track lessons on valuation apply here.
- BLUE: Pricing power protects margins and cash flow when costs rise.
- PURPLE: Legal protections can reinforce moats, but most expire or can be challenged.
- ORANGE: Marketing strengthens brand moats and communicates switching value.
- BLACK: Leaders must invest to maintain moats. Moats erode if neglected.

Moats are rarely permanent. Technology changes, competitors adapt, and customer preferences shift.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: A good test of pricing power: if you raised prices 10% tomorrow, what share of customers would leave? If very few, you likely have a moat. If many, your advantage may be weaker than you think.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: Three businesses face 12% cost increases from inflation.

Business A: Accounting software used daily by small firms. Switching would require moving years of data and retraining staff. It raises prices 10%. Very few customers leave.

Business B: A local bakery with a loyal following and a strong brand. It raises prices 8%. Most regulars stay; some price-sensitive customers buy less often.

Business C: A generic office supply reseller competing mainly on price. It tries raising prices 8% and loses many customers to cheaper online sellers. It reverses the increase and absorbs costs, cutting margins.

Analysis:
- Business A: Strong switching-cost moat.
- Business B: Moderate brand moat.
- Business C: Little or no moat.

An investor comparing the three would likely value A at the highest multiple of earnings, B in the middle, and C lowest (WHITE).

Business C's strategy response: build a moat by specializing in a niche (supplies for dental offices), offering automatic reordering that creates convenience and switching costs, and improving service. ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Moats can be built deliberately, but it takes time and investment.

**Q1. What is pricing power?**
A) The ability to lower prices
B) The ability to raise prices without losing many customers
C) A government price rule
D) A tax rate

**Q2. What is a switching cost?**
A) A shipping fee
B) The cost, time, or risk customers face when changing to a competitor
C) A tax
D) A discount

**Q3. What are network effects?**
A) A product becoming more valuable as more people use it
B) Internet speed
C) Employee networks
D) Advertising reach

**Q4. Why did Business C lose customers when raising prices?**
A) It competed mainly on price and had little moat
B) Its products were illegal
C) It raised prices too little
D) It had strong switching costs

**Q5. What test of pricing power does the lesson suggest?**
A) Counting employees
B) Measuring store size
C) Checking tax rates
D) Asking what share of customers would leave after a 10% price increase

## Answer Key
Q1: B
Q2: B
Q3: A
Q4: A
Q5: D

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** Pricing power signals a strong business.

**Q2.** High switching costs keep customers.

**Q3.** Network effects strengthen with growth.

**Q4.** Without a moat, customers switch easily.

**Q5.** Customer response reveals moat strength.

## Key Takeaways
- Pricing power reflects a durable competitive moat
- Moats come from brand, switching costs, network effects, cost advantages, legal protection, and unique assets
- Moats support higher valuations and inflation resilience
- Moats must be maintained and can be built deliberately
