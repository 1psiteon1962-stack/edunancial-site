# Customer Research Before Committing Capital

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a large trading bloc suddenly imposing steep tariffs on a broad category of imported manufactured goods, raising costs for companies that depend on those imports as inputs.

Apply it here: New tariffs raise the cost of imported products just as an entrepreneur plans to launch a private-label product. Ask across colors: Has real customer demand been tested (ORANGE)? What minimum order and capital are required (BLUE)? How crowded is the market? Would a lower-capital model reduce risk while testing the idea?

## Learning Objectives
- Validate customer demand before large investments
- Evaluate minimum order quantities, capital needs, and competition
- Recognize when to pivot or walk away

## Core Content
Many businesses fail not because the founders worked too little, but because they invested heavily before confirming that enough customers would pay enough to cover costs.

Validation steps before committing capital:
1. Customer interviews: Talk to potential customers about their problems and current solutions.
2. Demand tests: Pre-orders, waitlists, landing pages, or small test sales.
3. Competition review: How many competitors exist? What do they charge? How are they positioned?
4. Unit economics: Price, cost per unit, margins, and customer acquisition cost (ORANGE-L5-003).
5. Capital requirements: Minimum order quantities (MOQs), inventory, equipment, and marketing costs.

Minimum order quantity (MOQ) is the smallest order a supplier will accept. For private-label products, MOQs can require large upfront inventory purchases, tying up capital and creating risk if products do not sell.

A curriculum case study, drawn from the author's family: a young founder planned a private-label dog food company. Research revealed high MOQs, significant capital requirements, and a crowded, competitive market with established brands. He made the sound decision to walk away from that model, and pivoted to a website and service-based business with much lower capital needs. This was not failure. It was disciplined decision-making.

How colors connect:
- ORANGE: Customer research and competitive positioning.
- BLUE: Capital requirements, MOQs, and cash flow. The BLUE track uses this case study for capital constraints and low-capital models.
- PURPLE: Supplier contracts, product regulations (especially for food, pet food, and health products), and labeling. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE.
- WHITE: Compare the expected return of the venture with the risk and alternative uses of capital.
- BLACK: The "fall back and regroup" principle applies. Walking away from a weak opportunity preserves resources for a better one.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: The cheapest mistakes are the ones caught during research, before money is spent.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: Selena plans to launch a private-label line of reusable water bottles.

Her research:
- Customer interviews: 30 people. Most like the idea, but few say they would pay more than existing brands.
- Demand test: A landing page with a pre-order option. 2% of visitors pre-order.
- Competition: Dozens of established brands with strong reviews and low prices.
- MOQ: The manufacturer requires 5,000 units minimum. With tariffs, cost is $6 per unit: $30,000 upfront.
- Unit economics: At a competitive $18 price, after marketing, shipping, and platform fees, her margin per bottle is only about $3.

Her conclusion: It would take 10,000 sales to recover her investment, in a crowded market with weak demand signals.

Instead, she pivots to a lower-capital model: custom-printed bottles for local businesses and events, ordered only after customers pay deposits, using a supplier with low MOQs. Her upfront risk falls dramatically.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Selena's research saved her from a $30,000 inventory bet. Her pivot let her test demand with customers' money instead of her own.

## Key Takeaways
- Validate demand, competition, and unit economics before investing
- MOQs and capital requirements can create large upfront risk
- Walking away from a weak model is disciplined, not failure
- Test demand with low-capital models where possible
