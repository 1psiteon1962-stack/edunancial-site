# Launch Sequencing and Capital Runway

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a central bank raising its benchmark interest rate sharply and repeatedly over a short stretch of months to fight rising inflation.

Apply it here: Rising interest rates make funding harder to find. A startup has enough money for 12 months. Ask across colors: Should it launch everywhere at once or in stages (ORANGE)? How long will the money last under each plan (BLUE)? What milestones would convince investors to fund the next stage (WHITE)? What contracts and legal steps are needed at each stage (PURPLE)?

## Learning Objectives
- Explain capital runway and burn rate
- Design a staged launch that matches available capital
- Use milestones to guide further investment

## Core Content
Runway is how long a business can operate before running out of money. It is calculated by dividing available cash by monthly burn rate (net cash spent per month).

ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: $300,000 in cash and a $25,000 monthly burn gives 12 months of runway.

Launching everything at once (all products, all markets, full marketing) burns cash quickly. If results disappoint, there may be no money left to adjust. Staged launches reduce this risk.

A staged launch approach:
1. Stage 1: Minimum viable offering in a small test market. Goal: prove customers will buy and learn what works.
2. Stage 2: Refine and expand to a larger market segment once key metrics are met.
3. Stage 3: Scale marketing, channels, and markets once unit economics are proven.

Each stage has milestones: specific results that must be achieved before moving on (for example, a target customer acquisition cost, repeat purchase rate, or monthly revenue).

How colors connect:
- BLUE: Runway, burn rate, and cash planning. Staging extends runway.
- WHITE: Investors often fund the next stage when milestones are met. In tight funding markets, proving results with less money is especially valuable.
- ORANGE: Each stage produces customer insight that improves the next.
- PURPLE: Each stage may require new contracts, registrations, or compliance steps (for example, entering a new region, ORANGE-L5-014).
- BLACK: Leaders must resist the temptation to scale before evidence supports it.

The fall back and regroup principle: If a stage misses its milestones, the business can adjust, pivot, or pause before spending everything.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: In uncertain funding environments, the goal is not to launch as big as possible. It is to learn as much as possible per dollar spent.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: Ines has $360,000 to launch a healthy snack brand. Funding markets are tight.

Plan A (launch everywhere): National online launch, three product lines, heavy advertising. Burn: $60,000/month. Runway: 6 months.

Plan B (staged):
- Stage 1 (months 1 to 4): One product line, sold online and in 10 local stores. Burn: $20,000/month. Milestones: 30% repeat purchase rate, customer acquisition cost under $25, and positive store feedback.
- Stage 2 (months 5 to 9): If milestones are met, add a second product and expand to 50 stores in the region. Burn: $35,000/month.
- Stage 3: If Stage 2 succeeds, seek outside funding to scale nationally, using proven results.

Stage 1 results: Repeat rate is 35%, but customer acquisition cost is $40, too high. Ines learns that in-store sampling works far better than online ads. She shifts spending before Stage 2.

By month 9, she has spent $260,000, has proven results, and still has runway. An investor funds Stage 3 based on her data.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Under Plan A, Ines might have run out of money before learning that sampling worked better than ads.

## Key Takeaways
- Runway equals cash divided by burn rate
- Staged launches extend runway and reduce risk
- Use milestones to decide when to invest more
- Learn as much as possible per dollar spent
