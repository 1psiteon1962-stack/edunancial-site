# Negotiating with Suppliers: Price, Terms, and Minimums

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a large trading bloc suddenly imposing steep tariffs on a broad category of imported manufactured goods, raising costs for companies that depend on those imports as inputs.

Apply it here: New tariffs raise costs from a company's main supplier. The company must renegotiate or find alternatives. Ask across colors: What leverage does the company have (ORANGE)? Can it negotiate longer payment terms to protect cash (BLUE)? What contract terms protect against future price shocks (PURPLE)? Should it diversify suppliers to reduce risk?

## Learning Objectives
- Apply negotiation skills to supplier relationships
- Negotiate price, payment terms, minimums, and quality together
- Reduce supplier concentration and price risk

## Core Content
ORANGE usually focuses on selling. But negotiating with suppliers uses the same skills from the other side of the table, and it directly affects margins and cash flow.

What can be negotiated with suppliers:
- Price: Unit costs, volume discounts, and price adjustment formulas.
- Payment terms: Longer terms (such as Net 60 instead of Net 30) help cash flow (ORANGE-L5-029 in reverse).
- Minimum order quantities: Lower MOQs reduce inventory risk (ORANGE-L5-038).
- Quality standards: Specifications, inspection rights, and remedies for defects.
- Delivery: Lead times, reliability, and penalties for delays.
- Exclusivity: Whether the supplier can sell to competitors, or whether you must buy only from them.
- Risk sharing: Price adjustment clauses, currency bands, and force majeure (PURPLE-L5-039 in the law track).

Sources of leverage:
- Volume and growth potential.
- Reliable payment history.
- Alternative suppliers (your BATNA).
- Long-term commitment.
- Willingness to be flexible on terms the supplier values (such as larger orders at predictable times).

How colors connect:
- BLUE: Payment terms and MOQs directly affect working capital.
- PURPLE: Supply agreements, quality terms, and dispute resolution.
- WHITE: Supplier concentration is a risk, just like customer concentration.
- BLACK: Supplier relationships built on fairness tend to hold up better in crises.
- GREEN: REGION-SPECIFIC RULE: Import duties and tariffs affect landed cost and may depend on product origin.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Squeezing suppliers to the limit may win a better price today but can leave you without support when supply is scarce. The best supplier relationships are partnerships where both sides can succeed.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: Joaquin's company makes outdoor furniture. His main supplier provides 80% of his aluminum. Tariffs raise costs 18%.

His BATNA: He identifies two alternative suppliers in regions not affected by the tariffs, though with longer lead times.

Negotiation with his main supplier:
- He shares his growth plan and offers a 12-month volume commitment.
- In exchange, he asks for: a price increase of 10% instead of 18%, payment terms extended from 30 to 45 days, and a lower MOQ for specialty parts.
- He proposes a price adjustment clause tied to a published aluminum index, so both sides share future changes fairly.

The supplier agrees to a 12% increase, 45-day terms, and the index clause, but keeps the MOQ.

Diversification: Joaquin shifts 25% of his purchases to an alternative supplier to reduce concentration risk.

Tax: His CPA confirms how tariffs apply based on the origin of each supplier's materials. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Joaquin's alternative suppliers gave him leverage, his volume commitment gave the supplier value, and the index clause made the relationship more stable for both.

## Key Takeaways
- Negotiate price, terms, MOQs, quality, and risk sharing together
- Alternative suppliers create leverage
- Supplier concentration is a risk; diversify
- Fair partnerships hold up better in crises
