# Upselling and Cross-Selling Within Ethical Limits

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a central bank raising its benchmark interest rate sharply and repeatedly over a short stretch of months to fight rising inflation.

Apply it here: With rising rates, a company's new customer growth slows, and leadership pushes staff to sell more to existing customers. Ask across colors: Which additional products truly help customers (ORANGE)? Could pressure to hit targets lead to unsuitable sales (BLACK)? What legal limits apply (PURPLE)? How do ethical practices affect long-term customer value (BLUE/WHITE)?

## Learning Objectives
- Distinguish helpful upselling from harmful pressure
- Design cross-selling that increases customer value
- Connect ethical selling to legal compliance and long-term revenue

## Core Content
Upselling means offering a better or larger version of what the customer is buying. Cross-selling means offering related products. Both can increase customer lifetime value (ORANGE-L5-003) without the cost of finding new customers.

When done well, they help customers: a warranty that genuinely protects them, an accessory they will actually use, or an upgrade that saves them money over time.

When done poorly, they harm customers and the business:
- Pressure tactics that push people into purchases they do not need.
- Hidden add-ons or pre-checked boxes.
- Selling products unsuitable for the customer's situation.
- Sales targets so aggressive they encourage employees to cut corners.

How colors connect:
- ORANGE: Upselling works best when it solves a real customer problem.
- PURPLE: Consumer protection laws may prohibit deceptive practices, hidden fees, and unauthorized add-ons. Financial and insurance products often have suitability rules (ORANGE-L5-027). REGION-SPECIFIC RULE.
- BLACK: Compensation and targets shape behavior (ORANGE-L5-008). Leaders who push unrealistic cross-selling targets can create cultures where employees mislead customers.
- BLUE/WHITE: Short-term revenue from pressure selling can be followed by refunds, complaints, penalties, and lost customers, harming long-term value.

Principles for ethical upselling:
1. Understand the customer's needs first.
2. Recommend only what genuinely fits.
3. Explain costs and benefits clearly.
4. Make saying no easy.
5. Measure success by customer outcomes, not just sales.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: The best upsell is one the customer thanks you for later. The worst is one they complain about to others.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: Two bike shops try to increase revenue per customer.

Shop A sets aggressive targets: every sale must include an extended warranty and at least two accessories. Staff are paid bonuses per add-on. Customers begin complaining about pressure. Some find warranties added without clear explanation. Online reviews decline, and a few customers file complaints with a consumer protection agency.

Shop B trains staff to ask questions first: How will you use the bike? How far do you ride? Where will you store it?
- Commuters are shown lights, locks, and fenders, explaining safety and theft risks.
- Occasional riders are told a basic tune-up plan may be enough, without an extended warranty.
- All add-ons are explained with prices, and customers choose freely.
- Staff bonuses are based on customer satisfaction scores and repeat visits, not number of add-ons.

After a year, Shop B's average sale is slightly lower than Shop A's, but its repeat customer rate and referrals are much higher. Its total revenue grows faster.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Shop B earned more by helping customers buy what they actually needed.

## Key Takeaways
- Upselling and cross-selling can raise customer value when they fit real needs
- Pressure, hidden add-ons, and unsuitable sales harm customers and the business
- Consumer protection and suitability rules apply
- Measure success by customer outcomes
