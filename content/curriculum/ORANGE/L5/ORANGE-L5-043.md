# Measuring Marketing ROI After Taxes and Time

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a central bank raising its benchmark interest rate sharply and repeatedly over a short stretch of months to fight rising inflation.

Apply it here: With interest rates high, a company must cut its budget and wants to keep only the marketing that truly pays. Ask across colors: Which campaigns bring profitable customers, not just sales (ORANGE)? How does the time until payback matter when money is expensive (WHITE/BLUE)? How do taxes affect the true cost and return (GREEN)?

## Learning Objectives
- Calculate marketing ROI using gross profit, not revenue
- Include customer lifetime, time value, and taxes
- Recognize attribution limits in marketing measurement

## Core Content
Many businesses measure marketing by revenue: "This campaign brought in $100,000 in sales." But revenue is not profit. Level 5 measurement includes the whole board.

Basic marketing ROI:
ROI = (Gross profit from campaign - Campaign cost) / Campaign cost

ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: A $10,000 campaign brings $50,000 in sales at a 40% gross margin. Gross profit = $20,000. ROI = ($20,000 - $10,000) / $10,000 = 100%.

Improvements for Level 5:
- Customer lifetime: If customers keep buying after the first purchase, include expected future gross profit (ORANGE-L5-003), adjusted for churn.
- Time value of money (WHITE): Profit received in two years is worth less than profit today, especially when interest rates are high. Discounting future profits gives a more realistic picture.
- Taxes (GREEN): REGION-SPECIFIC RULE: Marketing costs are often deductible, reducing their after-tax cost for profitable businesses. Profits are taxed. After-tax comparisons can change rankings between options.
- Payback period (BLUE): How long until the campaign's cost is recovered? Faster payback reduces cash strain.

Attribution challenges:
- Customers often see several marketing touches before buying. Which one gets credit?
- Some marketing (brand building, content) works slowly and is hard to measure directly.
- Tools that track attribution are imperfect and affected by privacy limits (ORANGE-L5-021).

Practical approaches: Test campaigns against control groups when possible, track customer source at purchase, and compare trends over time rather than trusting any single number.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Precise marketing ROI is often impossible. Directionally correct measurement, including profit, time, and taxes, is far better than precise measurement of the wrong thing (revenue alone).

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: Maria compares two campaigns, each costing $20,000.

Campaign 1: Discount ads. Brings $80,000 in first-month sales at a 25% gross margin (because of the discount). Gross profit: $20,000. Few customers return.
ROI (first purchase only): ($20,000 - $20,000) / $20,000 = 0%.

Campaign 2: Educational content and email series. Brings $40,000 in first-month sales at a 45% margin. Gross profit: $18,000. But customers return: expected additional gross profit over two years is $30,000.

Adjustments:
- Time value: With high rates, Maria discounts the future $30,000 to about $26,000 in today's value. (Illustrative; the actual figure depends on the rate used and timing.)
- Campaign 2 lifetime gross profit (today's value): $18,000 + $26,000 = $44,000.
- ROI: ($44,000 - $20,000) / $20,000 = 120%.
- Taxes: Both campaigns' costs are deductible and profits taxed in her jurisdiction, so the ranking holds after taxes. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE.

Revenue made Campaign 1 look better. Profit, lifetime, and time value show Campaign 2 is far better. ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Maria shifts her budget toward Campaign 2's approach.

## Practice Quiz
**Q1. Why is revenue alone a poor measure of marketing success?**
A) Revenue is always wrong
B) Taxes do not apply
C) Revenue cannot be measured
D) Revenue is not profit; margins and costs matter

**Q2. How is basic marketing ROI calculated?**
A) (Gross profit - Campaign cost) / Campaign cost
B) Revenue divided by cost
C) Cost minus revenue
D) Sales times margin

**Q3. Why does the time value of money matter in marketing ROI?**
A) It does not
B) Future profits are worth less than profits today, especially when rates are high
C) It lowers taxes
D) It increases revenue

**Q4. What is an attribution challenge?**
A) A legal dispute
B) A tax problem
C) Deciding which marketing touch gets credit when customers see several
D) A pricing error

**Q5. Why did Campaign 2 perform better in Maria's analysis?**
A) Higher margins and repeat customers, even after discounting future profit
B) Higher first-month revenue
C) Lower costs
D) No taxes

## Answer Key
Q1: D
Q2: A
Q3: B
Q4: C
Q5: A

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** Profit, not revenue, shows real return.

**Q2.** ROI compares profit gained with cost.

**Q3.** Discounting future profits gives a realistic picture.

**Q4.** Multiple touches make credit hard to assign.

**Q5.** Lifetime profit made the difference.

## Key Takeaways
- Measure marketing by profit, not revenue
- Include customer lifetime, time value, and taxes
- Payback period matters when cash is expensive
- Attribution is imperfect; aim for directionally correct measurement
