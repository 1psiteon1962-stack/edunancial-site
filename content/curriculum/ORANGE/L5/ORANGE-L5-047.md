# Market Readiness: Building Capacity Before the Opportunity

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a major energy-producing region hit by a sudden regional conflict that knocks out a large share of its production capacity overnight, tightening global energy supply with little warning.

Apply it here: An energy shock suddenly creates huge demand for energy-efficient products. Some companies respond in days; others take months. Ask across colors: Did the fast movers have marketing materials, customer lists, and sales capacity ready (ORANGE)? Financing (BLUE)? Legal approvals and contracts (PURPLE)? Leadership decision criteria (BLACK)?

## Learning Objectives
- Explain why market readiness must be built in advance
- Build a market readiness checklist across colors
- Connect readiness to the ability to act decisively

## Core Content
The second part of the knife-fight doctrine is building readiness in advance. You cannot build a sales team, customer list, or brand in the week an opportunity appears. You can only use what you already built.

Market readiness checklist:
- ORANGE:
  - Owned customer relationships and consent-based contact lists (ORANGE-L5-019).
  - Marketing materials and messaging templates that can be adapted quickly.
  - Market intelligence: competitor tracking and customer feedback systems.
  - Sales processes documented, so new salespeople can be trained quickly.
- BLUE:
  - Cash reserves and unused credit lines.
  - Supplier relationships with capacity to scale (ORANGE-L5-040).
  - Inventory plans for sudden demand.
- PURPLE:
  - Standard contracts reviewed and ready.
  - Trademarks registered in current and planned markets.
  - Product certifications and compliance up to date.
  - Advertising claims substantiated in advance (ORANGE-L5-020).
- BLACK:
  - Written criteria for what opportunities to pursue.
  - Clear decision authority.
  - Cross-trained staff who can shift to high-demand areas.
- GREEN:
  - Clean books and understanding of tax effects of rapid growth.

The fall back and regroup principle applies in reverse: quiet periods are the time to build strength for the next advance.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Readiness is often invisible and feels like unnecessary cost during calm periods. Its value appears only when opportunities or crises arrive, and then it can decide which businesses win.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: Two companies sell home insulation products. An energy shock sends demand soaring.

Company A had built readiness during calm years:
- A consent-based email list of 25,000 past customers and interested homeowners.
- Energy savings claims already backed by independent testing and approved by counsel.
- Supplier agreements allowing a 50% increase in orders with 30 days' notice.
- An unused credit line.
- A documented sales process and two cross-trained staff who can move from office roles to sales.
- Written criteria: pursue any demand surge where margins stay above a set level.

Company B had none of these.

When demand surges:
- Company A emails its list within two days, with compliant claims. Supplier capacity and financing support higher inventory. Cross-trained staff handle new inquiries.
- Company B spends weeks preparing materials, securing supply, arranging financing, and checking claims.

By the time Company B is ready, Company A has captured most of the early demand and built relationships with new customers.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Company A's readiness cost money in calm years. It paid for itself many times over in a single season.

## Key Takeaways
- Readiness must be built before opportunities appear
- Prepare customer relationships, materials, supply, financing, legal approvals, and decision criteria
- Quiet periods are for building strength
- Readiness often decides who captures opportunities
