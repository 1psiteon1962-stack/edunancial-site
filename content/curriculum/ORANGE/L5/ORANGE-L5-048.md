# Honest Reassessment of an Active Campaign

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a central bank raising its benchmark interest rate sharply and repeatedly over a short stretch of months to fight rising inflation.

Apply it here: A company launched an aggressive expansion into a new market when rates were low. Now rates have spiked, results lag, and costs are rising. Ask across colors: What do the real numbers show (ORANGE)? Can the business afford to continue (BLUE)? Is leadership holding on because of pride or past spending (BLACK)? What contracts would need to be ended (PURPLE)?

## Learning Objectives
- Explain why active campaigns need regular honest review
- Identify sunk cost, ego, and anchoring traps in marketing
- Choose between continuing, adjusting, scaling back, or regrouping

## Core Content
The third part of the knife-fight doctrine is honest ongoing reassessment. Committing fully to a market push does not mean refusing to reconsider it as conditions and evidence change.

Why reassessment matters:
- Early results reveal whether assumptions were right.
- Market conditions change (rates, competition, customer behavior).
- Costs often exceed plans.
- Opportunity costs grow: money and people spent here cannot be used elsewhere.

Common traps:
- Sunk cost: "We've spent $500,000 on this market; we can't stop now." Past spending is gone either way. Decide based on future costs and future value.
- Ego: Leaders who championed a campaign may resist admitting it is not working.
- Anchoring: Clinging to original projections despite new data.
- Vanity metrics: Focusing on impressions, followers, or revenue instead of profit and customer lifetime value (ORANGE-L5-043).

A reassessment routine:
1. Compare actual results with milestones (ORANGE-L5-039).
2. Update forecasts using current data, not original assumptions.
3. Estimate remaining costs and expected future value.
4. Consider opportunity costs.
5. Invite honest input, including from people who disagree.
6. Check contract commitments (PURPLE): leases, distributor agreements, and marketing contracts that affect exit costs.

Options:
- Continue as planned.
- Adjust (change targeting, channels, pricing, or messaging).
- Scale back to a smaller, profitable core.
- Fall back and regroup: pause or exit, preserve resources, and return later if conditions improve. The military analogy frames this as a legitimate tactic, not failure.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Knowing when to pull back is as important as knowing when to push forward. Both are part of disciplined competitive strategy.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: Andile's company expanded into a new city with a $600,000 marketing and staffing budget, projecting profitability within 12 months.

After 10 months:
- Spent: $520,000.
- Customer acquisition cost: $180 (projected $90).
- Monthly revenue: 40% of target.
- Rates have spiked, raising the cost of his credit line.

His team's first reaction: "We're almost at 12 months. Let's push harder."

Honest reassessment:
- Sunk cost: The $520,000 is gone regardless.
- Updated forecast: At current trends, profitability would take 30 more months and another $400,000.
- Opportunity cost: His home market has strong demand he has neglected.
- Contracts (PURPLE): His office lease in the new city has a termination option with 3 months' notice.

Decision: Scale back. He keeps a small team serving existing customers profitably, cuts marketing spending there, exercises the lease termination option and moves to a shared workspace, and redirects resources to his home market.

A year later, his home market grows strongly. He plans to revisit the new city when conditions improve. ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Falling back and regrouping preserved his company's strength for a better opportunity.

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** Changing evidence can change the right decision.

**Q2.** Past spending should not drive future decisions.

**Q3.** Vanity metrics can mislead decisions.

**Q4.** Honest reassessment revealed better uses of resources.

**Q5.** The military analogy frames it as strategic.

## Key Takeaways
- Committing fully does not mean refusing to reconsider
- Ignore sunk costs, ego, anchoring, and vanity metrics
- Check contract exit terms before deciding
- Scaling back or regrouping can preserve strength for better opportunities
