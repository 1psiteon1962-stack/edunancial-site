# Calculated Competitive Risk vs. Reckless Marketing Gambles

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a large trading bloc suddenly imposing steep tariffs on a broad category of imported manufactured goods, raising costs for companies that depend on those imports as inputs.

Apply it here: New tariffs shake up a market. One company considers starting a price war to drive out rivals; another considers a bold but well-researched product launch. Ask across colors: Which move is based on evidence and survivable downside (ORANGE/BLUE)? Which could violate competition or advertising law (PURPLE)? Which leader is prepared to own the consequences (BLACK)?

## Learning Objectives
- Distinguish calculated competitive risk from reckless gambling
- Identify warning signs of reckless marketing moves
- Apply courage and consequence ownership to competitive decisions

## Core Content
The fourth part of the knife-fight doctrine separates calculated risk from reckless gambling and emphasizes courage and consequence ownership.

Calculated competitive risk:
- Based on evidence: customer research, market data, and tested assumptions.
- Downside is understood and survivable.
- Resources are adequate for the move.
- Legal and ethical boundaries are respected.
- Clear exit criteria if results disappoint.

Reckless marketing gambles:
- Betting the company on untested assumptions.
- Price wars that the company cannot afford to sustain, destroying margins for everyone.
- Exaggerated or misleading claims to win customers quickly (PURPLE-L5-035 in the law track).
- Tactics that may violate competition law, such as agreements with competitors to fix prices, or in some jurisdictions pricing below cost intended to drive out rivals. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE.
- Spending borrowed money on campaigns with no measurement.

Courage in competition: Calculated risk takes courage. Launching a new product, entering a market, or challenging a larger competitor can be frightening. The doctrine encourages boldness when evidence supports it.

Consequence ownership: Leaders who take calculated risks accept responsibility for outcomes. They do not blame the team, the market, or advisors if a well-reasoned bet does not work. They communicate honestly with employees, investors, and customers.

How colors connect:
- ORANGE: Evidence-based competitive strategy.
- BLUE: Survivable downside.
- PURPLE: Legal boundaries, especially competition and advertising law.
- BLACK: Courage and accountability.
- WHITE: Risk relative to potential return.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: The line between bold and reckless is not the size of the move. It is whether the move is grounded in evidence, survivable if it fails, and within legal and ethical limits.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: Two companies respond to tariff-driven changes in the kitchen appliance market.

Company A (calculated): Research shows customers want a mid-priced, locally assembled blender that avoids tariff costs. Company A:
- Tests the concept with 200 customers and pre-orders.
- Budgets a launch that, if it fails, would cost 15% of reserves.
- Backs marketing claims with testing.
- Sets exit criteria: if sales are below 60% of target after 6 months, reduce investment.
- The CEO tells employees and investors about the risks openly.

Sales reach 80% of target. The product becomes profitable in year two.

Company B (reckless): The CEO decides to cut prices 40% below cost to drive smaller competitors out, funded by borrowed money. Marketing claims "the lowest prices forever." No research, no exit plan.

Within months, margins collapse. Competitors match some prices. A regulator begins reviewing whether the pricing and claims raise concerns. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE. The CEO blames the market.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Both companies acted boldly. Only Company A's boldness was grounded, survivable, lawful, and owned.

## Practice Quiz
**Q1. What characterizes calculated competitive risk?**
A) Evidence-based decisions with survivable downside and clear exit criteria
B) Betting everything on a guess
C) Ignoring legal limits
D) Avoiding all risk

**Q2. Why can price wars be reckless?**
A) They are always illegal
B) They raise prices
C) They can destroy margins, and a company may not be able to sustain them
D) They improve profits always

**Q3. What legal concerns can arise from aggressive competitive tactics?**
A) None
B) Price fixing with competitors or, in some places, below-cost pricing meant to drive out rivals
C) Trademark registration
D) Lower taxes

**Q4. What does consequence ownership look like?**
A) Accepting responsibility and communicating honestly about outcomes
B) Blaming the team
C) Hiding results
D) Blaming advisors

**Q5. According to the lesson, what separates bold from reckless?**
A) The size of the move
B) Who approves it
C) How fast it happens
D) Whether it is grounded in evidence, survivable, and within legal and ethical limits

## Answer Key
Q1: A
Q2: C
Q3: B
Q4: A
Q5: D

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** Calculated risk is grounded and bounded.

**Q2.** Unsustainable price wars harm everyone.

**Q3.** Competition law limits some tactics. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE.

**Q4.** Leaders own their decisions.

**Q5.** Grounding and boundaries define calculated risk.

## Key Takeaways
- Calculated risk is evidence-based, survivable, lawful, and has exit criteria
- Reckless gambles include unsustainable price wars and misleading claims
- Competition and advertising law set boundaries
- Courage and consequence ownership distinguish responsible leaders
