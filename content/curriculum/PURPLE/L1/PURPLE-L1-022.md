# Deeds: Types and What They Actually Transfer

## Learning Objectives

- Define a deed in plain terms.
- Distinguish general warranty deeds from quitclaim deeds.
- Explain why the type of deed matters practically.

## Core Content

A deed is the legal document that actually transfers ownership of real property from one party to another. Not all deeds provide the same level of protection to the buyer, and understanding the difference matters practically, not just technically. A **general warranty deed** provides the strongest protection: the seller guarantees they hold clear title and will defend against any claims against that title, even from before their own ownership. A **quitclaim deed** provides the weakest protection: the seller transfers whatever interest they happen to have, with no guarantee that interest is actually valid or that title is clear — it's a "transfer what I have, if anything" document, not a guarantee.

This distinction matters enormously in practice. A general warranty deed is standard and expected in most arm's-length real estate purchases, since a buyer paying real money wants real assurance about what they're actually getting. A quitclaim deed is more common in situations without a genuine sale — transferring property between family members, clearing up a title technicality, or divorce settlements — where the parties already understand and accept the limited nature of the transfer. Receiving a quitclaim deed in what should be a normal purchase transaction is a red flag worth immediate professional attention.

## Worked Example

**Question:** A buyer is purchasing a property from an unrelated seller for full market value, and the seller offers a quitclaim deed instead of a general warranty deed. Should this concern the buyer?

**Student:** Based on this lesson, yes — a quitclaim deed offers no guarantee the seller actually has clear title, which is a real concern in a normal, full-value purchase transaction.

**Guide:** Why would a quitclaim deed be more appropriate in a different kind of situation, like a transfer between family members?

**Student:** Because in that context, the parties already understand and accept the limited nature of the transfer — they're not relying on the deed itself for a strong ownership guarantee.

**Guide:** Exactly — the same document type can be perfectly normal in one context and a serious red flag in another.

## Answer Key

## Answers and Explanations

**1.** A deed is specifically the legal transfer instrument for real property ownership.

**2.** The general warranty deed's strength comes from the seller's affirmative guarantee and ongoing defense obligation.

**3.** A quitclaim deed's weakness is its lack of any guarantee about the validity of what's being transferred.

**4.** Context determines appropriateness — the same document type is normal in one setting and alarming in another.

## Key Takeaways

- A deed is the legal document that transfers real property ownership.
- A general warranty deed guarantees clear title and defense against claims.
- A quitclaim deed transfers whatever interest exists, with no guarantee of validity.
- The right deed type depends on context — a quitclaim deed in a normal purchase is a red flag.
