# What Is a Security, Legally?

## Learning Objectives

- Define a security in legal terms.
- Identify common examples of securities.
- Explain why the legal definition matters beyond the common-sense understanding.

## Core Content

This lesson opens PURPLE's investment law section, connecting directly to WHITE. A security is, broadly, a legally defined category of investment — an instrument representing an ownership stake, a debt relationship, or rights to profits from the effort of others. Common, obvious examples include stocks and bonds, both covered in depth in WHITE. But the legal definition of a security is actually broader and more nuanced than most people assume — it can extend to certain investment contracts, some forms of profit-sharing arrangements, and other structures that don't look like a traditional stock certificate at all.

This broader legal definition matters because securities law imposes real requirements — registration, disclosure, and other investor protections — that apply based on whether something legally qualifies as a security, not based on what it's casually called. An investment opportunity marketed as something else entirely (a "membership," a "profit-sharing club," a "revenue share agreement") can still legally be a security if it meets the legal test, meaning it's still subject to securities regulation regardless of the label used to market it. Understanding this sets up the next several lessons, which explore why this regulation exists and what it's designed to protect against.

## Worked Example

**Question:** A promoter markets an investment opportunity as a 'profit-sharing club,' explicitly avoiding the word 'security' or 'stock.' Does that labeling choice determine whether securities law actually applies?

**Student:** Based on this lesson, no — the legal definition of a security depends on what the arrangement actually is, not what it's called, so a 'profit-sharing club' could still legally be a security if it meets the legal test.

**Guide:** Why would this matter to someone considering that investment opportunity?

**Student:** Because if it does legally qualify as a security, it's still subject to registration, disclosure, and other investor protection requirements, regardless of how it was marketed or labeled.

**Guide:** Exactly — the substance of the arrangement matters more than the label used to describe it.

## Answers and Explanations

**1.** This broad definition intentionally captures a wide range of investment-like arrangements, not just obvious examples.

**2.** Stocks and bonds are the clearest, most familiar examples, covered in depth elsewhere in this curriculum.

**3.** Legal substance, not marketing language, is what actually triggers securities regulation.

**4.** This regulatory reach is precisely what protects investors from arrangements designed to evade it through clever labeling.

## Key Takeaways

- A security is broadly any instrument representing ownership, debt, or profit-sharing rights.
- Stocks and bonds are the most familiar examples, but the legal definition extends further.
- What something is called doesn't determine its legal status — the actual substance does.
- This sets up the next lessons, which explore why securities regulation exists and what it protects.
