## PURPLE-L2-015
### Power of Attorney: Types and General Purpose

**1. Learning Objectives**
By the end of this lesson, the learner will be able to:
- Define power of attorney at a conceptual level.
- Distinguish general and durable power of attorney.
- Explain why this instrument addresses incapacity, not just death.

**2. Core Content**
A power of attorney (POA) is a legal document granting one person (the "agent" or "attorney-in-fact") authority to act on another person's (the "principal's") behalf, generally for financial or legal matters. Unlike wills and many trust provisions, which primarily address what happens after death, a POA addresses situations during the principal's lifetime, particularly incapacity — a significant illness or injury preventing the principal from managing their own affairs.

A general power of attorney typically grants broad authority but generally becomes invalid if the principal becomes incapacitated — precisely the situation where such authority might be most needed, unless the document specifically includes "durability" language. A durable power of attorney specifically includes provisions ensuring the authority remains valid even if the principal becomes incapacitated, making it the more commonly recommended instrument for incapacity planning specifically, since a standard, non-durable POA can fail exactly when it would otherwise be most needed.

**3. Worked Example**
Before undergoing a planned surgery with some risk of a difficult recovery, a man establishes a durable power of attorney naming his spouse to manage his financial affairs — paying bills, managing accounts — specifically ensuring this authority would remain valid even if complications left him temporarily unable to manage these matters himself. Had he instead signed only a general, non-durable power of attorney, his spouse's authority to act on his behalf could have become invalid at exactly the moment his incapacity made it most necessary, defeating the entire purpose of having arranged for this authority in advance.

**4. Practice Quiz**
1. What is a power of attorney (POA)?
   a) A type of will
   b) A legal document granting one person authority to act on another's behalf
   c) A type of trust exclusively for businesses
   d) A tax filing form
2. What does "durability" add to a power of attorney?
   a) Nothing of significance
   b) Provisions ensuring the authority remains valid even if the principal becomes incapacitated
   c) A guarantee that the document never expires under any circumstance
   d) Additional financial value to the estate
3. True or False: A standard, non-durable power of attorney remains valid even if the principal becomes incapacitated.
4. Why did the man specifically choose a durable power of attorney before his surgery?
   a) He wanted the authority to become invalid if he became incapacitated
   b) He wanted his spouse's authority to remain valid specifically during a period of potential incapacity
   c) Durable POAs are required by law for all surgeries
   d) He had no concerns about incapacity at all

**5. Answer Key**
1. b  2. b  3. False  4. b

**6. Answers and Explanations**
1. A power of attorney grants one person legal authority to act on another's behalf.
2. Durability ensures the POA's authority remains valid even if the principal becomes incapacitated.
3. A standard, non-durable POA generally becomes invalid upon the principal's incapacitation.
4. The man's durable POA ensured his spouse's authority would remain valid specifically during a period of potential incapacity.

**7. Key Takeaways**
- A power of attorney grants an agent authority to act on a principal's behalf, generally for financial or legal matters.
- Unlike wills, a POA addresses situations during the principal's lifetime, particularly incapacity.
- A general, non-durable POA generally becomes invalid upon incapacitation, unless durability provisions are included.
- A durable POA is generally the recommended instrument specifically for incapacity planning.
