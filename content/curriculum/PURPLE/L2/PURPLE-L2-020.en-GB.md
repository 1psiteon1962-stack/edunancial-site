## PURPLE-L2-020
### Intellectual Property Overview: Patents, Trademarks, Copyrights, and Trade Secrets Compared

**1. Learning Objectives**
By the end of this lesson, the learner will be able to:
- Compare the four major categories of intellectual property protection.
- Identify what each category generally protects.
- Explain why choosing the right protection type matters.

**2. Core Content**
This lesson introduces a comparative overview before Lessons 21–24 examine each category in depth. A patent generally protects inventions — new, useful, and non-obvious processes, machines, or compositions of matter — for a limited time period, in exchange for public disclosure of the invention. A trademark generally protects brand identifiers — names, logos, slogans — that distinguish one party's goods or services from another's. A copyright generally protects original creative and written works — books, music, software code, artwork — from unauthorised copying or use. A trade secret generally protects confidential business information (formulas, processes, customer lists) through secrecy itself, rather than through public registration.

Choosing the right protection type matters because these categories are not interchangeable: attempting to protect a brand name through copyright, for example, would generally be a mismatch, since copyright protects creative expression, not brand identifiers, which trademark law specifically addresses. Many businesses use multiple types of IP protection simultaneously for different aspects of their operations — a software company might patent a novel technical process, trademark its brand name, copyright its written documentation and code, and protect its internal algorithms as trade secrets, all at once.

**3. Worked Example**
Launching a new software product, a founder works with an intellectual property attorney to identify the appropriate protection for each distinct aspect of the business: trademarking the product's brand name and logo, copyrighting the software's underlying code and user documentation, exploring whether a specific novel technical process within the software might qualify for patent protection, and maintaining certain internal algorithms as trade secrets rather than publicly disclosing them through a patent application, given a business judgement that secrecy better serves this particular asset's competitive value.

**4. Practice Quiz**
1. What does a patent generally protect?
   a) Brand names and logos
   b) New, useful, and non-obvious inventions
   c) Creative and written works exclusively
   d) Confidential information kept secret
2. What does a trademark generally protect?
   a) Inventions
   b) Brand identifiers distinguishing one party's goods or services from another's
   c) Confidential formulas
   d) Written books exclusively
3. True or False: All four intellectual property categories are interchangeable and protect identical things.
4. Why did the software founder use multiple types of IP protection simultaneously?
   a) Only one type of protection is ever available to any business
   b) Different aspects of the business (brand, code, technical processes, algorithms) required different, appropriate protection types
   c) IP protection is unnecessary for software businesses
   d) Trademarks alone protect everything a business needs

**5. Answer Key**
1. b  2. b  3. False  4. b

**6. Answers and Explanations**
1. Patents generally protect new, useful, and non-obvious inventions.
2. Trademarks generally protect brand identifiers distinguishing one party's goods or services from another's.
3. These four categories protect distinct types of intellectual property and are not interchangeable.
4. The founder's different business aspects each required a different, appropriately matched type of IP protection.

**7. Key Takeaways**
- Patents, trademarks, copyrights, and trade secrets each protect distinct categories of intellectual property.
- Patents protect inventions; trademarks protect brand identifiers; copyrights protect creative works; trade secrets protect confidential information through secrecy.
- These categories are not interchangeable, and choosing the wrong protection type creates a genuine mismatch.
- Many businesses use multiple IP protection types simultaneously for different aspects of their operations.
