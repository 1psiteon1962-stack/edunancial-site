## PURPLE-L2-033
### Negligence: The Building Blocks of a Tort Claim

**1. Learning Objectives**
By the end of this lesson, the learner will be able to:
- Identify the general elements of a negligence claim.
- Explain how each element must generally be established.
- Apply these elements to a hypothetical scenario.

**2. Core Content**
Building on Lesson 32's introduction of tort law, negligence is among the most common tort claims, generally requiring the plaintiff to establish four elements: duty (the defendant owed a legal duty of reasonable care to the plaintiff), breach (the defendant failed to meet that standard of reasonable care), causation (the defendant's breach actually caused the plaintiff's harm), and damages (the plaintiff suffered actual, legally recognized harm as a result).

Each element must generally be established, not merely one or two — a defendant who breached a duty of care but whose breach did not actually cause any harm to the plaintiff would generally not be liable for negligence, since the causation and damages elements would be missing even if duty and breach were clearly present. This structured, multi-element framework is why negligence claims often require careful factual and, where applicable, expert analysis to properly establish each piece, rather than relying on a general sense that "someone did something wrong."

**3. Worked Example**
A store owner fails to clean up a spilled liquid on the floor for an extended period despite being aware of it (potentially establishing duty and breach — a duty of reasonable care to customers, breached by failing to address a known hazard). If a customer then slips on the spill and suffers a genuine injury requiring medical treatment (establishing causation — the breach directly caused the harm — and damages — actual, legally recognized harm), all four negligence elements would likely be present. If, instead, no customer was ever harmed by the spill despite the store owner's breach of duty in leaving it unaddressed, no negligence claim would generally succeed, since the causation and damages elements would be missing entirely.

**4. Practice Quiz**
1. What are the four general elements of a negligence claim?
   a) Offer, acceptance, consideration, and intent
   b) Duty, breach, causation, and damages
   c) Only damages, with no other requirements
   d) Only intent, with no other requirements
2. What happens if a defendant breaches a duty of care, but the breach causes no actual harm to the plaintiff?
   a) Negligence liability automatically applies regardless
   b) A negligence claim would generally not succeed, since causation and damages elements would be missing
   c) The breach element becomes irrelevant
   d) Duty is no longer required in this situation
3. True or False: Establishing only one of the four negligence elements is generally sufficient for a successful negligence claim.
4. Why did the customer's slip-and-fall scenario likely establish all four negligence elements?
   a) Only duty was present
   b) The store's known duty, breach in leaving the hazard, and the customer's actual resulting injury together established all four elements
   c) No harm occurred at all
   d) The elements are unrelated to this scenario

**5. Answer Key**
1. b  2. b  3. False  4. b

**6. Answers and Explanations**
1. The four general elements of a negligence claim are duty, breach, causation, and damages.
2. Without actual causation and resulting damages, a negligence claim generally does not succeed, even if duty and breach are present.
3. All four elements must generally be established together for a negligence claim to succeed.
4. The scenario's known hazard (duty/breach) combined with the customer's actual resulting injury (causation/damages) established all four elements.

**7. Key Takeaways**
- Negligence generally requires establishing duty, breach, causation, and damages together.
- All four elements must generally be present; missing any one generally defeats the claim.
- This structured framework requires careful factual analysis, not merely a general sense that something went wrong.
- Understanding these elements clarifies why some seemingly careless conduct does not result in negligence liability if no actual harm resulted.
