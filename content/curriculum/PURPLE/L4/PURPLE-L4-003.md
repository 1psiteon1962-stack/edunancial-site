# Contract Remedies Analysis: Why Damages Are the Default and Specific Performance the Exception

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a mid-sized sovereign government signaling it may be unable to make an upcoming bond payment, raising default risk on its debt. As a mid-sized sovereign government signals possible default on its own debt obligations, businesses and investors with contracts tied to that government's performance want to understand what legal remedies are actually available when a contracting party fails to perform, and specifically why courts generally award money rather than simply forcing the breaching party to do what they originally promised. This lesson examines the general hierarchy of contract remedies and its underlying logic.

## Learning Objectives
- Explain why monetary damages, specifically 'expectation damages,' are generally the default remedy for a breach of contract.
- Explain what expectation damages are designed to accomplish, in contrast to simply punishing the breaching party.
- Explain the 'adequacy of legal remedy' requirement that generally must be satisfied before a court will order specific performance instead of monetary damages.
- Explain why specific performance is generally available for contracts involving unique goods or real property, but not for most ordinary contracts for the sale of readily available goods or services.

## Core Content
VERIFIED FACT: monetary damages, specifically "expectation damages" (an amount of money intended to put the non-breaching party in the same financial position they would have occupied had the contract actually been fully performed as originally promised), are generally the default, most commonly awarded remedy for a breach of contract, with specific performance (a court order actually compelling the breaching party to perform their specific original contractual obligation) available only in more limited, specific circumstances.

Here is the "why" expectation damages are designed to accomplish this specific compensatory purpose, rather than to punish the breaching party: contract law generally operates on a compensatory, rather than punitive, principle — the goal is generally to make the non-breaching party financially whole, placing them in essentially the same economic position performance would have provided, rather than to penalize the breaching party beyond that compensatory amount, meaning punitive damages (additional damages specifically intended to punish and deter, rather than merely compensate) are generally not available in an ordinary contract dispute, REGION-SPECIFIC RULE, confirm current specific jurisdictional exceptions, which are narrow and specific, unlike in many tort cases, where a defendant's genuinely egregious conduct can sometimes justify this kind of additional, non-compensatory punitive award.

This is why the "adequacy of legal remedy" requirement generally must be satisfied before a court will order specific performance instead of simply awarding monetary damages: courts generally require the party seeking specific performance to first show that monetary damages alone would be an inadequate remedy — meaning money could not actually put that party in as good a position as receiving the originally promised performance itself would — before a court will take the more unusual, more burdensome step of actually compelling a party to perform (a burden connecting directly to enforcement and ongoing judicial supervision concerns that a straightforward damages award does not carry to nearly the same degree). This is why specific performance is generally available for contracts involving unique goods or real property, but not for most ordinary contracts for the sale of readily available goods or services: since real property is generally considered legally unique (no two specific parcels of real property are considered truly interchangeable, meaning a monetary damages award could not actually purchase an equivalent substitute), and since certain specific goods (a one-of-a-kind piece of art, a specific rare item, or a specific unique piece of custom equipment, for example) similarly lack any readily available substitute, courts have historically been considerably more willing to order specific performance in these particular categories of contract, while for an ordinary contract involving readily available, fungible goods or services (where the non-breaching party could reasonably use a monetary damages award to simply purchase a comparable substitute elsewhere), monetary damages are generally considered an entirely adequate remedy, meaning specific performance is generally unavailable and unnecessary in that more ordinary context.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: A buyer contracts to purchase a specific, unique historic property, and the seller later breaches by refusing to complete the sale. Because real property is considered legally unique, with no truly equivalent substitute available on the open market, a court is likely to order specific performance, actually compelling the seller to complete the originally promised sale. A different buyer, under a separate contract to purchase a standard, readily available piece of commercial equipment from a different seller who similarly breaches, would generally be limited to a monetary damages award (the difference between the original contract price and the cost of purchasing a comparable substitute elsewhere), since that specific type of equipment is readily available from other sources, making monetary damages an entirely adequate remedy in that more ordinary context.

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** This compensatory purpose — restoring the non-breaching party to the position full performance would have provided — is precisely why expectation damages are the generally preferred, default contract remedy.

**Q2.** This compensatory-versus-punitive distinction is precisely why punitive damages are generally unavailable in an ordinary contract dispute, in contrast to their availability for genuinely egregious tort conduct in certain circumstances.

**Q3.** This specific showing — that money alone cannot adequately substitute for the originally promised performance — is precisely what the adequacy of legal remedy requirement demands before a court will order specific performance.

**Q4.** This distinction based on the availability of an equivalent substitute is precisely why specific performance is generally reserved for unique subject matter like real property, rather than being available for ordinary, readily substitutable goods.

**Q5.** This direct contrast between a unique asset with no substitute and a readily available, fungible good exemplifies precisely why courts distinguish these two contexts when deciding whether to order specific performance.

## Key Takeaways
- Expectation damages, intended to place the non-breaching party in the position full performance would have provided, are the generally preferred, default contract remedy.
- Contract law is generally compensatory rather than punitive, meaning punitive damages are generally unavailable in an ordinary contract dispute.
- A party seeking specific performance must generally show that monetary damages alone would be an inadequate remedy for their specific situation.
- Specific performance is generally available for unique subject matter like real property, but not for most ordinary contracts involving readily available, substitutable goods or services.
