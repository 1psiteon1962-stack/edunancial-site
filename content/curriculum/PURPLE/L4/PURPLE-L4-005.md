# Force Majeure Clause Analysis: Why an 'Act of God' Provision Doesn't Automatically Excuse Every Disruption

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a widespread public health disruption halting global shipping and manufacturing for an extended stretch, snarling supply chains across many industries at once. As a widespread disruption to global shipping and manufacturing prevents countless businesses from fulfilling their existing contractual obligations, parties on all sides look closely at their contracts' force majeure clauses to determine whether this specific event actually excuses their non-performance, only to discover that the answer depends heavily on the precise, specific wording each individual contract happens to use. This lesson examines why force majeure clauses require this kind of careful, specific analysis rather than a generic assumption about what should count.

## Learning Objectives
- Explain the general purpose of a force majeure clause in a commercial contract.
- Explain why a force majeure clause's actual scope depends on the specific events it lists, rather than any generic understanding of an excusable disruption.
- Explain the general requirement that the specific triggering event must have actually caused the party's inability to perform, connecting to basic causation principles.
- Explain why courts generally interpret force majeure clauses narrowly, construing genuine ambiguity against the party seeking to invoke the clause to excuse their own non-performance.

## Core Content
A force majeure clause is a specific contractual provision that excuses a party's non-performance, or delays their required performance without penalty, when a specifically defined, extraordinary event outside that party's reasonable control prevents them from performing their contractual obligations. VERIFIED FACT: a force majeure clause is not a standardized, universal provision automatically included or automatically interpreted the same way in every contract — its actual scope and effect depend entirely on the specific language the parties themselves negotiated and included within their own particular contract.

Here is the "why" a force majeure clause's actual scope depends on the specific events it lists, rather than any generic understanding of what should count as an excusable disruption: many force majeure clauses include a specific, enumerated list of qualifying events (such as war, natural disasters, government action, or a labor strike), sometimes followed by a broader, general catch-all phrase (such as "or any other event beyond the reasonable control of the parties"), and REGION-SPECIFIC RULE, confirm current specific jurisdictional interpretive doctrines, courts applying a specific interpretive canon (sometimes called ejusdem generis, "of the same kind") have often interpreted a broad, general catch-all phrase as limited to events genuinely similar in kind and character to the specifically enumerated events actually listed nearby, rather than as a limitless, all-encompassing excuse covering literally any conceivable disruption — meaning a specific event like the widespread public health disruption described in the scenario above may or may not actually fall within a particular contract's specific force majeure clause, depending entirely on that clause's own specific enumerated list and the broader catch-all language's own specific scope, rather than being automatically covered simply because the disruption was genuinely severe and widely felt.

This is why the specific triggering event must have actually caused the party's inability to perform, connecting to basic causation principles: even where a listed force majeure event genuinely occurred, a party invoking the clause generally must also show that this specific event actually caused their own particular inability to perform their specific contractual obligations, rather than simply pointing to the event's general existence somewhere in the broader world while their own specific performance was, in fact, unaffected by it or was hindered by some separate, unrelated cause instead — a party whose own performance would have been genuinely impossible regardless of the specific listed event, perhaps due to their own unrelated financial difficulties or poor planning, generally cannot successfully invoke a force majeure clause merely because a qualifying event happened to occur around the same general time. This is why courts generally interpret force majeure clauses narrowly, construing genuine ambiguity against the party seeking to invoke the clause to excuse their own non-performance: OPINION and a widely observed judicial tendency, since a force majeure clause operates specifically to excuse a party from an obligation they would otherwise be legally required to perform, courts have generally required the party invoking this kind of clause to clearly demonstrate that the specific event and its actual effect on their own performance genuinely fall within the clause's own specific language, rather than resolving any genuine ambiguity in that party's own favor by default, meaning careful, specific contract drafting regarding exactly which events are covered, and precisely how directly they must have caused the actual non-performance, matters considerably more than many parties may initially assume when a real disruption actually occurs.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: A supplier's contract includes a force majeure clause specifically listing "war, government embargo, and natural disaster" as qualifying events, followed by a general catch-all phrase covering "any other similar event beyond the parties' reasonable control." When a widespread public health disruption prevents the supplier from obtaining a critical raw material, the supplier attempts to invoke the clause, but a court applying the ejusdem generis interpretive principle finds that a public health disruption is not sufficiently similar in kind to the specifically listed events (war, embargo, natural disaster) to fall within the broader catch-all language, meaning the supplier's force majeure defense fails, and they remain contractually liable for their non-performance — illustrating precisely why the specific enumerated language in a force majeure clause, not simply the general severity of a real-world disruption, determines the clause's actual legal scope.

## Key Takeaways
- A force majeure clause excuses non-performance when a specifically defined, extraordinary event outside a party's reasonable control prevents performance.
- The clause's actual scope depends on its specific enumerated events and how a general catch-all phrase is interpreted relative to those events, not on generic disruption severity.
- A party invoking the clause must generally show the specific event actually caused their own inability to perform, not merely that a qualifying event occurred somewhere generally.
- Courts generally interpret force majeure clauses narrowly, requiring the invoking party to clearly demonstrate coverage rather than resolving genuine ambiguity in their favor.
