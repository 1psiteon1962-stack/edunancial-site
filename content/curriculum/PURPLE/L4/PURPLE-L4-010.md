# Non-Compete Enforceability Analysis: Why the Same Restrictive Covenant Can Be Valid in One State and Void in Another

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a widespread public health disruption halting global shipping and manufacturing for an extended stretch, snarling supply chains across many industries at once. As a widespread disruption pushes many workers toward new employment opportunities, often with a former employer's competitor, both employees and employers want to understand exactly when an employment-based non-compete agreement will actually be enforced by a court, and why the same general type of restriction can be treated completely differently depending on which state's law applies. This lesson examines the specific analysis courts generally apply, building on the BLUE-L4 track's introduction to non-competes in a business-sale context.

## Learning Objectives
- Distinguish an employment-based non-compete agreement from the business-sale non-compete context covered in the BLUE-L4 track, and explain why courts generally scrutinize the employment context more closely.
- Explain the general 'reasonableness' factors — scope, geography, and duration — courts commonly apply when evaluating an employment non-compete's enforceability.
- Explain why non-compete enforceability varies dramatically across different states, and why some jurisdictions have moved to significantly restrict or prohibit these agreements in the employment context.
- Explain why an employee or employer should confirm current, jurisdiction-specific law rather than assuming any general rule applies uniformly.

## Core Content
Building on the BLUE-L4 track's introduction to non-compete agreements in a business-sale context, an employment-based non-compete agreement — restricting a departing employee from working for a competitor or starting a competing business for a specified period and within a specified geographic area — generally receives considerably more judicial scrutiny than a business-sale non-compete. VERIFIED FACT: this heightened scrutiny reflects courts' general recognition of a meaningful difference in bargaining power and underlying purpose between these two contexts — a business-sale non-compete specifically protects the substantial value and goodwill a buyer has just paid for (per the BLUE-L4 track's analysis), generally negotiated between two comparably sophisticated commercial parties, while an employment non-compete more directly restricts an individual worker's own ability to earn a living in their chosen field, often presented on a take-it-or-leave-it basis with considerably less genuine individual negotiating power.

Here is the "why" courts commonly apply a "reasonableness" standard examining scope, geography, and duration when evaluating an employment non-compete's enforceability: REGION-SPECIFIC RULE, confirm current specific jurisdictional standards, which vary considerably, courts in jurisdictions that do enforce employment non-competes generally require the restriction to be no broader than reasonably necessary to protect the employer's own legitimate business interest (such as genuinely confidential trade secrets or substantial customer relationships the employee had direct access to, rather than merely a general desire to prevent ordinary competition), reasonable in its specific geographic scope (limited to the area where the employer actually does business and where the employee's specific role gave them genuine competitive insight, rather than a needlessly broad, nationwide restriction for a role with only local significance), and reasonable in its specific duration (long enough to provide meaningful protection for the employer's legitimate interest, but not so long as to impose an excessive, unwarranted burden on the departing employee's own ability to work).

This is why non-compete enforceability varies dramatically across different states, and why some jurisdictions have moved to significantly restrict or prohibit these agreements specifically in the employment context: some states enforce reasonably scoped employment non-competes fairly readily, applying the reasonableness analysis described above, while other states have adopted considerably stricter limits, and a small but growing number of jurisdictions, REGION-SPECIFIC RULE, confirm current specific jurisdictional developments, since this is an actively evolving area of law, have moved to substantially restrict or, in some cases, effectively prohibit non-compete agreements in most or all employment contexts, particularly for lower-wage workers, reflecting a policy judgment that these specific restrictions can meaningfully suppress worker mobility and wage growth in ways some legislatures and courts have increasingly viewed as outweighing employers' own legitimate protective interests. This is why both an employee asked to sign a non-compete and an employer considering using one should specifically confirm current, jurisdiction-specific law rather than assuming any general rule applies uniformly: given both the significant, ongoing state-by-state variation in this specific area and its genuinely active, evolving legal landscape, a non-compete that would be readily enforced in one specific state might be entirely void and unenforceable in another, meaning neither party should assume, without specific, current legal confirmation, that a particular non-compete's terms will actually be enforced (or that they will actually be unenforceable) purely based on a general impression of how these agreements are typically treated, without confirming the specific, currently applicable law of the specific jurisdiction actually governing the particular employment relationship in question.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: An employee in one state signs an employment non-compete restricting them from working for any competitor within a 50-mile radius for two years after leaving the company. Under that specific state's current law, which enforces reasonably scoped non-competes, a court finds the restriction reasonable given the employee's genuine access to confidential customer relationships, and enforces it. A different employee, performing a similar role for a similar company but working in a different state that has recently moved to substantially restrict employment non-competes as a matter of legislative policy, signs a nearly identical agreement that is instead found entirely void and unenforceable under that state's current law — illustrating precisely why the same general type of non-compete restriction can produce dramatically different legal outcomes depending entirely on which state's specific, currently applicable law actually governs the relationship.

## Key Takeaways
- Employment non-competes generally receive more judicial scrutiny than business-sale non-competes, given differing bargaining power and underlying purpose.
- Courts commonly apply a reasonableness standard examining whether the restriction's business justification, geographic scope, and duration are all appropriately limited.
- Non-compete enforceability varies dramatically by state, with a growing number of jurisdictions substantially restricting or prohibiting these agreements in employment contexts.
- Both employees and employers should confirm current, jurisdiction-specific law rather than assuming a general rule applies uniformly, given this area's significant variation and ongoing evolution.
