# Patent Novelty and Non-Obviousness Analysis: Why an Invention Must Clear Two Distinct Hurdles to Be Patentable

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture two large economic powers escalating export controls against each other on a category of advanced technology components used across many industries. As two large economic powers escalate technology restrictions and companies rush to patent new innovations developed in response, inventors want to understand exactly what standards their inventions must actually satisfy to receive valid patent protection, building on this level's Lesson 8 introduction to patents, and why simply being new is not, by itself, sufficient for an invention to be patentable.

## Learning Objectives
- Explain the novelty requirement for patentability, and what constitutes disqualifying 'prior art.'
- Explain the separate non-obviousness requirement, and why an invention can be genuinely novel yet still fail this second, distinct hurdle.
- Explain the general standard used to evaluate non-obviousness — whether the invention would have been obvious to a person having ordinary skill in the relevant field.
- Explain why both requirements must independently be satisfied, and why failing either one alone defeats patentability regardless of the other.

## Core Content
Building on this level's Lesson 8 introduction to patents, VERIFIED FACT, an invention must satisfy two genuinely distinct requirements to be patentable: novelty (the invention must not already exist in the "prior art") and non-obviousness (the invention must not be an obvious variation of the existing prior art to a person of ordinary skill in the relevant field), each representing its own separate hurdle an invention must independently clear.

Here is the "why" the novelty requirement asks whether the invention already exists in the prior art: prior art generally includes any patent, publication, public use, or sale that disclosed the same invention before the relevant filing date, REGION-SPECIFIC RULE, confirm current specific statutory prior art categories and relevant timing rules, which have been subject to legislative revision, meaning an invention that has already been publicly disclosed or is already described in some existing prior art reference simply is not "new" in the legally relevant sense, regardless of whether the specific inventor seeking the patent developed their own version entirely independently and in good faith, without any actual knowledge of that existing prior art.

This is why an invention can be genuinely novel yet still fail the separate, distinct non-obviousness hurdle: even if no single existing prior art reference discloses the exact, complete invention (satisfying novelty), the invention can still be denied patent protection if it would have been an obvious variation or combination of the existing prior art to a person of ordinary skill in the relevant technical field — this is precisely why novelty and non-obviousness represent two genuinely separate legal requirements, since an invention combining two previously known, separate elements from the existing prior art in a way that would have been an obvious, straightforward combination to a skilled person in that particular field can fail the non-obviousness requirement even though the exact, complete combination itself had never before been explicitly disclosed anywhere in the prior art, satisfying novelty in a strictly literal sense. This is why the general standard for evaluating non-obviousness specifically asks whether the invention would have been obvious to a hypothetical "person having ordinary skill in the art" (a legally constructed reference standard representing an ordinarily skilled, though not exceptionally brilliant or inventive, practitioner in the specific relevant technical field) at the time the invention was actually made, REGION-SPECIFIC RULE, confirm current specific factors and case law standards applied in this analysis, since this remains an actively litigated area: this analysis generally considers the specific scope and content of the relevant prior art, the specific differences between that prior art and the claimed invention, the level of ordinary skill genuinely present in the relevant field, and certain additional, so-called "secondary considerations" (such as genuine commercial success attributable specifically to the claimed invention, or evidence of a long-felt but previously unsolved need the invention actually addressed) that can sometimes provide meaningful additional evidence supporting a finding of non-obviousness. This is why both requirements must independently be satisfied, and why failing either one alone defeats patentability regardless of the other: an invention that is genuinely novel but obvious, or one that is genuinely non-obvious but not actually novel (perhaps because it was already disclosed, even if not widely known, in some specific existing prior art reference), fails to qualify for patent protection either way, meaning a patent applicant must successfully clear both of these genuinely distinct hurdles together, rather than satisfying either one alone being sufficient on its own.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: An inventor develops a new device combining two previously known, separately existing technical components in a way that produces a genuinely useful result. Even though no single existing prior art reference discloses this exact combination (satisfying novelty), a patent examiner determines that combining these two specific, well-known components in this particular way would have been an obvious, straightforward step for a person of ordinary skill in the relevant technical field, given the specific existing prior art already disclosing each individual component separately, resulting in the patent application being rejected on non-obviousness grounds despite satisfying the separate novelty requirement — illustrating precisely why an invention must independently clear both of these distinct hurdles, and why satisfying novelty alone is not sufficient for patentability.

## Key Takeaways
- An invention must satisfy two distinct patentability requirements: novelty (not already disclosed in the prior art) and non-obviousness (not an obvious variation of the prior art).
- An invention can be genuinely novel yet still fail non-obviousness if it represents an obvious combination or variation of existing prior art elements.
- Non-obviousness is evaluated against a hypothetical 'person having ordinary skill in the art,' considering the prior art's scope, the invention's differences from it, and certain secondary considerations.
- Both novelty and non-obviousness must be independently satisfied; failing either requirement alone defeats patentability regardless of the other.
