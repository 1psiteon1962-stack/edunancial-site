# Subject Matter Jurisdiction Analysis: Why Federal Courts Can Only Hear Certain Specific Types of Cases

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a mid-sized economy's currency losing a large share of its value against major reserve currencies over a few months, driven by capital fleeing the country and eroding investor confidence. As a mid-sized economy's currency instability triggers cross-border commercial disputes involving parties from different U.S. states, litigants want to understand exactly when a case can actually be brought in federal court rather than state court, and why federal courts, unlike most state courts, can only hear certain specific, limited categories of cases in the first place. This lesson examines this foundational jurisdictional distinction.

## Learning Objectives
- Explain why federal courts are described as courts of 'limited jurisdiction,' in contrast to most state courts, which generally have broader general jurisdiction.
- Explain 'federal question' jurisdiction, and why a case arising under federal law generally can be brought in federal court on this specific basis.
- Explain 'diversity jurisdiction,' the specific complete diversity requirement it generally imposes, and the amount-in-controversy threshold that must also generally be satisfied.
- Explain why a party's belief that federal court is a more favorable forum does not, by itself, create federal subject matter jurisdiction absent one of these specific recognized bases.

## Core Content
VERIFIED FACT: federal courts are generally described as courts of "limited jurisdiction," meaning they can only hear specific categories of cases that fall within the particular jurisdiction the U.S. Constitution and federal statutes have specifically granted them, in contrast to most state courts, which generally hold broader "general jurisdiction" extending to most types of legal disputes arising within that particular state, absent some more specific limitation.

Here is the "why" "federal question" jurisdiction generally allows a case arising under federal law to be brought in federal court: federal question jurisdiction generally applies when a plaintiff's own claim itself arises under the U.S. Constitution, a federal statute, or a federal treaty, meaning the plaintiff's own cause of action is itself specifically created by, or its resolution otherwise genuinely depends on, federal law rather than purely state law — REGION-SPECIFIC RULE, confirm current specific standards for what genuinely qualifies as a claim "arising under" federal law, since this analysis can become genuinely complex in certain specific circumstances, a case presenting a genuine federal question of this kind can generally be brought in federal court (and, in many circumstances, may also, or instead, be brought in an available state court, since state courts generally retain concurrent jurisdiction over many, though not all, federal law claims as well, absent a specific federal statute providing that federal jurisdiction is exclusive for that particular type of claim).

This is why "diversity jurisdiction" provides a second, genuinely distinct basis for federal court jurisdiction, imposing its own specific complete diversity requirement and amount-in-controversy threshold: diversity jurisdiction generally allows a case to be brought in federal court, even though it presents no federal question whatsoever and is based entirely on ordinary state law claims, specifically when the parties are citizens of different states (or one party is a foreign citizen) and the amount genuinely in controversy exceeds a specific statutory threshold, REGION-SPECIFIC RULE, confirm the current specific dollar threshold, which has been periodically adjusted by statute — the "complete diversity" requirement generally means that every single plaintiff must be a citizen of a different state than every single defendant (meaning even a single shared state of citizenship between any one plaintiff and any one defendant generally destroys complete diversity and defeats this specific basis for federal jurisdiction entirely), a considerably more demanding requirement than simply showing that at least some of the parties happen to be from different states. This is why a party's own belief that federal court represents a more favorable forum for their own particular case does not, by itself, create federal subject matter jurisdiction absent one of these two specific recognized bases: since federal courts can only hear cases falling within their own specifically, constitutionally and statutorily limited jurisdiction, a case that presents neither a genuine federal question nor satisfies the complete diversity and amount-in-controversy requirements for diversity jurisdiction simply cannot be heard in federal court, regardless of how strongly either party might genuinely prefer that particular forum for strategic, procedural, or other reasons — this is precisely why a federal court will generally dismiss a case, on its own initiative if necessary, upon determining that it genuinely lacks subject matter jurisdiction over the case actually presented, even if neither party has specifically raised or challenged this particular jurisdictional issue themselves, since subject matter jurisdiction, unlike certain other procedural defenses, generally cannot be waived by the parties' own mutual agreement or simple inattention to the issue.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: A plaintiff from one state sues a defendant from a different state over an ordinary state law contract dispute, with the amount genuinely in controversy exceeding the applicable statutory diversity jurisdiction threshold. Because complete diversity exists between the parties (no plaintiff shares a state of citizenship with any defendant) and the amount-in-controversy requirement is satisfied, this case can be brought in federal court under diversity jurisdiction, despite presenting no federal question whatsoever. A different case, involving parties from the same state and no federal law claim of any kind, could not be brought in federal court at all, regardless of either party's own preference for that forum, since neither federal question jurisdiction nor diversity jurisdiction would actually be satisfied.

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** This restriction to specifically granted categories of cases, unlike the broader general jurisdiction most state courts hold, is precisely why federal courts are described as courts of limited jurisdiction.

**Q2.** This requirement that the claim itself arise under federal law is precisely what federal question jurisdiction demands as a basis for federal court subject matter jurisdiction.

**Q3.** This demanding requirement that no plaintiff share a state of citizenship with any defendant is precisely what complete diversity requires, distinguishing it from a more lenient partial diversity standard.

**Q4.** This inherent limitation to specifically recognized jurisdictional bases, regardless of party preference, is precisely why subject matter jurisdiction cannot simply be created by a party's own strategic desire for a particular forum.

**Q5.** This direct contrast between a case satisfying diversity jurisdiction's specific requirements and one satisfying neither recognized basis exemplifies precisely why subject matter jurisdiction depends on these specific, limited grounds rather than party preference alone.

## Key Takeaways
- Federal courts have limited jurisdiction, hearing only cases falling within specific categories the Constitution and federal statutes have granted them, unlike most state courts' broader general jurisdiction.
- Federal question jurisdiction applies when a plaintiff's claim itself arises under the Constitution, a federal statute, or a federal treaty.
- Diversity jurisdiction requires complete diversity of citizenship between all plaintiffs and all defendants, plus an amount in controversy exceeding a specific statutory threshold.
- A party's preference for federal court does not, by itself, create subject matter jurisdiction absent satisfying one of these two specific recognized bases.
