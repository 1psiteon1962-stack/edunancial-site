# Summary Judgment Analysis: Why a Genuine Dispute of Material Fact Determines Whether a Case Goes to Trial

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a systemically important financial institution suddenly revealing a large, previously undisclosed loss, raising fears about counterparty risk rippling through the broader banking system. As fears about counterparty risk drive a rise in commercial litigation, parties on both sides of many disputes want to understand when a court can actually resolve a case without a full trial, through a procedural device specifically designed to avoid unnecessary trials in cases where the actual underlying facts are not genuinely disputed. This lesson examines that specific procedural standard.

## Learning Objectives
- Explain the general purpose of summary judgment, and why it exists specifically to avoid unnecessary trials.
- Explain what constitutes a 'genuine dispute of material fact,' and why both the genuineness and the materiality of a disputed fact matter to this analysis.
- Explain why a court evaluating a summary judgment motion must view the evidence in the light most favorable to the party opposing the motion.
- Explain why summary judgment is appropriate only when the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law, even after resolving any genuine factual disputes in the non-moving party's favor.

## Core Content
Summary judgment is a procedural device that allows a court to resolve a case, or a specific claim within a case, without proceeding to a full trial, when the court determines that no genuine dispute of material fact actually exists and the moving party is therefore entitled to judgment as a matter of law. VERIFIED FACT: this procedural device exists specifically to avoid the genuine time, expense, and burden of an unnecessary trial in cases where the actual underlying facts relevant to the outcome are not genuinely disputed between the parties, reflecting a recognition that a full trial is specifically designed to resolve genuine factual disputes, meaning a trial serves no genuine purpose when the material facts are not actually in genuine dispute in the first place.

Here is the "why" both the "genuineness" and the "materiality" of a disputed fact matter to this analysis: a fact is generally considered "material" if it could actually affect the outcome of the case under the applicable substantive law, meaning a factual dispute regarding some genuinely peripheral, ultimately inconsequential detail does not prevent summary judgment even if the parties genuinely disagree about that particular detail, since resolving that specific dispute would not actually change the ultimate legal outcome either way; and a dispute is generally considered "genuine" if the evidence presented would allow a reasonable factfinder to actually find in favor of the non-moving party on that specific disputed point, meaning a party cannot defeat summary judgment simply by asserting a bare, unsupported denial or a purely speculative, evidence-free disagreement with the moving party's own properly supported factual showing.

This is why a court evaluating a summary judgment motion must view the evidence in the light most favorable to the party opposing the motion: since summary judgment specifically resolves the case without the benefit of a full trial (and therefore without the benefit of a jury or judge actually observing witness testimony and weighing competing evidence and credibility in the way a full trial would allow), courts apply this specifically protective standard to ensure that summary judgment is granted only when the case genuinely presents no real factual dispute requiring this kind of full trial-based resolution, rather than a court simply weighing the evidence itself and effectively deciding disputed factual questions on its own, in the moving party's favor, without the full trial process that would ordinarily be required to properly resolve any genuinely disputed factual question. This is why summary judgment is appropriate only when the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law, even after resolving any genuine factual disputes in the non-moving party's favor: even where certain underlying facts are undisputed, or where any genuinely disputed facts have specifically been resolved in the non-moving party's favor for purposes of this specific analysis, summary judgment is appropriate only if the moving party would still be legally entitled to win the case even under this most favorable-to-the-opponent view of the actual facts — meaning a court denies summary judgment and allows the case to proceed toward trial whenever a genuine, material factual dispute remains that could actually change the ultimate legal outcome depending on how that specific dispute is eventually resolved, reserving the trial process specifically for the genuine factual disputes that actually require it, while allowing summary judgment to efficiently resolve the genuinely undisputed cases that do not.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: In a contract dispute, the parties agree on every fact relevant to whether a valid contract was actually formed, disagreeing only about a specific detail entirely unrelated to any element the applicable law actually requires for contract formation — a dispute that, however genuine, is not material and therefore does not prevent summary judgment. A different case, in which the parties genuinely and specifically dispute, with each side presenting supporting evidence, whether a key contractual term was actually agreed to at all, presents a genuine dispute of material fact, since resolving this specific disagreement would directly determine the case's ultimate outcome, meaning summary judgment would be inappropriate and the case would instead need to proceed to trial for a jury or judge to actually resolve this genuine, material factual dispute.

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** This specific efficiency-driven purpose — avoiding unnecessary trials when material facts are not genuinely disputed — is precisely why summary judgment exists as a distinct procedural device.

**Q2.** This connection between a fact's actual potential to affect the case's outcome and its materiality is precisely what determines whether a factual dispute is significant enough to prevent summary judgment.

**Q3.** This requirement that evidence actually support a reasonable factfinder's potential decision in the non-moving party's favor is precisely what distinguishes a genuine factual dispute from a bare, unsupported denial.

**Q4.** This protective, non-moving-party-favorable standard, ensuring summary judgment is not simply a substitute for a genuinely necessary trial, is precisely why courts apply this specific evidentiary standard.

**Q5.** This direct contrast between an immaterial, inconsequential dispute and a genuine, material dispute directly affecting the case's outcome exemplifies precisely why one case was suited to summary judgment while the other required trial.

## Key Takeaways
- Summary judgment allows a court to resolve a case without trial when no genuine dispute of material fact exists and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.
- A fact is material if it could affect the case's outcome, and a dispute is genuine if the evidence would allow a reasonable factfinder to decide in the non-moving party's favor.
- Courts must view the evidence in the light most favorable to the party opposing summary judgment, ensuring the device does not substitute for a genuinely necessary trial.
- Summary judgment is appropriate only if the moving party would still win even after resolving genuine factual disputes in the non-moving party's favor, reserving trial for cases that genuinely require it.
