# The Operating Agreement as a Risk-Allocation Map

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a widespread public health disruption halting global shipping and manufacturing for an extended stretch, snarling supply chains across many industries at once.

Apply it here: A long shipping shutdown cuts a small business's revenue in half. One partner wants to put in more money. The other cannot. Ask across colors: Does the agreement (PURPLE) require extra contributions? If one partner funds the gap, does their ownership share (BLUE) grow? Who decides whether to cut staff (BLACK)?

## Learning Objectives
- Identify the key risk-allocation clauses in an operating agreement
- Explain how capital calls, distributions, and voting interact
- Evaluate how an agreement handles deadlock and exit

## Core Content
At Level 2, you learned what contracts do. At Level 4, you analyzed partnership conflicts. At Level 5, you read an operating agreement (for an LLC) or partnership agreement as a map of who carries which risk when things go wrong.

The key clauses coordinate several colors:
1. Capital contributions and capital calls (BLUE + PURPLE): What did each partner put in? Can the company demand more later? What happens to a partner who cannot pay?
2. Distributions and waterfall (BLUE + GREEN): Who gets paid first? Is there a preferred return for investors before profits are split? Are distributions required to cover each partner's tax bill on pass-through income?
3. Voting and management (BLACK + PURPLE): Which decisions need a simple majority, which need everyone, and who runs daily operations?
4. Transfer restrictions (PURPLE): Can a partner sell their share to anyone, or do others get a right of first refusal?
5. Buyout and exit (BLUE + PURPLE): What triggers a buyout (death, disability, divorce, retirement, breach)? How is the price set? How is it paid?
6. Deadlock (BLACK + PURPLE): If two equal partners disagree, what breaks the tie? Mediation, a neutral third party, a buy-sell "shotgun" clause, or dissolution?

VERIFIED FACT: In many jurisdictions, if an LLC has no written agreement, default rules from the local statute fill the gaps. Those defaults may not match what the partners actually intended. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE.

The Level 5 insight: every clause answers a "what if." A good agreement is written while everyone is still friendly, because that is the only time people agree calmly on what happens when they are not.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: The clause that causes the most trouble in small partnerships is often the capital call. Partners assume everyone can put in more money when times get hard. A shock like the one in the scenario tests that assumption quickly.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: Priya and Tom each own 50% of a bakery LLC. Revenue drops sharply during a supply disruption. The bakery needs $40,000 to survive six months.

Their agreement says:
- Capital calls require both partners' approval.
- If one partner funds a capital call alone, that contribution becomes a loan to the company at a stated interest rate, repaid before any profit distributions.
- Deadlock goes to mediation, then to a buy-sell clause.

Priya can fund the $40,000. Tom cannot. Because the agreement treats solo funding as a loan, Priya does not automatically gain control, but she is protected: she gets repaid first with interest. Tom keeps his 50% ownership.

Without that clause, they might have argued for months about whether Priya's money should buy her more ownership. ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Their agreement did not prevent the crisis, but it prevented the crisis from turning into a partnership breakup.

Q2: B

Q5: B

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** Capital calls ask partners for additional contributions, often during hard times or expansion.

**Q2.** A waterfall sets who is paid first and how profits are split after that.

**Q3.** Statutory defaults apply, and they may not match what partners intended. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE.

**Q4.** Deciding the 'what ifs' before conflict is far easier than during it.

**Q5.** The loan treatment protected her money without shifting control.

## Key Takeaways
- An operating agreement is a map of who carries which risk
- Capital calls, waterfalls, voting, transfers, buyouts, and deadlock are the core clauses
- Default statutory rules fill gaps and may not match your intent
- Write the 'what ifs' while everyone is still friendly
