# Economic Ownership vs. Enterprise Control

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture two large economic powers escalating export controls against each other on a category of advanced technology components used across many industries.

Apply it here: Export controls suddenly cut a tech company off from a key supplier. The board must decide fast whether to redesign the product or sell the company. Ask across colors: Who actually gets to make that call (PURPLE/BLACK)? Is it the people with the most money invested (WHITE/BLUE), or the people with the most votes? They are not always the same people.

## Learning Objectives
- Distinguish economic rights from voting and control rights
- Explain how share classes and liquidation preferences separate them
- Evaluate the trade-offs of founder control structures for founders and investors

## Core Content
The Philosophical Framework for Advanced Coursework makes a teaching point that surprises many learners: owning part of a company's money is not the same as controlling what the company does.

Ownership rights can be split into separate bundles:
- Economic rights: the right to share in profits, dividends, and sale proceeds.
- Voting rights: the right to elect directors or managers and approve major decisions.
- Liquidation rights: the order in which owners are paid if the company is sold or wound down.
- Information rights: the right to see financial statements and records.

VERIFIED FACT: Many legal systems allow companies to issue different classes of shares with different combinations of these rights. For example, one class may carry more votes per share, while another carries a preference to be paid first in a sale.

How this coordinates colors:
- WHITE: An investor in a publicly traded company with multiple share classes may own economic value but have little voting power. That affects how much the investor can influence management.
- BLUE: Founders raising money can sell economic value while keeping control through voting shares or board seats. But investors often ask for protective provisions, such as a veto over selling the company.
- PURPLE: The company's charter and shareholder agreements are where all of this is written.
- BLACK: Control determines who leads during a crisis, like the one in the scenario.

Liquidation preference deserves special attention. ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: If an investor has a 1x preference on $2 million, they get their $2 million back before common shareholders receive anything in a sale. If the company sells for $3 million, the common shareholders split only the remaining $1 million, even if they "own" most of the shares.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Founder control can protect a long-term vision from short-term pressure. It can also shield weak leadership from accountability. Neither side is automatically right. The key is to understand what you are giving up or getting before you sign.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: Ana founds a company and raises money twice. After both rounds:
- Ana owns 40% of the shares, all Class B, with 10 votes per share.
- Investors own 60% of the shares, all Class A preferred, with 1 vote per share and a 1x liquidation preference on the $5 million they invested.

Votes: Ana has 40 x 10 = 400 vote units. Investors have 60 x 1 = 60 vote units. Ana controls about 87% of the votes while owning only 40% of the economic value.

Now the scenario hits. A buyer offers $6 million.
- Investors take their $5 million preference first.
- The remaining $1 million is shared according to the charter. If investor shares also take part in the remainder (participating preferred), Ana's share of that remainder shrinks even more.

Ana has the votes to reject the sale. But the investors may hold a protective veto over other key decisions. ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Ana's real power is not just her vote count. It is the full combination of charter terms, protective provisions, and her relationship with her board.

A) It is always good

## Answer Key
Q1: B
Q2: B
Q3: D
Q4: C
Q5: D

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** They are separate bundles that can be held by different people.

**Q2.** A 1x preference returns the investor's money first.

**Q3.** Multiple-vote shares separate control from economic ownership.

**Q4.** Charter and shareholder agreements define share classes and their rights.

**Q5.** This is labeled ASSUMPTION/OPINION because reasonable people disagree.

## Key Takeaways
- Economic ownership and enterprise control are separate bundles of rights
- Share classes, votes per share, and liquidation preferences split them
- Charters and shareholder agreements define who really decides
- Understand what you give up or gain before signing
