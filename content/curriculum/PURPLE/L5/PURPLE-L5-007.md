# Dilution, Anti-Dilution, and Protecting Founder Position

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a systemically important financial institution suddenly revealing a large, previously undisclosed loss, raising fears about counterparty risk rippling through the broader banking system.

Apply it here: A major bank's hidden loss shakes investor confidence, and startup funding dries up. A company that raised money last year at a high valuation now has to raise again at a lower one. Ask across colors: Whose ownership shrinks most (BLUE)? What do the contract terms (PURPLE) say? How should the founder negotiate (ORANGE)?

## Learning Objectives
- Calculate how new investment dilutes existing owners
- Explain how anti-dilution clauses work in a down round
- Negotiate funding terms using the full picture of dilution and control

## Core Content
VERIFIED FACT: When a company issues new shares, each existing owner's percentage goes down, even though the number of shares they hold stays the same. This is called dilution.

Dilution is not automatically bad. If the new money helps the company grow, a smaller percentage of a larger company can be worth more. The Level 5 question is whether the terms that come with the money shift more risk onto you than you realize.

Anti-dilution clauses protect investors if the company later sells shares at a lower price (a "down round"). Two common forms:
- Full ratchet: The earlier investor's price is reset all the way down to the new lower price, as if they had bought at that price originally. This can shift a large amount of ownership away from founders and employees.
- Weighted average: The earlier investor's price is adjusted partway down, based on how much new money comes in and at what price. This is usually less harsh.

This coordinates several colors:
- BLUE: How much money is truly needed, and could a smaller raise work?
- PURPLE: Which anti-dilution formula is in the documents, and are there carve-outs (for example, shares issued to employees)?
- ORANGE: Negotiating leverage. Founders with alternatives (a strong BATNA, from ORANGE-L4) get better terms.
- WHITE: Valuation logic. A very high earlier valuation can set up a painful down round later.
- BLACK: Employee morale. If employee options lose value, retention may suffer.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: A founder who chases the highest possible valuation in a good market may create a trap for themselves in a bad one. A fair, defensible valuation with clean terms often ages better.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: A company has 1,000,000 shares. The founder owns 800,000 (80%). An investor bought 200,000 shares at $5 each (20%, $1,000,000).

A year later, the scenario hits. The company raises $500,000 at $2.50 per share, issuing 200,000 new shares.

Without anti-dilution: Total shares = 1,200,000. Founder = 800,000 / 1,200,000 = 66.7%. First investor = 16.7%. New investor = 16.7%.

With full ratchet: The first investor's price resets to $2.50. Their $1,000,000 now converts into 400,000 shares instead of 200,000. Total = 1,400,000. Founder = 800,000 / 1,400,000 = about 57.1%. First investor = about 28.6%. New investor = about 14.3%.

With a weighted-average clause, the first investor's adjustment would land somewhere between these two outcomes.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: The difference between 66.7% and 57.1% is large. It may even change who controls the board. That is why the anti-dilution formula deserves as much attention in negotiation as the valuation itself.

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** Your share count stays the same, but your percentage falls as total shares rise.

**Q2.** Down rounds happen when valuations fall, often in tight funding markets.

**Q3.** Full ratchet resets the earlier investor's price all the way down.

**Q4.** 800,000 divided by 1,400,000 is about 57.1%.

**Q5.** An inflated earlier price makes later down rounds and anti-dilution adjustments more likely.

## Key Takeaways
- Dilution reduces percentage, not share count
- Anti-dilution clauses shift down-round risk from investors to founders and employees
- Full ratchet is harsher than weighted average
- Negotiate the formula as carefully as the valuation
