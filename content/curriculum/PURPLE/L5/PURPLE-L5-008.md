# Buy-Sell Agreements Funded with Insurance

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a widespread public health disruption halting global shipping and manufacturing for an extended stretch, snarling supply chains across many industries at once.

Apply it here: During a widespread public health disruption, business owners realize that an owner's sudden illness or death could leave partners, families, and employees in chaos. Ask across colors: Does a written buy-sell agreement (PURPLE) say who buys the share? Where does the money come from (BLUE/WHITE)? How is the payout taxed (GREEN)?

## Learning Objectives
- Explain the purpose of a buy-sell agreement
- Compare cross-purchase and entity-redemption structures
- Describe how insurance funds a buyout and why valuation terms matter

## Core Content
A buy-sell agreement answers a painful but necessary question: if an owner dies, becomes disabled, retires, or leaves, who buys their share, at what price, and with what money?

Without an agreement, a deceased owner's share may pass to a spouse or child who has no interest or skill in running the business. The surviving owners may end up with an unwanted partner. The family may be stuck owning part of a company they cannot sell. Employees may lose their jobs while everyone argues.

Common structures:
- Cross-purchase: The owners agree to buy each other's shares. Each owner often holds insurance on the others.
- Entity redemption: The company itself buys back the departing owner's share. The company often holds the insurance.
- Hybrid: A mix, sometimes giving the company the first option and the owners the second.

This coordinates several colors:
- PURPLE: The agreement sets triggers (death, disability, divorce, retirement, breach), the valuation method, and payment terms.
- BLUE/GOLD: Life and disability insurance provide cash at the moment it is needed. Without funding, a buy-sell agreement is just a promise nobody can afford to keep.
- GREEN: REGION-SPECIFIC RULE: The structure affects how insurance proceeds are taxed, what the buyers' new cost basis is, and whether the payment is treated as a sale or a distribution.
- BLACK: The agreement keeps leadership stable and reassures employees, lenders, and customers.

Valuation is where many agreements fail. A fixed price written years ago may be far off from today's value. Better options include a formula tied to earnings, an annual agreed value that owners actually update, or an independent appraisal at the time of the trigger.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: An outdated valuation clause is one of the most common sources of family-versus-partner lawsuits in small businesses.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: Luis and Mei each own 50% of a machine shop valued at about $1.2 million. They sign a cross-purchase buy-sell agreement:
- Trigger: death or long-term disability.
- Price: independent appraisal at the time of the trigger.
- Funding: each owns a $600,000 life policy and a disability buyout policy on the other.

Three years later, Mei dies unexpectedly. Luis receives $600,000 from the policy and uses it to buy Mei's shares from her estate. Her family receives fair value in cash. Luis now owns 100% and keeps the shop running. The employees keep their jobs.

A complication: the appraisal comes in at $1.4 million, so Mei's share is $700,000. The agreement says any amount above insurance is paid over five years through a promissory note. ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Because they planned for that gap in writing, the difference caused an adjustment, not a lawsuit.

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** It plans the ownership transition before the triggering event happens.

**Q2.** In cross-purchase, owners buy each other's shares directly.

**Q3.** Without funding, owners may be unable to afford the buyout.

**Q4.** A price set years ago can be far from current value and spark disputes.

**Q5.** Planning the gap in writing prevented a dispute.

## Key Takeaways
- A buy-sell agreement plans ownership transfer before a crisis
- Cross-purchase and entity redemption have different legal and tax effects
- Insurance turns the promise into cash when it is needed
- Keep valuation terms current and plan for funding gaps
