# Wills vs. Revocable Living Trusts: Probate, Privacy, and Cost

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a populous nation's real estate and consumer credit markets, after years of rapid expansion, beginning to show signs of a sharp correction as defaults start rising.

Apply it here: During a sharp real estate correction, a property owner dies. Ask across colors: Will the family be able to sell or refinance the property quickly (RED), or will it be frozen in a court process for months? Will investment accounts (WHITE) pass smoothly? Does the answer change depending on whether the owner used a will or a trust (PURPLE)?

## Learning Objectives
- Compare how wills and revocable trusts transfer assets
- Explain probate and why people plan around it
- Identify the step most people forget: funding the trust

## Core Content
At Level 2, you learned what wills and trusts are. At Level 5, you compare them as tools that coordinate real estate, investments, and business interests at the moment of death or incapacity.

A will is a legal document that says who receives your property and who manages your estate. In many places, a will goes through probate, a court-supervised process that confirms the will, pays debts, and transfers assets.

A revocable living trust is a legal arrangement where you (as trustee) hold title to assets for your own benefit during life, then a successor trustee takes over at your death or incapacity. Because the trust, not you personally, owns the assets, they often pass without probate. You can change or cancel a revocable trust while you are alive.

VERIFIED FACT: Probate records are public in many jurisdictions. Trust administration is usually more private.

How the colors connect:
- RED: Real estate in a trust can often be sold or managed by the successor trustee without waiting for a court. Owning property in more than one state or country may mean more than one probate process if it is not in a trust. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE.
- WHITE: Brokerage accounts can be titled in the trust or use transfer-on-death designations, depending on local law.
- BLUE: Business interests may need the operating agreement's permission before transfer to a trust.
- GREEN: REGION-SPECIFIC RULE: In many systems, a revocable trust does not change income taxes during life and does not by itself reduce estate taxes.

The most common failure: funding. A trust only controls assets actually transferred into it. People sign a trust, pay the lawyer, then never retitle the house or accounts. At death, those assets may go through probate anyway.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: A trust usually costs more upfront; probate may cost more later. For someone with property in several jurisdictions, business interests, or privacy concerns, a trust often makes sense. For someone with a simple estate in a place with a fast, low-cost probate process, a will may be enough. Most trust-based plans still include a "pour-over" will to catch anything missed.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: Elena owns a home, a rental property in another state, a brokerage account, and 30% of a family restaurant LLC.

Option 1: Will only. Her estate may face probate in her home state and a second process where the rental sits. The restaurant share passes under the will, subject to the operating agreement.

Option 2: Revocable trust with a pour-over will. She retitles both properties into the trust, names the trust on her brokerage account (or uses a transfer-on-death designation), and gets written consent from her restaurant partners to assign her LLC interest to the trust.

At her death, her successor trustee can manage the rental, keep collecting rent, and sell the home without waiting for court approval. The restaurant partners already agreed to the transfer, so there is no dispute.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Elena's upfront legal cost is higher, but she avoids two probate processes and keeps her affairs private. The key was not signing the trust. It was finishing the funding.

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** Probate is the court process many estates go through, especially those passing under a will.

**Q2.** Assets titled in the trust pass under the trust's terms.

**Q3.** An unfunded trust controls nothing.

**Q4.** Many operating agreements limit transfers, even to the owner's own trust.

**Q5.** It is a backup for anything that was not funded into the trust during life.

## Key Takeaways
- Wills often go through probate; funded revocable trusts often do not
- Probate is often public; trust administration is usually more private
- A trust only works if assets are actually retitled into it
- Business interests may need partner consent before transfer
