# How You Title Real Estate Changes Everything

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a central bank raising its benchmark interest rate sharply and repeatedly over a short stretch of months to fight rising inflation.

Apply it here: Rising rates push two siblings who co-own a rental to disagree: one wants to sell, one wants to hold. Ask across colors: Can one sibling force a sale (PURPLE)? If one dies, does their share go to the other sibling or to their own children? What happens to the loan (BLUE) and the tax basis (GREEN)?

## Learning Objectives
- Compare common forms of real estate title
- Explain how title affects inheritance, liability, and taxes
- Choose a title form that fits a specific goal

## Core Content
Many people sign closing documents without paying attention to one line: how the property is titled. That single line affects who inherits, whether a co-owner can force a sale, how creditors can reach the property, and how it is taxed at transfer.

Common forms (names and rules are REGION-SPECIFIC RULE):
- Sole ownership: One person owns it. At death, it passes by will, trust, or local inheritance law.
- Joint tenancy with right of survivorship: Co-owners own it together. When one dies, their share generally passes automatically to the survivors, outside the will.
- Tenancy in common: Each co-owner owns a separate share (which can be unequal). When one dies, their share passes to their own heirs, not automatically to the other owners.
- Marital forms: Some jurisdictions have special forms for married couples, such as community property or tenancy by the entirety, with their own creditor and inheritance rules.
- Trust ownership: The trustee holds title for beneficiaries (see PURPLE-L5-011 and -012).
- Entity ownership: An LLC or company holds title (see PURPLE-L5-004).

How colors connect:
- RED: Can a co-owner force a sale? In many places, co-owners can ask a court to divide or sell the property ("partition"), unless an agreement says otherwise.
- BLUE: Lenders may require all titleholders to sign the mortgage. Changing title may trigger loan clauses.
- GREEN: REGION-SPECIFIC RULE: In some systems, heirs may receive a "stepped-up" tax basis at death, while property given during life keeps the original basis. Title form can change how much basis adjusts.
- PURPLE: A written co-ownership agreement can override default partition rights and set buyout terms.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Joint tenancy is often used between spouses for simplicity, but between siblings or business partners it can produce surprising results, such as a partner's family receiving nothing.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: Siblings Nia and Jordan buy a duplex together.

Version 1: They take title as joint tenants with right of survivorship. Jordan dies. His half passes automatically to Nia. Jordan's two children receive nothing from the duplex, even though his will leaves "everything" to them. The will does not control joint tenancy property in many jurisdictions.

Version 2: They take title as tenants in common, 50/50, and sign a co-ownership agreement:
- Neither can force a sale for five years.
- After that, either can offer to buy the other out at an appraised price.
- If one dies, their share passes to their heirs, and the survivor has a first right to buy it.

Jordan dies. His children inherit his half. Nia has the first right to buy it at appraisal. The children can take cash instead of co-owning a rental with their aunt.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Version 2 took one extra document and a few hours with a lawyer. It matched what both siblings actually wanted.

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** Survivorship passes the share outside the will in many jurisdictions.

**Q2.** Tenants in common own separate shares that can be inherited.

**Q3.** In many places, co-owners can ask a court to divide or sell the property.

**Q4.** Survivorship rights generally override a will for that property.

**Q5.** The agreement replaced default rules with the siblings' actual wishes.

## Key Takeaways
- Title form decides inheritance, co-owner rights, and tax outcomes
- Joint tenancy often overrides a will for that property
- Tenancy in common plus a written agreement gives more control
- Always confirm title rules locally before signing at closing
