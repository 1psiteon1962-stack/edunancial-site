# Beneficiary Designations: The Forms That Override Your Will

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a systemically important financial institution suddenly revealing a large, previously undisclosed loss, raising fears about counterparty risk rippling through the broader banking system.

Apply it here: A financial institution's troubles push many people to move their accounts elsewhere. Ask across colors: When an account is moved (WHITE), does the old beneficiary form carry over, or does the new account start blank (PURPLE)? If blank, where does the money go at death? How does that affect taxes for heirs (GREEN)?

## Learning Objectives
- Explain why beneficiary designations often override a will
- Identify accounts that use beneficiary forms
- Build a routine to keep designations aligned with the estate plan

## Core Content
VERIFIED FACT: Many financial accounts pass by contract, not by will. When you name a beneficiary on a retirement account, life insurance policy, annuity, or a payable-on-death or transfer-on-death account, the institution generally pays that person directly, regardless of what your will says. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE: Availability and exceptions vary.

This is powerful and dangerous. Powerful because it skips probate and pays fast. Dangerous because outdated forms quietly override carefully written plans.

Common problems:
- An ex-spouse is still named years after a divorce.
- A beneficiary died first, and no backup (contingent) beneficiary was named.
- A minor child is named directly, which may require a court-supervised guardian to manage the money. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE.
- "My estate" is named, sending the money back through probate and possibly losing tax advantages.
- An account is moved to a new institution, and a new form is never filled out.

How colors connect:
- WHITE: Retirement and brokerage accounts carry the largest balances for many families.
- GREEN: REGION-SPECIFIC RULE: Inherited retirement accounts often have specific withdrawal timelines and tax treatment. Naming a trust vs. a person can change those timelines.
- GOLD: Insurance proceeds are often the fastest cash available to a family after a death.
- PURPLE: The will and trust set the plan; the beneficiary forms must match the plan.

The Level 5 habit: keep a single list of every account, who is named as primary and contingent beneficiary, and the date it was last checked. Review after every major life event: marriage, divorce, birth, death, and account moves.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: For many families, beneficiary forms control more money than the will does. They deserve the same attention.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: Carlos writes a will leaving everything equally to his three adult children. He also has:
- A retirement account naming his first wife, from whom he divorced 12 years ago.
- A life insurance policy naming his oldest child only, from before the other two were born.
- A brokerage account with no beneficiary.

At his death:
- The retirement account may go to his ex-wife, depending on local rules about divorce and beneficiary forms. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE.
- The insurance goes entirely to his oldest child.
- Only the brokerage account follows the will, through probate.

The "equal shares" plan in his will controls only a small part of his wealth.

Fix: Carlos reviews every account with his planner. He names his three children as equal primary beneficiaries on the retirement account and the policy, adds his revocable trust as contingent beneficiary, and adds a transfer-on-death designation to the brokerage account. ASSUMPTION/OPINION: One afternoon of paperwork made his will's intent come true.

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** The account agreement controls who receives the money.

**Q2.** Naming a contingent beneficiary prevents gaps if the primary has died.

**Q3.** Naming the estate defeats the probate-skipping benefit.

**Q4.** Beneficiary forms controlled the rest.

**Q5.** Marriage, divorce, births, deaths, and account moves all call for review.

## Key Takeaways
- Beneficiary forms often override the will for those accounts
- Outdated forms quietly undo careful estate plans
- Name contingent beneficiaries and avoid naming minors or 'my estate' directly
- Keep one list of every account and review after every life event
