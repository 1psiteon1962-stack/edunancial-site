# Insurance as the Legal First Line of Defense

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a major energy-producing region hit by a sudden regional conflict that knocks out a large share of its production capacity overnight, tightening global energy supply with little warning.

Apply it here: A sudden energy shock raises costs and disrupts operations for many businesses. Ask across colors: Does the business's insurance (GOLD/PURPLE) cover interruption from events like this, or does the policy exclude them? Do contracts with customers (BLUE) promise delivery the business can no longer make? Who bears the loss?

## Learning Objectives
- Identify the major types of insurance for owners and investors
- Explain how insurance works together with contracts and entities
- Read key parts of a policy: coverage, exclusions, and limits

## Core Content
Insurance is a contract (PURPLE) that moves certain risks to an insurer in exchange for a premium. For most owners, it is the first layer of protection, handling the everyday claims so entities and trusts only matter in rare, severe cases.

Main types:
- General liability: Injuries or property damage to others caused by the business.
- Property: Damage to buildings, equipment, and inventory.
- Business interruption: Lost income when operations stop due to a covered event.
- Professional liability (errors and omissions): Mistakes in professional services.
- Umbrella: Extra liability coverage above other policies.
- Key person: Pays the business if a critical person dies or is disabled.
- Landlord policies: Specific coverage for rental property (RED).

How colors connect:
- PURPLE: A policy's wording controls everything. Three parts matter most: what is covered, what is excluded, and the limits.
- BLUE: Customer and supplier contracts often shift risk through indemnity clauses ("you will cover my losses if…") and require certain insurance. A business can promise more in a contract than its insurance covers.
- RED: Lenders often require property insurance naming them as a loss payee.
- GOLD/WHITE: Insurance protects investment capital from being wiped out by a single event.

VERIFIED FACT: Policies commonly contain exclusions. Some exclude certain disasters, pandemics, war, or events that did not cause physical damage. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE: Coverage rules and regulator requirements vary.

The Level 5 move is matching three documents: the contracts you sign, the insurance you hold, and the entity that signs. If a contract promises to cover a customer's losses, does your policy cover that promise?

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Many owners learn what their policy excludes only after a claim is denied. Reading the exclusions once a year with an agent is one of the highest-value hours an owner can spend.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: Aisha runs a food distribution company. She has general liability, property, and business interruption coverage.

The energy shock hits. Her fuel costs double, and a supplier shuts down for two months. She files a business interruption claim. The insurer denies it: the policy only covers interruption caused by physical damage to her own property, and there was none.

Meanwhile, a major customer contract says Aisha must deliver on schedule or pay the customer's losses. The contract has no force majeure clause (covered in PURPLE-L5-039). Her liability policy does not cover contract penalties.

After the crisis, Aisha:
- Adds contingent business interruption coverage (covering a key supplier's shutdown), where available.
- Renegotiates customer contracts to add a force majeure clause and cap her liability.
- Moves the customer contracts into her operating company, separate from the company that owns her warehouse.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Aisha's first mistake was not buying too little insurance. It was signing contracts that promised more than her insurance and structure could support.

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** Insurance covers ordinary losses at a known cost.

**Q2.** These define what is paid, what is not, and how much.

**Q3.** Indemnity shifts losses from one party to another by contract.

**Q4.** The policy's trigger was physical damage, which did not happen.

**Q5.** Her contracts, insurance, and entity were not aligned.

## Key Takeaways
- Insurance is a contract; wording controls coverage
- Read coverage, exclusions, and limits every year
- Match contracts, insurance, and the signing entity
- Never promise more in a contract than your protection supports
