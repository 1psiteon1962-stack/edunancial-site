# Legal Due Diligence Before Buying a Business

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture two large economic powers escalating export controls against each other on a category of advanced technology components used across many industries.

Apply it here: A buyer is days from closing on a company that sells technology products. New export controls suddenly limit a key supplier. Ask across colors: Does the company's supply contract (PURPLE) allow termination in this situation? Does its financial value (BLUE/WHITE) depend on that supplier? Are there licenses or permits (PURPLE) that could be revoked?

## Learning Objectives
- List the main categories of legal due diligence
- Connect legal findings to price, structure, and financing decisions
- Decide when a finding should change or stop a deal

## Core Content
Due diligence is the investigation a buyer does before closing. Financial diligence (BLUE/GREEN) looks at numbers. Legal diligence (PURPLE) looks at rights, obligations, and risks that numbers may not show.

A practical legal checklist:
1. Entity and ownership: Is the company in good standing? Who actually owns it? Are there options or promises of ownership to others?
2. Material contracts: Customer, supplier, lease, loan, and partnership agreements. Look for change-of-control clauses that let the other side terminate if ownership changes.
3. Employment: Employee agreements, contractor classification (PURPLE-L5-031), benefit obligations, non-competes, and pending claims.
4. Intellectual property: Does the company actually own its brand, software, and content, or do founders or contractors own them? (PURPLE-L5-028)
5. Real estate: Owned property title, lease terms, zoning, and environmental issues.
6. Litigation: Pending or threatened lawsuits, government investigations, and settlement history.
7. Regulatory: Licenses, permits, data privacy compliance, export and import rules. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE.
8. Debt and liens: Loans, guarantees, and liens on company assets.

How findings change the deal:
- BLUE: A key customer contract that can be canceled on change of control may lower the price or require the customer's consent before closing.
- PURPLE: Findings drive specific reps, indemnities, and escrow sizes (PURPLE-L5-021).
- GREEN: Tax exposures affect whether an asset purchase or share purchase makes more sense (PURPLE-L5-023).
- BLACK: Some findings should stop a deal entirely. Knowing your walk-away conditions in advance protects you from deal fever.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: The most important diligence skill is not reading documents. It is asking "What happens to this company's value if this one contract, license, or person disappears?"

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: Andre is buying a small electronics accessories company for $2 million.

Diligence findings:
- 45% of revenue comes from one retailer, and that contract allows termination on change of control.
- The company's main product design was created by a freelance designer with no written IP assignment.
- The key supplier is in a region now affected by the scenario's export controls.
- One pending customer dispute worth about $40,000.

Andre's responses:
- Makes closing conditional on the retailer's written consent to the change of control.
- Requires the seller to obtain a signed IP assignment from the designer before closing.
- Lowers his offer to $1.7 million to reflect supply risk, with a $200,000 earnout if revenue holds for two years.
- Adds a specific indemnity and escrow for the pending dispute.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: None of these issues appeared in the financial statements. Each one could have cut the company's value sharply after closing.

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** Legal diligence complements financial diligence.

**Q2.** These clauses can put key contracts at risk in an acquisition.

**Q3.** Contractors may own what they create unless rights are assigned in writing.

**Q4.** Price and earnout adjusted for uncertain risk.

## Key Takeaways
- Legal diligence finds risks financial statements do not show
- Check entity, contracts, employment, IP, real estate, litigation, regulatory, and debt
- Findings shape price, structure, reps, escrows, and closing conditions
- Set walk-away conditions in advance
