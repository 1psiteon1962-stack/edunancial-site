# Contingencies in Real Estate Contracts as Risk Tools

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a central bank raising its benchmark interest rate sharply and repeatedly over a short stretch of months to fight rising inflation.

Apply it here: During rapid rate increases, a buyer signs a purchase contract, then the lender's loan approval falls through. Ask across colors: Does the contract (PURPLE) let the buyer cancel and recover the deposit? Did the buyer waive the financing contingency to win the deal (ORANGE)? How much deposit money (WHITE/BLUE) is now at risk?

## Learning Objectives
- Describe the main contingencies in real estate contracts
- Explain how deposits and deadlines interact with contingencies
- Evaluate the risk of waiving contingencies in competitive markets

## Core Content
A contingency is a condition in a contract that must be met for the deal to proceed. If it is not met, the protected party can often cancel without losing their deposit, as long as they follow the contract's deadlines and notice rules.

Common real estate contingencies:
- Financing: The buyer can cancel if they cannot obtain a loan on stated terms.
- Inspection: The buyer can inspect and cancel or renegotiate based on findings.
- Appraisal: The buyer can cancel or renegotiate if the property appraises below the price.
- Title: The buyer can cancel if title problems are not fixed.
- Sale of buyer's property: The buyer can cancel if they cannot sell their current property.
- Due diligence (commercial): Time to review leases, zoning, environmental reports, and finances.

REGION-SPECIFIC RULE: Standard contract forms, deadlines, and how deposits are handled vary widely by jurisdiction.

How colors connect:
- RED: Inspection and appraisal findings affect property value and repair costs.
- BLUE: Financing contingencies protect buyers when loan terms change, as in the scenario.
- ORANGE: In competitive markets, buyers often waive contingencies to make offers more attractive. That improves their chance of winning but moves risk onto them.
- WHITE: The earnest money deposit is often a meaningful amount of savings. Losing it is a real loss.
- PURPLE: Deadlines are critical. Missing a notice deadline can waive a contingency by default.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Waiving a contingency is a bet. Before waiving one, ask: "If this goes wrong, can I afford to lose the deposit, or close anyway?" If the answer is no, the waiver is gambling, not strategy. Partial approaches, such as shortening a contingency period instead of eliminating it, can balance competitiveness and protection.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: In a hot market, Beatriz offers on a small duplex. The seller has multiple offers.

Her offer:
- Price: slightly above asking.
- Deposit: $25,000.
- Inspection contingency: shortened from 14 days to 7 days, not waived.
- Financing contingency: kept, but she provides a strong written pre-approval.
- Appraisal contingency: partially waived. She agrees to cover up to $15,000 of any gap between appraisal and price.

Rates rise sharply during escrow. Her lender approves her, but at a higher rate. Because her financing contingency required approval "at a rate not exceeding" a stated number, and the new rate exceeds it, she has a choice: cancel and recover her deposit, or proceed. After recalculating cash flow, she proceeds but negotiates a $10,000 price reduction, using her right to cancel as leverage.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Beatriz stayed competitive without giving up every protection. Her carefully worded financing contingency turned a rate shock into a negotiation instead of a loss.

## Key Takeaways
- Contingencies let buyers exit if key conditions fail
- Deadlines and notice rules must be followed exactly
- Waiving contingencies trades protection for competitiveness
- Partial approaches can balance both; never waive what you cannot afford to lose
