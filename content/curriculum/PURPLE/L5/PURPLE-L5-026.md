# Commercial Lease Clauses That Shape Business Value

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a widespread public health disruption halting global shipping and manufacturing for an extended stretch, snarling supply chains across many industries at once.

Apply it here: A long disruption closes a restaurant for months. Ask across colors: Does the lease (PURPLE) excuse rent during a forced closure? Can the owner sell the business (BLUE) if the lease cannot be assigned to a buyer? Who owns the kitchen equipment installed in the building (RED)?

## Learning Objectives
- Identify commercial lease clauses that affect business value
- Explain how assignment and renewal terms affect a sale
- Negotiate lease terms with the full business plan in mind

## Core Content
For many businesses, the lease is one of the most important contracts they sign. It can quietly decide whether the business can survive a crisis, grow, or be sold.

Key clauses:
- Term and renewal options: How long the lease lasts and whether the tenant can extend it. A buyer may pay much less for a business with only one year left on its lease and no renewal option.
- Assignment and subletting: Can the tenant transfer the lease to a buyer? Many leases require landlord consent. Better terms say consent "will not be unreasonably withheld."
- Rent escalations: Fixed increases, increases tied to an inflation index, or percentage rent based on sales.
- Operating expenses: Who pays property taxes, insurance, and maintenance? "Net" leases pass more of these costs to the tenant. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE on terminology.
- Use clause: What business is allowed. A narrow use clause can block a future change in business model.
- Exclusivity: Protection against the landlord renting nearby space to a direct competitor.
- Improvements and fixtures: Who owns improvements the tenant installs when the lease ends.
- Personal guarantee: Often required for small businesses (see PURPLE-L5-009).
- Force majeure and rent abatement: Whether rent is reduced or paused during certain events. Many leases do not excuse rent at all.

How colors connect:
- BLUE: Lease length and assignability directly affect business sale value.
- RED: The landlord's financing and property value depend on reliable tenants, which affects how much they will negotiate.
- GREEN: REGION-SPECIFIC RULE: Tenant improvement costs and allowances have tax treatment.
- ORANGE: Negotiation leverage is highest before signing.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: A tenant who negotiates a lease only on the monthly rent often gives up far more value in the other clauses.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: Omar opens a café. The landlord offers a 5-year lease with no renewal, assignment only with the landlord's "sole discretion" consent, and an unlimited personal guarantee.

Omar's negotiated lease:
- 5 years plus two 5-year renewal options at market rent.
- Assignment to a qualified buyer with consent not unreasonably withheld.
- Personal guarantee limited to 12 months of rent, burning off after 3 years of on-time payments.
- Exclusivity: no other café in the shopping center.
- Rent abatement if the premises cannot legally operate for more than 30 days due to a government order.

Omar accepts a slightly higher starting rent to get these terms.

Six years later, he sells the café. Because the buyer can take over the lease with two renewal options remaining, the buyer pays substantially more than they would for a café with a short lease. ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Omar's lease terms were worth more than the rent difference many times over.

## Key Takeaways
- Lease terms can decide survival, growth, and sale value
- Renewal and assignment rights matter as much as rent
- Guarantees, exclusivity, and abatement clauses are negotiable
- Negotiate the whole lease before signing, when leverage is highest
