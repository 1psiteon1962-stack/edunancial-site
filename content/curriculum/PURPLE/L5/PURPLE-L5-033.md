# Securities Law Basics When Raising Money

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a systemically important financial institution suddenly revealing a large, previously undisclosed loss, raising fears about counterparty risk rippling through the broader banking system.

Apply it here: After a banking scare, a business owner cannot get a bank loan and decides to raise money from friends, family, and local investors instead. Ask across colors: Is selling shares or promising returns (BLUE) a regulated securities offering (PURPLE)? Who is allowed to invest (WHITE)? What disclosures must be made, and how must the offering be marketed (ORANGE)?

## Learning Objectives
- Explain why many fundraising methods are regulated as securities offerings
- Describe the general role of exemptions and investor qualifications
- Identify common fundraising mistakes that create legal risk

## Core Content
VERIFIED FACT: In many legal systems, selling shares, membership interests, convertible notes, or other investment contracts to raise money is considered a securities offering. Securities laws exist to protect investors from fraud and require either registration or an exemption. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE: Definitions, exemptions, and filing requirements vary by country and sometimes by state or province.

Full public registration is expensive and complex, so most small businesses rely on exemptions. Common exemption features (all REGION-SPECIFIC RULE):
- Limits on who may invest, such as investors who meet income or net-worth thresholds.
- Limits on how much can be raised.
- Limits on public advertising or general solicitation.
- Required filings or notices.
- Required disclosures, especially for less experienced investors.

A key principle across most systems: anti-fraud rules apply even when an offering is exempt. Misleading investors, hiding risks, or promising returns you cannot support can create liability.

How colors connect:
- BLUE: The structure (equity, debt, convertible note, revenue share) affects which rules apply.
- WHITE: Investor protections exist because private investments are often illiquid, risky, and hard to value.
- ORANGE: How you market the offering matters. Public social media posts about an investment opportunity may count as general solicitation.
- PURPLE: Subscription agreements, investor questionnaires, and disclosure documents create the record.
- BLACK: Taking money from friends and family creates relationship risk as well as legal risk.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: "It's just my uncle and a few friends" does not remove securities law. Talking to a securities attorney before accepting the first dollar is much cheaper than unwinding a non-compliant offering.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: Isabel needs $250,000 to expand her bakery chain. Banks have tightened lending after a financial scare.

Her first idea: post on social media offering "10% guaranteed returns" to anyone who invests $5,000 or more.

Her securities attorney flags problems:
- A public post may count as general solicitation, which some exemptions prohibit. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE.
- "Guaranteed returns" could be misleading. A small business cannot guarantee returns.
- Some investors may not meet the qualifications required for the exemption she would need.

Revised plan:
- Raise from a limited group of investors who meet the exemption's requirements.
- Use a written disclosure document describing the business, risks, use of funds, and financial condition.
- Use a subscription agreement and investor questionnaire.
- File any required notices.
- Describe potential returns honestly, with clear risk warnings.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Isabel raises slightly less money than she hoped, and more slowly. But she avoids an offering that could have forced her to refund investors or face penalties later.

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** Securities laws exist to protect investors.

**Q2.** Exemptions reduce requirements, within limits. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE.

**Q3.** Anti-fraud rules generally apply even when an offering is exempt.

**Q4.** Public solicitation and guaranteed-return promises create legal risk.

**Q5.** Relationship-based fundraising is still regulated.

## Key Takeaways
- Selling investment interests is often a regulated securities offering
- Exemptions have limits on investors, amounts, and advertising
- Anti-fraud rules apply even to exempt offerings
- Get securities advice before accepting the first dollar
