# Currency Clauses and Force Majeure in Uncertain Times

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a major energy-producing region hit by a sudden regional conflict that knocks out a large share of its production capacity overnight, tightening global energy supply with little warning.

Apply it here: A regional conflict cuts energy supply, and a manufacturer cannot deliver on time. Ask across colors: Does the contract's force majeure clause (PURPLE) excuse the delay? Does a price adjustment clause let the manufacturer pass on higher energy costs (BLUE)? If payment is in a foreign currency, who bears exchange-rate swings (WHITE)?

## Learning Objectives
- Explain force majeure and hardship clauses
- Describe price adjustment and currency clauses
- Draft contracts that allocate uncertain risks clearly

## Core Content
Contracts are written assuming the world will behave roughly as expected. When it does not, specific clauses decide who carries the cost.

Force majeure: A clause that excuses or suspends a party's performance when certain events beyond their control make performance impossible or seriously impractical. Key points:
- The clause usually lists covered events (natural disasters, war, government orders, epidemics, and so on). REGION-SPECIFIC RULE: Courts often read these lists narrowly.
- It often requires prompt notice and efforts to reduce the impact.
- Higher costs alone often do not count as force majeure unless the clause says so.
- Some jurisdictions have general legal doctrines that may excuse performance even without a clause, but they are often narrow. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE.

Hardship clauses: Require parties to renegotiate if events make performance much more expensive, even if still possible.

Price adjustment clauses: Tie prices to an index (such as an energy or materials index) or allow adjustments when input costs rise beyond a threshold.

Currency clauses: State the currency of payment and may:
- Fix an exchange rate.
- Share exchange-rate changes beyond a band.
- Allow renegotiation if a currency moves more than a set percentage.

How colors connect:
- BLUE: These clauses protect margins and cash flow during shocks.
- WHITE: Currency clauses are a form of contractual hedging, related to the currency risk concepts in WHITE.
- ORANGE: Customers may resist price adjustment clauses. Negotiation may result in caps or shared bands.
- PURPLE: Clauses must match insurance coverage (PURPLE-L5-017).

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: The best time to negotiate who carries a shock is before it happens, when neither side knows who will be hurt.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: Lucia's factory signs a one-year contract to supply parts to a foreign customer.

Her negotiated clauses:
- Force majeure: Covers war, government orders, natural disasters, and epidemics. Requires notice within 10 days and good-faith efforts to find alternatives. Delivery deadlines extend during the event.
- Price adjustment: If a published energy price index rises more than 20% above the starting level, prices adjust by a set formula, capped at a 12% increase per quarter.
- Currency: Prices are in the customer's currency. If the exchange rate moves more than 10% from the starting rate, the two sides split changes beyond that band equally.

Six months in, the energy shock hits. The energy index rises 45%. A government order briefly limits factory operations.

Results: Delivery deadlines extend during the government order under force majeure. Prices adjust within the cap. Lucia absorbs some energy cost, but not all of it. The customer knew the rules in advance, so the relationship holds. ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Without these clauses, Lucia might have faced penalties for late delivery while losing money on every part.

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** It addresses extraordinary events that prevent performance.

**Q2.** Many clauses and courts do not treat cost increases alone as force majeure. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE.

**Q3.** Hardship clauses address costly but still possible performance.

**Q4.** The band shared large currency moves.

**Q5.** Pre-negotiated clauses are fairer and easier to agree on.

## Key Takeaways
- Force majeure excuses performance for listed extraordinary events
- Higher costs often need a separate price adjustment or hardship clause
- Currency clauses share exchange-rate risk
- Negotiate shock allocation before shocks happen
