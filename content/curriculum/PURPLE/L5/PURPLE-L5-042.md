# Building Your Professional Team: Lawyers, CPAs, and Advisors

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a central bank raising its benchmark interest rate sharply and repeatedly over a short stretch of months to fight rising inflation.

Apply it here: When interest rates rise sharply, a business owner must decide whether to refinance, restructure debt, or sell a property. Ask across colors: Has the lawyer (PURPLE) talked to the CPA (GREEN)? Does the lender (BLUE) know about the planned restructuring? Is the financial planner (WHITE) aware of the tax consequences?

## Learning Objectives
- Identify the key professionals in an advisory team
- Explain why coordination between advisors matters
- Work effectively with professionals as an informed client

## Core Content
The curriculum repeats a principle from earlier levels: business is a team sport. The myth of the solo founder who does everything alone is inaccurate. Seeking expert help is competence, not weakness.

At Level 5, decisions span several colors at once. That means advice must also span several professions, and those professionals must coordinate.

A typical advisory team:
- Attorney (PURPLE): Entity structure, contracts, estate planning, disputes. Often several specialists over time: business, real estate, estate, employment, IP.
- CPA or tax advisor (GREEN): Tax planning, compliance, and financial statements.
- Financial planner or investment advisor (WHITE/GOLD): Investment strategy, retirement, and risk. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE: Understand whether the advisor has a fiduciary duty or a suitability standard.
- Insurance agent or broker (PURPLE/GOLD): Coverage design and review.
- Banker or lender (BLUE): Financing relationships.
- Industry mentors and board advisors (BLACK): Experience and judgment.

Why coordination matters:
- A lawyer may design a trust that the CPA knows will create a poor tax result.
- A CPA may recommend an entity election that conflicts with an investor agreement.
- A planner may suggest moving assets without knowing about a pending lawsuit, creating fraudulent transfer risk (PURPLE-L5-016).

Being an informed client:
- Bring clear questions and organized documents.
- Share the full picture, including goals and concerns.
- Ask each advisor how their recommendation affects the other colors.
- Hold occasional joint meetings on major decisions.
- Understand how each professional is paid and any conflicts of interest.

The doctor analogy applies: paying professionals is insurance against much larger losses.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: The most valuable thing a Level 5 learner brings to their advisory team is not legal or tax knowledge. It is the ability to see how all the pieces connect and ask each expert the right question.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: Samuel owns a manufacturing business and three rental properties. Rates rise sharply, and he considers selling one rental to pay down business debt.

Uncoordinated approach: His real estate agent lists the property. After the sale, his CPA tells him the timing triggered a large tax bill that a different strategy might have reduced. His lawyer notes that the property was security for a business loan guarantee and the lender needed to consent.

Coordinated approach (what he does next time): Samuel schedules one joint call with his attorney, CPA, and banker before listing another property.
- The CPA explains tax options and timing. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE.
- The attorney reviews loan documents and title.
- The banker explains how paying down debt affects covenants and rates.

Together, they recommend refinancing the business debt with a longer term instead of selling, keeping the rental's income and avoiding an immediate tax bill.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: The joint call cost a few hours of professional fees. It saved Samuel far more than it cost and prevented a decision he would have regretted.
