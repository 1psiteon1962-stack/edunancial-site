# Charitable Structures: Foundations and Donor-Advised Funds

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a major agricultural region suffering a severe, multi-season drought that sharply cuts the global supply of a key food commodity.

Apply it here: A severe drought cuts food supply in one region, and a family wants to help the affected communities over many years. Ask across colors: Which legal structure (PURPLE) gives the family the right balance of control and simplicity? How is giving treated for tax purposes (GREEN)? How should the charitable funds be invested while they wait to be granted (WHITE)?

## Learning Objectives
- Compare common charitable giving structures
- Explain the control, cost, and tax trade-offs
- Connect charitable planning to family governance and stewardship

## Core Content
Charitable giving can be simple, such as writing a check. For families giving larger amounts over many years, legal structures can add organization, tax benefits, and a way to involve the next generation.

Common structures (names and rules REGION-SPECIFIC RULE):
- Direct gifts: Giving directly to charities. Simplest.
- Donor-advised fund (DAF): An account at a sponsoring charitable organization. The donor contributes, may receive a tax deduction at contribution (where available), and later recommends grants. The sponsor has legal control, but usually follows the donor's recommendations when they meet its rules.
- Private foundation: A separate charitable entity created and controlled by the family. It offers maximum control (hiring staff, running programs, making grants) but usually has more paperwork, costs, public reporting, and rules, such as minimum annual payouts and limits on transactions with family members.
- Charitable trusts: Some jurisdictions allow trusts that split benefits between charity and family over time.

How colors connect:
- PURPLE: Structure determines who legally controls the money and what rules apply.
- GREEN: REGION-SPECIFIC RULE: Deductions, limits, and treatment of donated assets vary. Donating appreciated assets instead of cash may provide additional tax benefits in some systems.
- WHITE: Charitable funds are invested while waiting to be granted. An investment policy should match the giving timeline.
- BLACK/STEWARD: Charitable structures can teach younger family members about responsibility, research, and decision-making.
- ORANGE: Foundations may develop public reputations that affect family businesses.

Important: A charitable structure must serve charitable purposes. Using a foundation for personal benefit, such as paying family members excessive salaries or using assets personally, can violate rules and bring penalties.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: For many families, a donor-advised fund provides most of the benefits of organized giving with far less administration. A private foundation makes more sense when the family wants to run its own programs or employ staff.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: The Mendoza family wants to support farming communities affected by drought over the next 15 years.

Option 1: Donor-advised fund. They contribute appreciated shares worth $500,000. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE: In their jurisdiction, donating appreciated shares may allow a deduction for the shares' value without paying capital gains tax on the appreciation. The family recommends grants each year. Minimal paperwork.

Option 2: Private foundation. They would control investments and could hire a program officer to run their own water projects. But they would face annual filings, minimum payout rules, and higher costs.

They choose the donor-advised fund to start. Each year, the family council (see PURPLE-L5-044) meets, and younger members research charities and present recommendations. The fund's investments follow a moderate policy matched to their 15-year timeline.

After five years, as their giving grows and they want to run a specific irrigation program, they consider converting part of their giving into a private foundation.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: The family started simple and added complexity only when their goals required it. Their giving also became a training ground for stewardship.

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** The sponsor controls the assets but usually follows donor recommendations within its rules.

**Q2.** Foundations offer control at the cost of more administration.

**Q3.** Rules vary. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE.

**Q4.** Charitable structures must serve charitable purposes.

**Q5.** Giving became a training ground for responsibility.

## Key Takeaways
- Charitable structures range from direct gifts to DAFs, foundations, and charitable trusts
- Trade-offs involve control, cost, rules, and REGION-SPECIFIC tax treatment
- Charitable assets must serve charitable purposes
- Giving can train the next generation in stewardship
