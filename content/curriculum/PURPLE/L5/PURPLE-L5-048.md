# Honest Reassessment of an Active Legal Position

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a central bank raising its benchmark interest rate sharply and repeatedly over a short stretch of months to fight rising inflation.

Apply it here: A business sued a supplier when rates were low and its cash was strong. Now rates have spiked, cash is tight, and discovery has revealed weaknesses in its case. Ask across colors: Does the original legal reasoning (PURPLE) still hold? Can the business afford to continue (BLUE)? Is leadership (BLACK) holding on because of sunk costs or because the case is still strong?

## Learning Objectives
- Explain why active legal positions need regular reassessment
- Identify sunk cost and pride traps in disputes
- Decide when to continue, adjust, settle, or fall back and regroup

## Core Content
The third part of the knife-fight doctrine is honest ongoing reassessment. Committing fully to a legal move does not mean refusing to reconsider it. Conditions change. New facts appear. Costs grow.

Why reassessment matters in legal matters:
- Discovery reveals new evidence, sometimes helping, sometimes hurting.
- Legal costs often exceed early estimates.
- The other side's financial condition may change, affecting collectability (PURPLE-L5-041).
- Laws or court decisions may shift. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE.
- Your business's needs and cash position may change, as in the scenario.

Common traps:
- Sunk cost: "We've already spent $80,000, so we can't quit now." Money already spent is gone either way. The decision should be based on future costs and future value only.
- Pride: Wanting to prove you were right, even when continuing no longer serves your interests.
- Anchoring: Holding onto an early estimate of what the case is worth.

A reassessment routine (at major milestones, such as after discovery, before trial, or when a settlement offer arrives):
1. Update the BATNA (PURPLE-L5-040) with current facts.
2. Estimate remaining costs and time, not total costs.
3. Check collectability.
4. Ask whether the matter still fits your broader goals (BLACK).
5. Get a candid assessment from counsel, and invite them to disagree with you.

Options:
- Continue as planned.
- Adjust strategy (narrow the claims, seek mediation).
- Settle.
- Fall back and regroup: pause, preserve your position, strengthen resources, and return later if appropriate. The military analogy from earlier levels applies. Falling back is a legitimate tactic, not failure.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: The strongest legal strategists are not the ones who never retreat. They are the ones who retreat at the right time and for the right reasons.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: Victor's company sued a former supplier for $400,000 for defective components. At the start, his attorney estimated a 70% chance of winning and $120,000 in fees.

Eighteen months later:
- Fees spent so far: $150,000.
- Discovery showed that Victor's team changed specifications late in the project, weakening his case. Counsel now estimates a 45% chance of winning.
- Remaining fees through trial: about $100,000.
- Rates have spiked, and Victor's company needs cash for operations.
- The supplier offers $140,000 to settle.

Victor's first reaction: "We've spent $150,000. We can't settle for less than that."

His reassessment:
- Future expected value of continuing: 45% x $400,000 = $180,000, minus $100,000 remaining fees = about $80,000, arriving in a year, with risk.
- Settlement: $140,000 now.
- The $150,000 already spent is gone in both cases.

He accepts the settlement. ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Victor's willingness to reassess honestly, rather than chase his sunk costs, protected his company's cash at a critical time.

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** Changing conditions can change the right decision.

**Q2.** Money already spent is gone either way.

**Q3.** Future costs drive the decision.

**Q4.** The military analogy frames it as strategic.

**Q5.** Honest reassessment showed settlement was better.

## Key Takeaways
- Committing fully does not mean refusing to reconsider
- Ignore sunk costs; decide on future costs and value
- Reassess at major milestones with candid counsel
- Falling back and regrouping is a legitimate strategy
