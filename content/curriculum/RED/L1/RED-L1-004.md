## Learning Objectives

- Identify the most common financing options for investment properties
- Calculate the impact of leverage on returns using a simple example
- Understand the role of the debt-service coverage ratio (DSCR) in lender decisions
- Recognize the risks of over-leveraging in real estate

## Core Content

Financing is the mechanism that makes real estate investing accessible without unlimited capital. Used correctly, leverage amplifies returns. Used recklessly, it amplifies losses.

### Conventional Investment Property Loans

**Terms**: 20–25% down payment, 15 or 30-year fixed, slightly higher rate than a primary residence loan.

Most lenders require the borrower to have 6 months of PITI (principal, interest, taxes, insurance) reserves in savings. Investment property loans are slightly stricter than owner-occupied loans.

**Tip**: Rates for investment properties are typically 0.5–1.0% higher than primary residence rates. Shop multiple lenders.

### FHA and VA Loans

FHA and VA loans require owner-occupancy. However, if you purchase a 2–4 unit property and live in one unit, you can use FHA (3.5% down) or VA (0% down) financing and rent the other units. This is the **house hacking** strategy.

### Portfolio Loans

Portfolio lenders hold loans on their own books rather than selling to secondary markets. This gives them flexibility in underwriting — especially useful for investors with multiple properties or non-W2 income.

### Hard Money Loans

Short-term loans (6–24 months) with high interest rates (9–15%) used for fix-and-flip projects or bridge financing. Hard money is expensive — it is a tool, not a default strategy.

### The Power of Leverage

Without leverage:
- Buy $200,000 property with $200,000 cash
- Property appreciates 5% → $10,000 gain → 5% return on cash

With leverage (20% down):
- Buy $200,000 property with $40,000 down
- Property appreciates 5% → $10,000 gain → 25% return on cash invested

Leverage amplifies gains — and losses. A 10% price decline on the all-cash purchase costs $20,000. The same decline on a 20%-down purchase wipes out your entire $40,000 equity.

### Debt-Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR)

DSCR = NOI / Annual Debt Service

Lenders typically require DSCR of 1.25 or higher. A DSCR of 1.0 means the property just covers the mortgage. A DSCR of 1.25 means income is 25% greater than debt obligation.

**Example**: NOI = $15,000/year; mortgage = $12,000/year → DSCR = 15,000 / 12,000 = 1.25 ✓

### Risks of Over-Leveraging

Investors who borrow maximum leverage leave no margin for vacancy, repairs, or market downturns. When rents fall or properties sit vacant, over-leveraged investors face negative cash flow and potential default.

**Rule of thumb**: If a property only cash flows with maximum leverage, it is marginal. If it still cash flows with 30–40% down, it has a margin of safety.

## Key Terms

- **Leverage**: Using borrowed money to amplify returns on invested capital
- **DSCR (Debt-Service Coverage Ratio)**: NOI divided by annual debt service; measures loan repayment capacity
- **PITI**: Principal, interest, taxes, and insurance — the four components of a mortgage payment
- **Hard Money Loan**: Short-term, asset-based loan at high interest rates, used for flipping or bridge financing
- **House Hacking**: Buying a multi-unit property, living in one unit, and renting the others to offset mortgage costs
