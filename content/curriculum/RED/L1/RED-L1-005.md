## Learning Objectives

- Identify the primary channels for finding off-market and on-market investment properties
- Apply a repeatable due diligence checklist before making an offer
- Calculate maximum allowable offer (MAO) using the 70% rule for fix-and-flip analysis
- Recognize red flags in property condition, title, and neighborhood

## Core Content

Finding the right property is part art and part systematic process. Investors who wait for the perfect deal never buy. Investors who skip due diligence often regret what they bought.

### Where to Find Investment Properties

**MLS (Multiple Listing Service)**
Properties listed by licensed agents. Competitive but accessible. Filter for properties with price reductions, days-on-market over 30, and motivated sellers.

**Direct Mail**
Mailing postcards to absentee owners, vacant properties, or owners in pre-foreclosure generates off-market leads. Response rates are 1–3% but competition is low.

**Driving for Dollars**
Physically driving neighborhoods looking for distressed properties — overgrown lawns, broken windows, newspaper piling up. These often indicate absentee or motivated owners.

**Wholesalers**
Real estate wholesalers find distressed properties and assign purchase contracts to investors for a fee. Their deals are often already analyzed — verify the numbers independently.

**Foreclosure Auctions**
County courthouse steps, online auction platforms, and bank REO (Real Estate Owned) lists all offer distressed properties. Due diligence is limited and risks are higher.

**Networking**
Probate attorneys, divorce attorneys, tax accountants, and property managers often encounter motivated sellers before properties hit the market.

### Property Due Diligence Checklist

Before making an offer:
- [ ] Verify legal description and confirm parcel matches address
- [ ] Review title for liens, easements, and encumbrances
- [ ] Calculate cash flow using full expense analysis
- [ ] Obtain a property inspection from a licensed inspector
- [ ] Verify zoning allows intended use
- [ ] Review local rent comps from recent leases
- [ ] Walk the neighborhood at different times of day
- [ ] Check flood zone designation (FEMA maps)
- [ ] Research property tax history and assessment

### The 70% Rule for Fix-and-Flip

For properties requiring renovation:
MAO = (After Repair Value × 70%) − Estimated Repair Costs

**Example**: ARV = $200,000; Repairs = $30,000
MAO = ($200,000 × 0.70) − $30,000 = $140,000 − $30,000 = $110,000

The 70% rule preserves a profit margin and covers holding costs (mortgage, insurance, utilities, taxes while renovating) and transaction costs (closing costs, agent fees).

### Red Flags to Avoid

**Property Condition**
- Foundation cracks indicating structural movement
- Evidence of water intrusion or mold
- Outdated electrical panels (knob-and-tube, Federal Pacific)
- Galvanized steel plumbing with low useful life remaining

**Title Issues**
- Unpaid tax liens or judgment liens
- Boundary disputes or encroachments
- Missing or unclear chain of title

**Neighborhood**
- Declining population and employment trends
- High crime rates near the property
- Excessive vacancy in nearby rentals
- Environmental contamination nearby

## Key Terms

- **MAO (Maximum Allowable Offer)**: The highest price an investor can pay and still achieve target returns
- **ARV (After Repair Value)**: Estimated market value of a property after renovations are complete
- **REO (Real Estate Owned)**: Properties owned by banks following foreclosure
- **Due Diligence**: The research and verification process before committing to a purchase
- **Chain of Title**: The historical sequence of ownership transfers for a property
