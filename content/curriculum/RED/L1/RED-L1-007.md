## Learning Objectives

- Differentiate between tax lien and tax deed investing
- Describe the tax deed auction process and what rights the buyer receives
- Apply a pre-auction research process to identify properties worth bidding on
- Identify the key legal and financial risks of tax deed purchases

## Core Content

Tax deed investing gives you the opportunity to purchase real property — not just a lien — directly from the county for a fraction of its market value. The county acquires the property through the tax lien foreclosure process and then sells it to the highest bidder to recover the delinquent taxes.

### Tax Deed vs. Tax Lien

| | Tax Lien | Tax Deed |
|--|---------|---------|
| What you buy | Certificate (debt) | Property title |
| Potential return | Interest (8–36%) | Property itself |
| Time to outcome | Months to years | Immediate (at auction) |
| Primary risk | Low value property | Title clouds, condition |
| States | ~30 states | ~20 states, some hybrid |

### How Tax Deed Auctions Work

1. County forecloses on properties with expired, unredeemed tax liens
2. County schedules and advertises a public auction
3. Opening bid = back taxes + fees + interest owed
4. Auction proceeds until no higher bids are received
5. Winning bidder receives a tax deed — a form of deed from the county
6. Winning bidder now owns the property

### What You Get — and What You Don't

A tax deed transfers ownership of the property. However, it does not automatically clear all other liens or claims. In most states, the tax deed process extinguishes junior liens, but:

- Federal IRS liens may survive (IRS has a 120-day right of redemption)
- Undisclosed easements may remain
- Title insurance is harder to obtain immediately after auction

**Best practice**: After acquiring a tax deed property, pursue a quiet title action to clear the title before selling or refinancing.

### Pre-Auction Research Process

1. **Obtain the auction list** from the county assessor or tax collector
2. **Drive or view each property** — exterior condition assessment
3. **Research ownership history** — find the current assessed value
4. **Check for senior liens** — run a preliminary title search
5. **Estimate repair costs** — assume worst-case inside condition
6. **Calculate maximum bid**: ARV × 70% − Estimated Repairs − Quiet Title Cost − Closing Costs

### Setting Your Maximum Bid

Never bid in the excitement of the auction. Set your maximum bid before the auction based on your analysis. Auction fever is real — it has caused many investors to overpay.

**Rule**: If bidding exceeds your pre-calculated maximum, stop. Walk away and find the next deal.

### Common Risks

- **Occupied properties**: Eviction may be required; budget time and cost
- **Environmental contamination**: Buyer assumes liability
- **Unknown interior condition**: What looks fine outside may need $50,000 inside
- **Redemption rights**: Some states allow a post-deed redemption period

## Key Terms

- **Tax Deed**: Legal document transferring property ownership from the county to the buyer at a tax sale
- **Quiet Title Action**: A lawsuit that establishes clear ownership of a property, removing competing claims
- **Redemption Right**: Owner's right to reclaim a property after a tax deed sale (varies by state)
- **Junior Lien**: A lien subordinate to the tax lien, typically extinguished by the tax deed process
- **IRS Right of Redemption**: The federal government's 120-day right to redeem a property subject to a federal tax lien
