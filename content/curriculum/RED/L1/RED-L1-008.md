## Learning Objectives

- Describe how seller financing, subject-to, and lease options work mechanically
- Identify the scenarios in which creative financing is most likely to be accepted by sellers
- Calculate the economics of a subject-to deal compared to a conventional purchase
- Recognize the legal considerations and disclosures required in creative finance transactions

## Core Content

Creative financing allows investors to acquire properties without qualifying for a traditional bank loan, and sometimes without any cash down. These strategies require creativity, negotiation skill, and a thorough understanding of the legal framework in your state.

### Seller Financing (Owner Carry)

The seller acts as the bank. Instead of paying a lender, the buyer makes monthly payments directly to the seller.

**When sellers accept seller financing:**
- Free-and-clear properties (no mortgage to pay off)
- Sellers seeking monthly income (retirement income)
- Sellers who want installment-sale tax treatment to spread capital gains
- Properties that are difficult to finance conventionally

**Typical terms**: 5–10% down, 5–8% interest, 15–30 year amortization, 3–7 year balloon payment.

**Investor benefit**: No bank qualifying, faster closing, negotiable terms, potentially lower total cost.

### Subject-To Existing Financing

You acquire the property deed while the seller's existing mortgage remains in place. You make payments on the seller's loan without assuming it or formally taking title to the debt.

**How it works**: Seller deeds you the property. You take over payments on their existing mortgage. Seller's name stays on the loan.

**Risk**: The due-on-sale clause allows the lender to call the loan due when ownership transfers. In practice, lenders rarely enforce this as long as payments are current. However, this risk must be disclosed to the seller.

**Best use case**: Seller behind on payments, facing foreclosure, with a low-interest mortgage you want to preserve.

**Example**: Seller has a 3.5% mortgage in a 7% rate environment. Assuming that loan is extraordinarily valuable.

### Lease Option (Rent-to-Own)

You lease the property with the option (not obligation) to purchase it within a set period.

**Components**:
- **Option consideration**: Upfront, non-refundable fee (1–5% of purchase price) securing your right to buy
- **Monthly rent**: Portion may be credited toward purchase price
- **Option price**: Fixed at lease signing, typically at or slightly above current value
- **Option period**: 1–3 years typically

**Investor strategy**: Control a property with minimal upfront capital, lock in today's price, and decide at option expiration whether to exercise.

### Wraparound Mortgage

A seller with an existing mortgage creates a new, larger mortgage to the buyer. The buyer makes payments to the seller at the new rate; the seller continues paying the underlying mortgage.

**Example**: Seller has a $100,000 mortgage at 4%. Investor buys at $150,000 using a $140,000 wraparound at 6%. Seller collects $840/month from investor, pays $477/month on their mortgage, keeps the spread.

### Legal Considerations

Creative financing requires proper documentation:
- Real estate attorney to draft contracts
- Full disclosure of all terms to both parties
- Title company or escrow to close the transaction properly
- Compliance with Dodd-Frank seller financing rules (for 1–4 unit residential properties)

## Key Terms

- **Seller Financing**: Arrangement where the seller provides a mortgage loan directly to the buyer
- **Subject-To**: Acquiring a property deed while the seller's existing mortgage remains in place
- **Due-on-Sale Clause**: Mortgage provision allowing the lender to demand full repayment upon property transfer
- **Lease Option**: Agreement giving a tenant the right (but not obligation) to purchase a property within a set period
- **Wraparound Mortgage**: A junior mortgage that encompasses an existing senior mortgage, with the seller collecting and forwarding payments
