## Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
1. State the exact unit-count threshold that separates residential from commercial real estate financing.
2. Explain how the valuation method changes once a property crosses that threshold.
3. Identify which loan type applies to a property based on its unit count.
4. Apply this threshold when evaluating a real property scenario.

## Core Content

In real estate, "residential" and "commercial" don't just describe how a property looks or how it's used day to day. They describe two entirely separate financing and valuation systems. The dividing line is **unit count**:

- **1 to 4 units** = Residential (single-family home, duplex, triplex, fourplex)
- **5 or more units** = Commercial (apartment building, commercial multifamily)

**VERIFIED FACT:** This 1–4 vs. 5+ threshold is the standard used by Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, and FHA for classifying residential vs. commercial multifamily lending in the United States.

### How Valuation Changes at the Threshold

Residential property (1–4 units) is valued using **comps** — comparable sales of similar properties nearby. Once a property hits 5+ units, commercial multifamily is valued based on **income** — specifically, the property's **Net Operating Income (NOI)** and the **capitalization rate (cap rate)**:

**Value ≈ NOI ÷ Cap Rate**

**ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE:** A 4-unit fourplex gets valued mostly by what similar fourplexes sold for nearby. A 12-unit apartment building gets valued mostly by how much rental income it produces after expenses, divided by the market's expected rate of return.

### How Financing Changes at the Threshold

**1–4 units (residential):**
- Conventional mortgages, FHA loans, VA loans are all available
- Underwriting looks heavily at YOUR personal income, credit, and debt-to-income ratio
- Loan terms are commonly 15 or 30 years, fixed-rate options widely available

**5+ units (commercial):**
- Requires a commercial mortgage or commercial multifamily loan
- Underwriting looks heavily at the PROPERTY's income (NOI, debt service coverage ratio)
- Loan terms are often shorter (5, 7, or 10 years) with a balloon payment
- Down payments are typically higher, often 20–30%+

**REGION-SPECIFIC RULE:** Specific down payment percentages, loan terms, and DSCR requirements vary by lender, country, and local banking regulation. The figures above are general U.S. market patterns.

### Why the Threshold Exists

A 1–4 unit property is small enough that it behaves like a single-family home in the eyes of a lender. A 5+ unit property starts to behave like a small business: it generates its own revenue, has its own operating expenses, and its ability to pay back a loan depends on how well that "business" performs.

Government-backed loan programs (FHA, VA, conventional conforming loans) cap out at 4 units. Once you're clearly operating an income-producing business (5+ units), you move into commercial lending.

**Key Definitions:**
- **Unit Count:** The number of separate, independently livable dwelling spaces in a property.
- **Comps (Comparable Sales):** Valuation method based on recent sale prices of similar nearby properties.
- **Net Operating Income (NOI):** Total income minus operating expenses, before mortgage payments and taxes.
- **Cap Rate (Capitalization Rate):** The rate of return an investor expects on a commercial property.
- **Debt Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR):** Whether a property's income is sufficient to cover its loan payments.

## Worked Examples

**ESTIMATE (for illustration only):**

*Property A — Fourplex (4 units):* Comparable fourplexes sold for $480,000–$520,000. Estimated value ≈ $500,000 using comps. A buyer can pursue a conventional residential mortgage.

*Property B — 8-Unit Building:* Annual income $144,000. Operating expenses $54,000. NOI = $90,000. At a 6% cap rate, estimated value = $90,000 ÷ 0.06 = $1,500,000. Requires commercial financing.

## Common Mistakes

- Assuming a 5-unit property can be financed like a fourplex.
- Valuing a small apartment building using comps only.
- Ignoring the Debt Service Coverage Ratio.
- Confusing "residential" with "small" and "commercial" with "big" — the distinction is unit count.

## Key Takeaways

- 1–4 units = residential financing and comps-based valuation. 5+ units = commercial financing and income-based valuation.
- Crossing this threshold changes the loan type, down payment expectations, loan term structure, and valuation method.
- Know which side of the line a property sits on before you evaluate it — it determines your entire approach.

## Practical Exercise

You're evaluating two potential purchases:
1. A triplex (3 units) renting for $3,900/month.
2. A 6-unit building renting for $9,000/month, with $3,200/month in operating expenses.

For each, identify: (a) residential or commercial, (b) valuation method, (c) expected financing type. Calculate the estimated value of the 6-unit building at a 7% cap rate.

*(Answer: Property 1 is residential, comps-based, conventional financing. Property 2 is commercial, income-based, commercial financing. NOI = $5,800/month = $69,600/year. Value = $69,600 ÷ 0.07 ≈ $994,286.)*
