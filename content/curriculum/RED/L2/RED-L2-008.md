## RED-L2-008
### Increasing Leverage: What Happens to Cash Flow, Reserves, and Survival

**1. Learning Objectives**
By the end of this lesson, the learner will be able to:
- Explain how increasing leverage on a property changes monthly cash flow.
- Describe why reserves become more important as leverage increases.
- Assess how leverage affects an investor's ability to survive a vacancy.

**2. Core Content**
Building on the previous lesson, increasing leverage — taking a smaller down payment relative to a property's price — doesn't just increase risk in the abstract. It changes specific, everyday numbers: monthly mortgage payment rises, cash flow available after expenses shrinks, and the buffer required to survive a bad month narrows.

A highly leveraged property may still "cash flow" on paper during normal months, but with less margin. A single missed rent payment, an unexpected repair, or a longer-than-expected vacancy can turn a thin positive cash flow negative. This is why reserves — cash set aside specifically for a property, separate from rent income — become more critical as leverage increases. Reserves are the buffer between a temporary setback and a missed mortgage payment.

Survivability is the real question Level 2 investors should ask: not "does this cash-flow in a good month?" but "can this property, and I, survive three bad months in a row?"

**3. Worked Example**
Two versions of the same $300,000 rental: Scenario A uses 30% down, producing a lower mortgage payment and $400/month positive cash flow. Scenario B uses 10% down, producing a higher mortgage payment and only $75/month positive cash flow.

Both scenarios look "profitable" on paper. But when a two-month vacancy hits, Scenario A's owner has more built-in cushion and stronger reserves relative to the smaller mortgage. Scenario B's owner, with thinner monthly margin and a larger fixed payment, must lean much harder on reserves — and if reserves are thin, faces real risk of missing a mortgage payment.

**4. Practice Quiz**
1. As leverage increases, what typically happens to monthly cash flow, all else equal?
   a) It increases
   b) It decreases
   c) It stays exactly the same
   d) It becomes irrelevant
2. What is the main purpose of reserves?
   a) To pay for vacations
   b) To buffer against vacancies, repairs, or income gaps
   c) To increase the down payment
   d) To avoid ever getting a mortgage
3. True or False: A property that cash-flows positively in a normal month is automatically safe from all future risk.
4. What is the key survivability question this lesson recommends asking?
   a) Does this cash-flow in a good month?
   b) Can this property survive several bad months in a row?
   c) Is this the cheapest property available?
   d) Will the market always go up?

**5. Answer Key**
1. b  2. b  3. False  4. b

**6. Answers and Explanations**
1. Higher leverage means a larger loan and typically a higher mortgage payment, shrinking cash flow.
2. Reserves exist specifically to absorb unexpected costs or income gaps without missing obligations.
3. A thin positive margin in good months can turn negative quickly during a bad stretch.
4. Level 2 thinking reframes the question from best-case performance to worst-case survivability.

**7. Key Takeaways**
- Higher leverage increases the mortgage payment and shrinks the cash-flow margin.
- Reserves become more critical as leverage increases, acting as a buffer against setbacks.
- A property that looks profitable in a normal month may not survive several bad months without reserves.
- The real Level 2 question is survivability, not just best-case profitability.
