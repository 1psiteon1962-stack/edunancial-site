## RED-L2-009
### Reserves and Vacancy: Preparing for the Piece That Might Not Move

**1. Learning Objectives**
By the end of this lesson, the learner will be able to:
- Define vacancy rate and explain its financial impact.
- Explain how reserves are typically sized relative to expenses.
- Describe how vacancy interacts with leverage and cash flow.

**2. Core Content**
Every rental property will, at some point, sit vacant between tenants — this is normal, not a sign of failure. The question is whether the investor planned for it. Vacancy rate is the percentage of time (or units, in a multi-unit property) that a property sits unrented over a given period.

Reserves are typically built as a set number of months of expenses — mortgage, taxes, insurance, and estimated maintenance — held in cash specifically for the property. Common practice among many investors is targeting three to six months of expenses in reserve, though the right amount depends on the property, market, and investor's overall financial picture, and is not a fixed universal rule.

Vacancy interacts directly with leverage: a highly leveraged property has a larger fixed monthly obligation, meaning a vacancy month costs more in absolute terms and depletes reserves faster than the same vacancy would on a less leveraged property.

**3. Worked Example**
Wanda owns a rental with a £1,600 monthly mortgage, tax, and insurance payment. She keeps £9,600 in reserves — six months of that payment — set aside specifically for this property, separate from her personal savings.

When her tenant moves out and the unit sits vacant for two months while she finds a new tenant, Wanda draws £3,200 from reserves to cover the mortgage during that gap, without missing a payment or touching her personal emergency fund. Because she planned for this "piece that might not move," the vacancy was an inconvenience, not a crisis.

**4. Practice Quiz**
1. What is vacancy rate?
   a) The interest rate on a vacant property
   b) The percentage of time a property sits unrented
   c) A type of insurance
   d) A tax deduction
2. A commonly cited (though not universal) reserve target is:
   a) One week of expenses
   b) Three to six months of expenses
   c) Ten years of expenses
   d) No reserves are ever needed
3. True or False: Higher leverage means a vacancy month costs less in absolute pounds.
4. In Wanda's example, what allowed her to handle the vacancy without financial stress?
   a) She skipped her mortgage payment
   b) She had pre-funded reserves specific to the property
   c) The tenant paid rent anyway
   d) She sold the property immediately

**5. Answer Key**
1. b  2. b  3. False  4. b

**6. Answers and Explanations**
1. Vacancy rate measures how often a property (or unit) is unrented over time.
2. Three to six months of expenses is a commonly referenced reserve target, though it varies by situation.
3. Higher leverage generally means a larger fixed payment, so a vacancy costs more, not less, in absolute pounds.
4. Wanda's pre-funded, property-specific reserves absorbed the gap without disrupting her mortgage or personal finances.

**7. Key Takeaways**
- Vacancy is a normal, expected part of owning rental property, not a sign of failure.
- Reserves are typically sized as a number of months of expenses, commonly three to six, depending on circumstances.
- Higher leverage increases the pound cost of a vacancy month, making reserves even more important.
- Planning for vacancy in advance turns a predictable event into a manageable inconvenience rather than a crisis.
