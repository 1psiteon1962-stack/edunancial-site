## RED-L2-014
### Property Taxes and Assessment: The Hidden Recurring Cost

**1. Learning Objectives**
By the end of this lesson, the learner will be able to:
- Explain how property taxes are generally calculated at a conceptual level.
- Describe how assessed value can change independently of a purchase price.
- Explain why rising property taxes affect cash flow over time.

**2. Core Content**
Property taxes are a recurring cost based on a property's assessed value, multiplied by a local tax rate — both of which vary significantly by jurisdiction, making this a strongly REGION-SPECIFIC topic. Assessed value is not always the same as market value or purchase price; local assessors periodically reassess properties, and values can rise (or occasionally fall) independent of what an owner actually paid.

This matters for Level 2 thinking because property taxes are often treated as a fixed number at purchase, when in reality they can rise over time — sometimes significantly after a sale, if a jurisdiction reassesses based on the new purchase price. A property that cash-flows well in year one could see thinner margins in year five if taxes rise faster than rent.

Some jurisdictions offer exemptions, caps, or appeal processes for property tax assessments; investors should learn their local rules and, when appropriate, work with a professional to ensure they aren't overpaying due to an inaccurate assessment.

**3. Worked Example**
Oscar buys a rental property assessed at a moderate value, with property taxes factored into his initial cash-flow projections. Two years later, the local assessor reassesses the property near its actual (higher) purchase price, and Oscar's property tax bill rises substantially.

Because Oscar had modeled only a small annual increase in his original projections, his actual cash flow is noticeably thinner than expected. He researches his jurisdiction's appeal process and, with supporting documentation, successfully argues for a modest reduction — but the experience teaches him to build more conservative tax growth assumptions into future underwriting.

**4. Practice Quiz**
1. What determines a property tax bill, generally speaking?
   a) The tenant's income
   b) Assessed value multiplied by a local tax rate
   c) The owner's credit score
   d) The insurance premium
2. Can assessed value differ from purchase price?
   a) No, they are always identical
   b) Yes, assessed value is set independently and can change over time
   c) Only in commercial properties
   d) Only if the owner requests it
3. True or False: Property taxes are always fixed for the life of ownership.
4. What did Oscar's experience teach him?
   a) Property taxes never change
   b) To build more conservative tax growth assumptions into future projections
   c) To avoid owning property with an assessor
   d) That appeals are never successful

**5. Answer Key**
1. b  2. b  3. False  4. b

**6. Answers and Explanations**
1. Property tax bills are generally the product of assessed value and a local tax rate.
2. Assessed value can be reset by local assessors and may differ meaningfully from purchase price or market value.
3. Property taxes commonly rise over time, sometimes significantly after a sale triggers reassessment.
4. Oscar learned to underwrite more conservatively for future tax increases, based on his real experience.

**7. Key Takeaways**
- Property taxes are based on assessed value and a local tax rate, both of which vary by jurisdiction.
- Assessed value can change independently of purchase price, sometimes rising sharply after a sale.
- Rising property taxes can quietly erode cash flow over time if not planned for.
- Investors should understand local exemptions, caps, and appeal processes rather than assume a static tax bill.
