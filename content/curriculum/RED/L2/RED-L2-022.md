## RED-L2-022
### Short-Term Rentals: New Revenue, New Regulations

**1. Learning Objectives**
By the end of this lesson, the learner will be able to:
- Explain the general short-term rental (STR) model.
- Identify how STRs differ from traditional long-term rentals in revenue and management.
- Recognize the significance of local regulation on STR viability.

**2. Core Content**
Short-term rentals (STRs) — properties rented out for days or weeks at a time, often through online platforms — can generate higher gross revenue per night than a traditional long-term lease, but they come with meaningfully different management demands: more frequent turnover, cleaning, guest communication, and often more active day-to-day involvement or a specialized management service.

The single biggest variable Level 2 investors must research before pursuing STRs is local regulation. Many cities and municipalities have passed rules restricting or licensing short-term rentals — sometimes limiting them to owner-occupied properties, capping the number of rental nights per year, or banning them in certain zones entirely. These rules change frequently and vary dramatically by location, making this one of the most REGION-SPECIFIC topics in this curriculum. A property that is profitable as an STR today could become non-compliant, or far less profitable, after a regulatory change.

STR income can also be more volatile than long-term rental income, fluctuating with seasonality, local events, and platform algorithm changes — factors a long-term lease largely insulates an owner from.

**3. Worked Example**
Before purchasing a property specifically for short-term rental use, Julia researches her target city's current STR ordinance, finding it currently allows non-owner-occupied STRs with a permit and a cap on the number of rental nights per year. She builds her financial projections around that cap, rather than assuming unlimited rental nights, and sets aside part of her research budget to monitor for regulatory changes, since a city council vote could change the rules with limited notice.

By underwriting to the current legal cap rather than a best-case scenario, Julia's projections remain more realistic — and she has a contingency plan (converting to a long-term rental) if regulations tighten further.

   a) Less frequent turnover
   b) More frequent turnover, cleaning, and guest communication
   c) No management required
   d) Identical to a 30-year lease
2. What is described as the single biggest variable to research before pursuing STRs?
   a) Paint color trends
   b) Local regulation
   c) The tenant's job history
   d) National interest rates only
3. True or False: Short-term rental regulations are uniform and unchanging across all cities.
4. Why did Julia underwrite to the current legal cap rather than a best-case scenario?
   a) She wanted to overestimate her income
   b) Regulations can change, and building in the current legal limit produced more realistic projections
   c) The cap was more profitable than unlimited rentals
   d) She was required to by her lender

**5. Answer Key**
1. b  2. b  3. False  4. b

**6. Answers and Explanations**
1. STRs typically require more frequent turnover and hands-on management than long-term rentals.
2. Local regulation is highlighted as the most significant and variable factor affecting STR viability.
3. STR regulations vary dramatically by city and change frequently, making this a strongly region-specific topic.
4. Julia's conservative underwriting to the current legal cap protected her projections from over-optimism about future rental night availability.

**7. Key Takeaways**
- Short-term rentals can generate higher gross revenue but require more active, frequent management.
- Local regulation is the single most important and most variable factor in STR viability.
- STR rules change frequently and differ dramatically by location — always verify current local law.
- Conservative underwriting to current legal limits, with a contingency plan, protects against regulatory shifts.
