## RED-L2-030
### Due Diligence: Inspections, Title, and Environmental Checks

**1. Learning Objectives**
By the end of this lesson, the learner will be able to:
- Define due diligence in a real estate transaction context.
- Identify the general purpose of inspections, title searches, and environmental checks.
- Explain why skipping due diligence increases risk significantly.

**2. Core Content**
Due diligence is the investigative process a buyer undertakes before finalising a real estate purchase, intended to uncover physical, legal, and environmental issues before they become the buyer's problem. Three common components are physical inspections, title searches, and environmental assessments.

A physical inspection examines the property's structural, mechanical, and system conditions (roof, foundation, plumbing, electrical, and more), helping a buyer understand repair needs and negotiate accordingly, or walk away from a property with serious undisclosed problems.

A title search examines public records to confirm the seller actually has clear legal ownership to sell, and to uncover liens, unresolved claims, or other title defects that could complicate or threaten the buyer's future ownership. Title insurance (introduced in the next lesson) works alongside this process.

An environmental assessment examines whether a property has contamination or environmental hazards (varying significantly by property type and prior use, with commercial and industrial properties often requiring more extensive review than typical residential purchases).

Skipping or rushing due diligence to "win" a competitive deal can expose a buyer to expensive, sometimes catastrophic, surprises after closing — problems that were often discoverable, and often the seller's responsibility to disclose in some form, before the deal closed.

**3. Worked Example**
In a competitive market, Nadia is tempted to waive her inspection contingency to make her offer more attractive to a seller facing multiple bids. Instead, she negotiates a shortened but still real inspection period, allowing her professional inspector enough time to identify a significant foundation issue that was not visible during a casual walkthrough.

Armed with this information, Nadia either renegotiates the price to account for the needed repair or walks away — either way, she avoids inheriting a costly, previously hidden problem, illustrating why due diligence protection is rarely worth fully waiving, even under competitive pressure.

**4. Practice Quiz**
1. What is the general purpose of a title search?
   a) To inspect the roof
   b) To confirm clear legal ownership and uncover liens or claims
   c) To test for environmental contamination
   d) To set the interest rate
2. What does a physical inspection primarily assess?
   a) The seller's income
   b) The property's structural, mechanical, and system conditions
   c) The neighbourhood's zoning history
   d) The buyer's credit score
3. True or False: Waiving all due diligence contingencies carries no additional risk to the buyer.
4. What did Nadia's shortened but real inspection period allow her to discover?
   a) A pricing error
   b) A significant, previously hidden foundation issue
   c) A zoning violation
   d) A title defect

**5. Answer Key**
1. b  2. b  3. False  4. b

**6. Answers and Explanations**
1. A title search confirms clear ownership and identifies liens or claims against the property.
2. Physical inspections focus on the property's structural, mechanical, and system conditions.
3. Waiving due diligence contingencies significantly increases the buyer's exposure to undiscovered problems.
4. Nadia's inspection uncovered a significant foundation issue that would otherwise have become her problem after closing.

**7. Key Takeaways**
- Due diligence investigates physical, legal, and environmental issues before a purchase is finalised.
- Physical inspections, title searches, and environmental assessments each address different risk categories.
- Skipping or rushing due diligence to win a competitive deal can expose a buyer to serious, costly surprises.
- Real, even shortened, due diligence protection is rarely worth fully waiving.
