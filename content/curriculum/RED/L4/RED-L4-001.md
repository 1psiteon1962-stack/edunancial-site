# Cap Rate Analysis: Why It Reveals Risk, Not Just Return

## Learning Objectives
- Explain what a capitalization rate actually measures.
- Calculate cap rate from net operating income and purchase price.
- Explain why a lower cap rate usually means the market sees less risk, not a worse deal.
- Compare cap rates across two properties to judge relative risk.

## Core Content
A capitalization rate, or cap rate, is Net Operating Income (NOI) divided by the property's purchase price, expressed as a percentage. Most beginners learn cap rate as a return number. That is only half the story. VERIFIED FACT: cap rate is a market-derived number, which means it reflects what investors are willing to pay for a dollar of income in that location and property type.

Here is the "why" that L1 and L2 skip: a low cap rate does not mean a bad deal, and a high cap rate does not mean a good one. A 4% cap rate in a stable downtown area usually means investors see low risk — steady tenants, strong appreciation history, easy financing. An 9% cap rate in a declining industrial town usually means investors are demanding a bigger cushion because the risk of vacancy, tenant turnover, or falling values is higher.

This is why comparing cap rates only makes sense within the same market and property type. Comparing a 5% cap rate apartment building in a growing city to an 11% cap rate apartment building in a shrinking one is not comparing two investments on the same scale — it is comparing two different risk profiles wearing the same math.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: A duplex generates $24,000 in annual NOI and sells for $300,000. Cap rate = $24,000 / $300,000 = 8%. A similar duplex two towns over generates the same $24,000 NOI but sells for $480,000, giving a cap rate of 5%. The 5% property is not "worse" — the market is pricing in lower perceived risk (better school district, lower crime, stronger job growth), so buyers accept a lower income return in exchange for more safety and likely appreciation.

A) The property's monthly cash flow

A) The lower cap rate property is a worse investment

C) The lower cap rate property has higher expenses
D) The seller made a pricing mistake

B) NOI cannot be measured outside the U.S.
C) The comparison ignores differing risk profiles baked into each market's pricing
D) Cap rates only apply to commercial property

**Q4. A property's NOI is $40,000 and it sells for $500,000. What is the cap rate?**
A) 6%
B) 7%
C) 8%
D) 9%

**Q5. Which factor would most likely push a market's average cap rates higher?**
A) Declining vacancy and rising rents
B) Increased investor confidence
C) Rising perceived risk, such as economic decline in the area
D) A drop in mortgage interest rates

## Answer Key
Q1: B
Q2: B
Q3: C
Q4: C
Q5: C

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** Cap rate = NOI / price. Because it is market-derived, it reflects how much risk investors believe the income stream carries.

**Q2.** Lower cap rates generally reflect lower perceived risk and stronger investor confidence in the income stream's stability.

**Q3.** Cap rate reflects local risk and demand conditions, so comparing across dissimilar markets compares different risk levels, not just returns.

**Q4.** $40,000 / $500,000 = 0.08, or 8%.

**Q5.** Higher perceived risk requires a higher return to compensate investors, which shows up as a higher cap rate.

## Key Takeaways
- Cap rate is NOI divided by price — a snapshot of return relative to price, not a stand-alone quality score.
- Low cap rates typically signal lower perceived risk; high cap rates typically signal higher perceived risk.
- Cap rates are only meaningfully comparable within similar markets and property types.
- A cap rate is a starting point for analysis, not a final verdict on whether a deal is good.
