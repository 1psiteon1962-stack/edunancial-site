# Supply and Demand Analysis: Why Vacancy Rates Signal Future Rent Growth

## Learning Objectives
- Define vacancy rate and explain how it is calculated.
- Explain why vacancy rate leads rent growth rather than following it.
- Distinguish between frictional vacancy and structural oversupply.
- Use a vacancy trend to make a forward-looking judgment about rents.

## Core Content
Vacancy rate is the percentage of available rental units that are unoccupied at a given time. It looks like a simple number, but it is one of the most useful leading indicators in real estate because it tells you about the balance between supply and demand before that imbalance shows up in rent prices.

Here is the why: landlords do not raise rents the moment vacancy drops — they typically wait to see if the trend holds, because raising rents too fast can cause turnover. This means vacancy tightens first, and rent growth follows with a lag, often two to four quarters later. The reverse is also true: rising vacancy usually precedes rent softening, because landlords cut rent (or offer concessions) reactively once they notice units sitting empty longer than usual.

It is also important to separate frictional vacancy (the normal 3-5% vacancy that exists simply because units turn over between tenants) from structural oversupply (vacancy well above the market's historical average, signaling too many units chasing too few renters). REGION-SPECIFIC RULE: what counts as a "normal" vacancy range differs by market and must be checked locally, not assumed.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: A submarket's vacancy rate falls from 8% to 4% over three quarters as a large employer adds jobs nearby. Rents are still flat because landlords are cautious. An investor who tracks the vacancy trend and buys before the market catches on can lock in a lower purchase price ahead of the rent growth that follows roughly two to three quarters later, once landlords gain confidence the tighter market will hold.

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** Vacancy rate is the share of available rental units that are unoccupied at a given time.

**Q2.** Landlords typically confirm a trend is holding before raising rents, creating a lag between tightening vacancy and rising rents.

**Q3.** Frictional vacancy is the baseline turnover-related vacancy present in any healthy rental market.

**Q4.** Local context determines what vacancy level signals genuine tightening or oversupply, since normal ranges differ by market.

**Q5.** This lag is expected — landlords usually wait to confirm the trend before adjusting rents upward.

## Key Takeaways
- Vacancy rate is a leading indicator that signals shifts in supply and demand before rents fully react.
- Falling vacancy tends to precede rising rents; rising vacancy tends to precede softening rents.
- Always distinguish normal frictional vacancy from a structural oversupply signal.
- Comparing vacancy to a market's own historical average, not a national figure, produces a more accurate read.
