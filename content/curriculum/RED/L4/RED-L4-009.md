# Depreciation Recapture: Why the Tax Bill Comes Due Eventually

## Learning Objectives
- Explain how depreciation reduces taxable income during ownership.
- Explain what depreciation recapture is and why it exists.
- Explain why depreciation is a timing benefit, not a permanent tax elimination.
- Identify strategies that can defer or offset depreciation recapture.

## Core Content
Depreciation lets real estate investors deduct a portion of a property's building value (not land) from taxable income each year, on the theory that the building wears out over time. VERIFIED FACT: in the U.S., residential rental property is generally depreciated over 27.5 years. This deduction can significantly reduce or even eliminate taxable income from a rental property while it is owned.

Here is the why this is not a free benefit: when the property is eventually sold, the IRS "recaptures" that depreciation by taxing it, generally at a rate up to 25% (REGION-SPECIFIC RULE, confirm current rate with a CPA), separate from the regular capital gains tax on any appreciation. The government is essentially saying: we let you defer this tax while you owned the property, but now that you are cashing out, part of that bill comes due.

This is why depreciation should be thought of as a timing tool, not a permanent tax elimination. It shifts tax liability from the ownership years to the sale year. Investors who do not plan for this can be surprised by how much of their sale proceeds go to taxes, especially after owning a property for many years and claiming large cumulative depreciation.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: An investor claims $80,000 in cumulative depreciation over several years of ownership, reducing their taxable rental income each year. When they sell the property, that $80,000 is subject to depreciation recapture tax (ESTIMATE: potentially around $20,000 at a 25% recapture rate), on top of any capital gains tax owed on the property's appreciation. An investor who did not plan for this bill may be caught off guard by how much smaller their net sale proceeds are than expected.

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** Depreciation lets owners deduct the building's theoretical wear-and-tear from taxable rental income annually.

**Q2.** Recapture taxes back the cumulative depreciation claimed once the property is sold.

**Q3.** Depreciation reduces tax during ownership but creates a deferred liability that comes due upon sale.

**Q4.** Residential rental property is generally depreciated over 27.5 years, applying only to the building value, not land.

**Q5.** A properly structured 1031 exchange can defer depreciation recapture along with capital gains tax, keeping more capital invested.

## Key Takeaways
- Depreciation reduces taxable rental income during ownership by deducting building value over time.
- Depreciation recapture taxes back that benefit when the property is sold, separate from capital gains tax.
- Depreciation is a timing benefit, shifting tax liability rather than eliminating it.
- Strategies like a 1031 exchange can defer recapture, but current rules should always be confirmed with a CPA.
