# Cost Segregation Analysis: Why Accelerating Depreciation Changes Cash Flow

## Learning Objectives
- Explain what a cost segregation study does.
- Explain why accelerating depreciation improves near-term cash flow.
- Identify the tradeoff between accelerated depreciation now and recapture later.
- Explain who typically benefits most from a cost segregation study.

## Core Content
A cost segregation study is an engineering-based analysis that breaks a property's cost basis into components — such as flooring, cabinetry, parking lots, and certain electrical or plumbing components — that can be depreciated over much shorter periods (often 5, 7, or 15 years) instead of the standard 27.5 or 39 years for the whole building. VERIFIED FACT: this is a legitimate, IRS-recognized method, but it typically requires a specialized study, not a simple guess, to allocate costs correctly.

Here is the why this matters for cash flow: by moving a chunk of depreciation earlier in the ownership timeline, an investor can significantly reduce taxable income in the early years of ownership, which frees up more after-tax cash to reinvest, cover expenses, or acquire additional properties sooner. This is especially powerful combined with bonus depreciation rules that, depending on current tax law, may allow a large percentage of eligible short-life components to be deducted immediately in year one. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE: bonus depreciation percentages change based on current tax law and must be verified with a CPA for the applicable tax year.

The tradeoff, following directly from the depreciation recapture lesson, is that accelerating deductions now generally means a larger recapture bill later at sale. This is why cost segregation tends to benefit investors with a longer hold horizon, high current income needing an offset, or a plan to use a 1031 exchange at sale to defer that recapture rather than pay it immediately.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: Without cost segregation, a $1,000,000 apartment building depreciates evenly, producing roughly $30,000-$35,000 in annual depreciation deductions (ESTIMATE, building value only, excludes land). With a cost segregation study reclassifying $250,000 of components into 5- and 15-year categories, an investor might claim a much larger deduction in year one — potentially well over $100,000 depending on current bonus depreciation rules — significantly reducing that year's taxable income and increasing after-tax cash available to reinvest.

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** It reclassifies parts of the building into shorter-life categories, accelerating depreciation deductions.

**Q2.** Larger early deductions lower taxable income, which reduces the tax bill and frees up more cash in those years.

**Q3.** Front-loading deductions typically increases the amount subject to depreciation recapture when the property is eventually sold.

**Q4.** Cost segregation benefits investors who can use the near-term deduction and can manage or defer the later recapture.

**Q5.** Proper allocation requires detailed, defensible analysis, which is why engineering-based studies are the standard approach.

## Key Takeaways
- A cost segregation study reclassifies parts of a building into shorter depreciation schedules.
- Accelerated depreciation reduces near-term taxable income, improving after-tax cash flow.
- The tradeoff is a larger recapture liability at sale, following the same logic as standard depreciation recapture.
- This strategy fits best with longer hold horizons, high current income, or a plan to defer recapture through a 1031 exchange.
