# Loan-to-Value Analysis: Why Leverage Cuts Both Ways

## Learning Objectives
- Calculate loan-to-value ratio from loan amount and property value.
- Explain why leverage amplifies both returns and losses.
- Explain why a smaller equity cushion increases the risk of negative equity.
- Identify factors that should influence how much leverage to use.

## Core Content
Loan-to-value ratio (LTV) equals the loan amount divided by the property's value. An 80% LTV loan on a $500,000 property means a $400,000 loan and $100,000 in buyer equity. VERIFIED FACT: LTV is a core underwriting metric lenders use to manage their risk, and it also defines how much equity cushion the borrower has against a decline in value.

Here is the why leverage cuts both ways: leverage amplifies percentage returns on the equity invested, in both directions. If a property appreciates, the gain is measured against the smaller equity amount, producing a higher percentage return than if the investor had paid all cash. But if the property declines in value, the loss is measured against that same smaller equity base, producing a larger percentage loss — and if the decline is large enough, the equity can be wiped out entirely, leaving the owner "underwater" (owing more than the property is worth).

This is why higher leverage is not automatically a better strategy. It increases both the potential upside and the potential downside, and it also increases fixed debt service obligations that must be paid regardless of whether the property performs as expected.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: An investor buys a $400,000 property with 20% down ($80,000 equity, 80% LTV). If the property appreciates 10% to $440,000, the investor's equity grows from $80,000 to $120,000 — a 50% return on equity from a 10% price increase. But if the property instead falls 10% to $360,000, the investor's equity shrinks from $80,000 to $40,000 — a 50% loss on equity from the same 10% move, in the opposite direction. A larger price drop of 20% would wipe out the equity entirely.

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** LTV = loan amount / property value, indicating how much of the purchase is financed versus owned outright.

**Q2.** Because equity is smaller than the full property value, the same dollar change in value produces a larger percentage change in equity.

**Q3.** At 80% LTV, a 20% decline in value roughly equals the investor's original equity, which can eliminate it entirely.

**Q4.** Leverage is a double-edged tool: it magnifies both gains and losses and adds fixed payment obligations regardless of performance.

**Q5.** Being underwater means negative equity — the loan balance exceeds the property's current market value.

## Key Takeaways
- LTV measures how much of a property's value is financed versus owned as equity.
- Leverage amplifies both gains and losses on the equity invested, not just gains.
- A smaller equity cushion increases the risk of being wiped out or going underwater during a value decline.
- The right leverage level depends on risk tolerance, cash flow stability, and market conditions, not just maximizing potential upside.
