# Refinance vs Sell Analysis: Why the Tax-Free Cash-Out Wins Sometimes

## Learning Objectives
- Explain why loan proceeds from a refinance are not taxable income.
- Compare the after-tax outcomes of refinancing versus selling to access equity.
- Identify the risks a cash-out refinance introduces that a sale does not.
- Explain when selling may still be the better choice despite the tax advantage of refinancing.

## Core Content
When an investor sells a property, any gain above their cost basis is generally subject to capital gains tax (and potentially depreciation recapture, as covered earlier). VERIFIED FACT: when an investor instead refinances and pulls out equity as a cash-out loan, that money is loan proceeds, not income, and is not taxed — because it is borrowed money that must eventually be repaid, not a realized gain.

Here is the why this often favors refinancing over selling for an investor who wants access to their equity but also wants to keep the property: refinancing lets the investor access a large sum of cash tax-free while continuing to hold an appreciating, cash-flowing asset and continuing to benefit from ongoing depreciation deductions. Selling converts the same equity into cash, but a meaningful portion goes to taxes first, and the investor gives up future appreciation, cash flow, and depreciation on that property entirely.

This is not a free lunch, though — the tradeoff is that a cash-out refinance increases the loan balance and the monthly debt service, which raises fixed costs and lowers DSCR (from Lesson 11), and if the market turns down, the higher loan balance increases the risk of being underwater (from Lesson 12) compared to an unleveraged or lower-leveraged position.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: A property purchased for $300,000 is now worth $500,000, with $150,000 in built-up equity available to access at 70% LTV. Selling might net the investor roughly $150,000 minus capital gains and depreciation recapture taxes — ESTIMATE, potentially $30,000-$40,000 depending on individual tax situation — leaving perhaps $110,000-$120,000, and the investor no longer owns the property. Refinancing instead lets the investor pull out the full $150,000 tax-free as loan proceeds while keeping the property, its cash flow, and future appreciation potential — but now carrying a larger loan balance and higher monthly payment.

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** Loan proceeds are debt, not income or realized gain, so they are not taxed the way a sale's profit would be.

**Q2.** Refinancing lets an investor extract equity without triggering a taxable sale, while continuing to hold the asset's future benefits.

**Q3.** Refinancing increases leverage, which raises fixed costs and downside risk if property values decline.

**Q4.** Selling can make sense when an investor wants to de-risk, exit a market, or move capital into a different opportunity, despite the tax cost.

**Q5.** Refinance proceeds are a loan, so unlike sale proceeds, they carry a repayment obligation with interest.

## Key Takeaways
- Cash-out refinance proceeds are tax-free loan proceeds, unlike sale proceeds which can trigger capital gains and recapture tax.
- Refinancing can access equity while preserving ongoing cash flow, appreciation, and depreciation benefits.
- The tradeoff is higher leverage, higher debt service, and increased downside risk if values decline.
- The right choice depends on the investor's risk tolerance, market outlook, and goals for the capital being accessed.
