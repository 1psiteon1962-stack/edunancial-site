# Comparative Market Analysis: Why Comps Can Mislead Without Adjustments

## Learning Objectives
- Explain the purpose of a comparative market analysis.
- Identify common factors that require adjustment between comps and a subject property.
- Explain why unadjusted comps can produce a misleading valuation.
- Apply basic adjustment logic to compare two non-identical properties.

## Core Content
A comparative market analysis (CMA) estimates a property's value by looking at recent sales of similar properties ("comps") in the same area. VERIFIED FACT: no two properties are truly identical, so a proper CMA requires adjusting each comp's sale price up or down based on differences from the subject property — square footage, condition, age, lot size, updates, and location within the neighborhood.

Here is the why unadjusted comps mislead: if an investor simply averages three nearby sale prices without adjusting for the fact that one comp was fully renovated, one was a distressed sale, and one had an extra bedroom, the resulting "average" reflects none of those properties accurately and does not represent the subject property's true value at all. This is a common mistake among less experienced buyers, who see a sale price nearby and assume it directly applies to their target property.

This is why professional appraisers and experienced investors make specific line-item adjustments for each material difference (for example, adding a per-square-foot adjustment for size differences, subtracting for needed repairs, adjusting for a distressed or non-arm's-length sale) before arriving at an adjusted value range, rather than treating raw comp prices as directly comparable.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: A subject property is a 1,600 sq ft, updated 3-bedroom home. Comp A sold for $310,000 but is 1,800 sq ft (larger, requiring a downward adjustment for the subject). Comp B sold for $265,000 but needs a new roof and kitchen (requiring an upward adjustment for the subject, which does not need those repairs). Simply averaging $310,000 and $265,000 to get $287,500 ignores both adjustments and would misstate the subject property's likely value; proper adjustments might narrow the estimate to something closer to $295,000 after accounting for size and condition differences.

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** A CMA estimates value by comparing the subject property to similar recently sold properties, with adjustments.

**Q2.** Ignoring differences in size, condition, and sale circumstances produces a distorted, inaccurate value estimate.

**Q3.** If a comp is larger than the subject, its price should be adjusted downward to reflect what a property the subject's actual size would likely sell for.

**Q4.** Distressed or non-market sales often reflect unusual circumstances rather than typical market value, requiring adjustment or exclusion.

**Q5.** An inaccurate valuation from unadjusted comps can lead to overpaying or missing a fair deal due to a skewed price benchmark.

## Key Takeaways
- A comparative market analysis estimates value using adjusted recent sales of similar properties.
- No two properties are identical, so raw comp prices without adjustment can seriously mislead a valuation.
- Key adjustment factors include size, condition, age, lot size, updates, and sale circumstances.
- Careful, line-item adjustments produce a far more accurate and defensible value estimate than simple averaging.
