# Highest and Best Use Analysis: Why Zoning Changes Everything

## Learning Objectives
- Define highest and best use and its four standard tests.
- Explain why a property's current use may not be its highest and best use.
- Explain how a zoning change can significantly increase or decrease value.
- Apply highest and best use thinking to identify hidden value in a property.

## Core Content
Highest and best use is the legally permitted, physically possible, financially feasible, and maximally productive use of a property. VERIFIED FACT: appraisers are trained to evaluate all four tests together — a use is not the "highest and best use" unless it clears all four bars, not just one.

Here is the why this concept matters so much: a property's current use is not always its highest and best use. A single-family home on a lot zoned for multi-family development may be far more valuable as a future duplex or small apartment building site than as a single-family residence, even though it is currently being used and priced as the latter. This gap between current use value and highest-and-best-use value is a common source of hidden opportunity — and hidden risk, if a buyer overlooks a zoning restriction that limits potential.

This is why zoning changes can dramatically shift value overnight. If a city rezones a corridor from single-family to allow multi-family or mixed-use development, properties along that corridor can jump significantly in value, because their highest and best use just changed to something far more productive than before — even though nothing physical about the property changed at all.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: A single-family home on a large lot is currently valued at $350,000 based on comparable single-family sales. The city rezones the area to allow up to four units per lot. A developer determines a four-unit building on that lot could generate enough NOI to justify a $700,000 land value once redeveloped. The highest and best use has shifted from single-family residential to multi-family development, and the property's true market value should reflect that new, more productive potential use — not just its value as a single-family home.

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** All four tests must be satisfied together for a use to qualify as highest and best use.

**Q2.** A property may be legally and financially capable of supporting a more valuable use than its current one.

**Q3.** Since zoning determines what uses are legally permitted, a rezoning can instantly change which use qualifies as highest and best, shifting value.

**Q4.** Allowing greater density typically increases development potential and land value, assuming the new use is financially feasible and marketable.

**Q5.** Spotting a gap between current-use value and a more productive, feasible legal use can reveal undervalued opportunity.

## Key Takeaways
- Highest and best use must be legally permitted, physically possible, financially feasible, and maximally productive.
- A property's current use is not always its highest and best use — this gap can represent hidden value.
- Zoning changes can shift a property's highest and best use, and therefore its value, without any physical change.
- Recognizing this gap is a key analytical skill for spotting undervalued properties or emerging risk from zoning shifts.
