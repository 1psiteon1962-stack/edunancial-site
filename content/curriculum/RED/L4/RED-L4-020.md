# Risk-Adjusted Return Analysis: Why Two Deals with the Same Return Aren't Equal

## Learning Objectives
- Explain why raw return figures alone are insufficient for comparing investments.
- Identify categories of risk that should factor into a risk-adjusted comparison.
- Explain why a lower return with lower risk can be the better choice.
- Apply risk-adjusted thinking to compare two hypothetical real estate deals.

## Core Content
Two real estate deals can show the identical projected return — say, 10% cash-on-cash — and still not be equally attractive, because return numbers alone say nothing about the risk taken to achieve them. Risk-adjusted return analysis asks: how much risk was taken on to generate this return, and would a rational investor accept that level of risk for that level of reward?

Here is the why this distinction matters: risk in real estate comes from many sources covered throughout this level — market cycle position (Lesson 5), leverage level (Lesson 12), tenant and lease quality (covered in upcoming lessons), neighborhood trajectory (Lesson 19), and property condition, among others. A 10% return achieved through heavy leverage on a declining-neighborhood property with month-to-month tenants carries dramatically more risk than the same 10% return achieved with modest leverage on a stabilizing-neighborhood property with long-term, creditworthy tenants.

This is why sophisticated investors do not simply rank deals by projected return. They ask what has to go right for that return to materialize, and what happens to the return if key assumptions — rent growth, occupancy, exit cap rate — turn out to be wrong. A return built on optimistic assumptions is not equivalent to the same number built on conservative, well-supported assumptions.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: Deal A projects a 10% return assuming rents grow 5% annually and the property sells at a 5% cap rate in five years — both fairly aggressive assumptions (ASSUMPTION labels apply here). Deal B projects the same 10% return using flat rent growth and a conservative 7% exit cap rate. Deal B's return is far more resilient to being wrong; even if actual performance falls short of projections, it has more room to still deliver an acceptable outcome, while Deal A's return could turn negative if its optimistic assumptions do not hold.

A) Identical returns always mean identical risk
B) The level of risk taken to achieve that return may be very different between the two deals

D) Risk-adjusted analysis only applies to stock investments

**Q2. Which of the following is a source of risk relevant to a risk-adjusted comparison?**
A) The property's street address alphabetically
B) Leverage level, tenant quality, neighborhood trajectory, and market cycle position

D) Higher risk always guarantees a higher actual outcome

**Q4. Why should an investor examine the assumptions behind a projected return, not just the final number?**
A) Assumptions are always guaranteed to be accurate

C) Assumptions are irrelevant to real estate underwriting
D) Examining assumptions is only necessary for commercial deals

**Q5. What question should an investor ask instead of simply ranking deals by projected return?**
A) Which deal has the shortest marketing brochure?
B) What has to go right for this return to materialize, and what happens if key assumptions are wrong?
C) Which deal is located closest to the investor's home?

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** Return alone does not capture how much risk, or how fragile the underlying assumptions are, behind that number.

**Q2.** Real, material risk factors include leverage, tenant and lease quality, location trajectory, and cycle timing, among others.

**Q3.** Risk-adjusted thinking weighs reward against the probability and consequences of underperformance, not just the headline number.

**Q4.** The resilience of a projected return depends heavily on how realistic and conservative its underlying assumptions are.

**Q5.** Risk-adjusted analysis requires stress-testing assumptions, not just comparing headline projected returns.

## Key Takeaways
- Raw return figures alone do not reveal how much risk was taken to produce them.
- Leverage, tenant quality, neighborhood trajectory, and cycle timing are all real sources of risk to weigh.
- A lower return built on conservative assumptions can be a better choice than a higher return built on optimistic ones.
- Always examine what must go right for a projected return to materialize before comparing deals by return alone.
