# Property Management Analysis: Why Self-Managing Isn't Always Cheaper

## Learning Objectives
- Identify the direct and indirect costs of self-managing a property.
- Explain why the management fee saved is not the full financial picture.
- Compare self-management and professional management using an opportunity cost framework.
- Identify situations where self-management is more likely to make sense.

## Core Content
A typical third-party property management fee runs roughly 8-10% of collected rent (ESTIMATE, varies significantly by market and service level), and it is tempting to view self-management as pure savings equal to that fee. This misses the full picture.

Here is the why self-managing isn't automatically cheaper: self-management has real costs that simply are not itemized on an invoice — the investor's own time spent marketing vacancies, screening tenants, coordinating repairs, handling late-night emergency calls, and staying current on landlord-tenant law. From Lesson 15's opportunity cost framework, that time has alternative uses, whether that is finding the next deal, working a primary career, or simply having more free time. There are also real financial risks: an inexperienced self-manager may screen tenants less rigorously, respond to maintenance issues more slowly, or make legal missteps around lease terms or eviction procedures that a professional manager, versed in current landlord-tenant law, would be less likely to make.

This is why the right comparison is not "management fee saved" versus "zero," but rather the management fee saved versus the value of the investor's time, the risk of costly management mistakes, and the investor's own skill and availability. Self-management tends to make more sense for investors with fewer units, more available time, strong local knowledge, and genuine interest in the operational side of the business.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: An investor self-manages a four-unit property to save an estimated $4,000 a year in management fees (10% of $40,000 collected rent). Over the year, the investor spends roughly 150 hours on tenant issues, repairs coordination, and vacancy turnover. If that time could otherwise generate $50 an hour in the investor's primary career or in finding new deals, the true opportunity cost of self-managing is roughly $7,500 — meaning the "savings" from avoiding a $4,000 management fee actually cost the investor more once their own time is properly valued.

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** Typical management fees run roughly 8-10% of collected rent, though this varies by market and service level.

**Q2.** The true cost of self-managing includes the opportunity cost of the investor's time and the risk of inexperienced mistakes.

**Q3.** When the investor's time is valued at their alternative earning rate, the true cost of self-managing exceeded the avoided fee.

**Q4.** Self-management tends to be a better fit when time availability, skill, and interest align with the operational demands involved.

**Q5.** Professional managers are typically more versed in current landlord-tenant law, reducing the risk of costly legal mistakes.

## Key Takeaways
- Self-management's true cost includes the investor's time and risk of mistakes, not just the avoided management fee.
- Opportunity cost analysis should be applied to the decision, valuing the investor's time at its alternative use.
- Inexperienced self-management carries real legal and operational risk that professional management can reduce.
- Self-management tends to make more sense with fewer units, more available time, and genuine operational interest.
