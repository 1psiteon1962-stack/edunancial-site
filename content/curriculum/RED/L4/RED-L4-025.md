# Lease Structure Analysis: Why Triple Net Shifts Risk to Tenants

## Learning Objectives
- Explain the difference between a gross lease and a triple net (NNN) lease.
- Identify which expenses are typically shifted to the tenant under a triple net lease.
- Explain why NNN leases tend to produce more predictable landlord cash flow.
- Identify the tradeoffs a landlord accepts in exchange for that predictability.

## Core Content
In a gross lease, the landlord pays most or all operating expenses (property taxes, insurance, maintenance) out of the rent collected. In a triple net (NNN) lease, common in commercial real estate, the tenant pays rent plus their pro-rata share of property taxes, insurance, and maintenance directly, in addition to base rent. VERIFIED FACT: the "triple" in triple net refers to these three expense categories being passed through to the tenant.

Here is the why this shift matters for an investor's risk profile: under a gross lease, if property taxes rise sharply or a major repair is needed, the landlord's NOI takes the hit directly, because those costs come out of collected rent before the landlord sees any income. Under a true triple net lease, those same cost increases are passed through to the tenant, which means the landlord's NOI is far more insulated from expense volatility and inflation, producing more predictable, bond-like cash flow.

This predictability is not free. In exchange for this reduced expense risk, NNN properties, especially those leased to strong, creditworthy tenants (such as national retail chains) on long-term leases, typically trade at lower cap rates than gross-leased properties, because the market prices in the lower risk. This is why NNN properties tend to appeal to more conservative, income-focused investors, while gross-lease properties, with higher expense risk but potentially higher cap rates, may appeal to investors seeking higher returns and willing to actively manage expense risk.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: A landlord leases a retail building to a national chain under a 15-year triple net lease. When property taxes rise by $8,000 in a given year due to a reassessment, the tenant's lease requires them to cover that increase directly, so the landlord's NOI is unaffected. A comparable landlord with a gross-leased property absorbs that same $8,000 tax increase directly out of their own NOI, since their lease does not pass expenses through to the tenant.

D) A shorter lease term than a gross lease

**Q5. Which type of investor might prefer a gross-lease property over a triple net property?**
A) An investor who wants zero involvement in expense management and the lowest possible risk
B) An investor seeking potentially higher returns and willing to actively manage expense risk
C) An investor who is legally prohibited from owning triple net properties
D) There is no type of investor who would prefer a gross lease

## Answer Key
Q1: B
Q2: B
Q3: B
Q4: B
Q5: B

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** Triple net leases shift the three named expense categories to the tenant, unlike a gross lease where the landlord covers them.

**Q2.** Because operating expense increases are passed to the tenant, the landlord's NOI is insulated from that volatility.

**Q3.** Reduced risk and payment reliability from strong tenants and passed-through expenses justify a lower required return, reflected as a lower cap rate.

**Q4.** Predictability and reduced risk generally come at the cost of a lower going-in yield compared to riskier gross-lease alternatives.

**Q5.** Investors comfortable actively managing expense risk in exchange for potentially higher returns may prefer gross-lease properties.

## Key Takeaways
- A triple net lease shifts property tax, insurance, and maintenance costs to the tenant, unlike a gross lease.
- This shift insulates landlord NOI from expense volatility, producing more predictable cash flow.
- NNN properties with strong tenants typically trade at lower cap rates, reflecting their reduced risk.
- The choice between lease structures reflects a tradeoff between predictability and potential return, matched to investor risk tolerance.
