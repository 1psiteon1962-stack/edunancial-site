# REIT vs Direct Ownership Analysis: Why Liquidity Has a Cost

## Learning Objectives
- Explain the basic structure of a REIT.
- Compare the liquidity of REIT shares to direct property ownership.
- Explain why publicly traded REITs can be more correlated with the broader stock market than direct real estate.
- Identify which investor profile is better suited to each approach.

## Core Content
A Real Estate Investment Trust (REIT) is a company that owns, operates, or finances income-producing real estate, with shares that can be bought and sold much like a stock, particularly for publicly traded REITs. VERIFIED FACT: REITs are required by law to distribute at least 90% of their taxable income to shareholders as dividends, which is part of why they are popular for income-focused investors seeking real estate exposure without direct ownership.

Here is the why the liquidity tradeoff matters: direct property ownership is illiquid — selling a property takes weeks to months and involves significant transaction costs (Lesson 28). Publicly traded REIT shares can be bought or sold within seconds during market hours, offering vastly greater liquidity and no control over individual property decisions. This liquidity is valuable, but it comes at a cost: REIT share prices, especially for publicly traded REITs, tend to be more correlated with broader stock market movements than direct real estate values are, meaning REIT investors can experience more short-term price volatility, sometimes disconnected from the underlying real estate's actual operating performance.

This is why direct ownership tends to appeal to investors seeking maximum control, the ability to use leverage on individual assets, and the value-add and tax benefits (depreciation, 1031 exchanges) covered earlier in this level, while REITs tend to appeal to investors prioritizing liquidity, diversification across many properties with a single purchase, and a more passive, hands-off approach to real estate exposure.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: An investor who owns a rental property directly cannot access their equity quickly if they need cash unexpectedly — a sale or refinance can take weeks or months. An investor holding shares in a publicly traded REIT covering hundreds of similar properties can sell those shares within seconds if cash is needed, but during a broad stock market downturn, that REIT's share price may fall sharply even if the underlying properties' rents and occupancy have not meaningfully changed, reflecting the REIT's correlation with overall market sentiment rather than just its real estate fundamentals.

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** This distribution requirement is a defining legal feature of REITs and drives their popularity among income-focused investors.

**Q2.** REIT shares trade like stocks, offering vastly faster access to cash compared to the lengthy process of selling physical property.

**Q3.** Being publicly traded ties REIT prices to overall market sentiment, which can move independently of the properties' actual rents and occupancy.

**Q4.** Direct ownership offers control and specific tax tools that REIT investing does not provide to individual shareholders.

**Q5.** REIT shares offer built-in diversification across many properties with a relatively small capital commitment, unlike concentrated direct property ownership.

## Key Takeaways
- REITs offer real estate exposure with stock-like liquidity, while direct ownership is illiquid but offers more control.
- Publicly traded REIT prices tend to correlate with broader stock market sentiment, adding a layer of volatility beyond property fundamentals.
- Direct ownership offers specific tax tools like depreciation and 1031 exchanges that REIT shareholders do not access directly.
- The right choice depends on whether an investor prioritizes liquidity and diversification or control and property-level tax strategy.
