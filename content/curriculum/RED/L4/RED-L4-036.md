# House Hacking Analysis: Why Living in Your Investment Changes the Numbers

## Learning Objectives
- Explain what house hacking means as a real estate strategy.
- Explain why owner-occupant financing terms differ favorably from investment property financing.
- Explain how house hacking effectively subsidizes an investor's own housing cost.
- Identify the tradeoffs of house hacking compared to a traditional rental investment.

## Core Content
House hacking means an investor purchases a property, typically a small multi-family building (commonly two to four units) or a single-family home with rentable space, lives in one unit or portion, and rents out the rest. VERIFIED FACT: because the owner occupies part of the property, they typically qualify for owner-occupant financing (such as certain FHA or conventional loan programs), which generally requires a lower down payment and offers more favorable terms than investment property financing, which usually requires a larger down payment and carries a rate premium.

Here is the why this changes the numbers: an investor using owner-occupant financing to house hack effectively accesses more favorable leverage terms than they could get purchasing the same property purely as a rental, while simultaneously using the rental income from the other units to offset or fully cover their own housing costs. This combination — better financing terms plus reduced personal living expense — can produce a return on the investor's own capital that is difficult to replicate with a traditional, non-owner-occupied rental purchase.

The tradeoff is a lifestyle one, not just financial: house hacking requires living in close proximity to tenants, which suits some investors and not others, and it is generally intended as a stepping-stone strategy — commonly, an investor house hacks for a year or more (often tied to owner-occupancy requirements in the loan terms) before potentially moving out and converting the property to a full rental, then repeating the strategy with a new property.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: An investor buys a fourplex using owner-occupant financing with 5% down, versus the 20-25% down typically required for the same property purchased purely as an investment. The investor lives in one unit and rents out the other three for a combined $2,700 a month, which fully covers the mortgage, taxes, insurance, and expenses on all four units. The investor effectively lives for free while building equity, a combination of favorable financing and expense offset not available to an investor purchasing the same fourplex purely as a rental property.

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** House hacking specifically involves owner-occupancy of part of the property while renting out the rest.

**Q2.** Owner-occupancy is associated with lower default risk in lenders' underwriting models, resulting in more favorable loan terms.

**Q3.** Income from the rented units is applied against the shared property expenses, reducing or eliminating the owner's net housing cost.

**Q4.** Living close to tenants is a genuine lifestyle consideration that differs meaningfully from owning a purely separate rental property.

**Q5.** The strategy is often used repeatedly, with investors moving on to a new property while converting the prior one to a standard rental.

## Key Takeaways
- House hacking involves owner-occupying part of a property while renting out the rest.
- Owner-occupant financing typically offers lower down payments and better terms than investment property financing.
- Rental income from the other units can substantially offset or eliminate the investor's own housing cost.
- The main tradeoff is lifestyle-related — living near tenants — and it is often used as a repeatable stepping-stone strategy.
