# Wholesaling Analysis: Why Speed Matters More Than Margin

## Learning Objectives
- Explain the basic mechanics of a wholesale real estate transaction.
- Explain why wholesaling relies on contract assignment rather than the wholesaler taking ownership.
- Explain why speed and deal volume are central to wholesaling's business model.
- Identify the primary risk a wholesaler faces if they cannot find an end buyer.

## Core Content
Wholesaling involves an investor (the wholesaler) putting a distressed or below-market property under contract with a seller, then assigning that purchase contract to a different end buyer — typically another investor planning a fix-and-flip or a BRRRR strategy (Lesson 37) — for an assignment fee, without the wholesaler ever taking ownership of the property themselves. VERIFIED FACT: this differs fundamentally from flipping, where the investor does take ownership, renovates, and resells.

Here is the why speed matters more than margin in this model: because the wholesaler is not investing significant capital in the property itself (typically only a small earnest money deposit) and is not holding the property through a renovation period, their profit per deal (the assignment fee) is usually much smaller than a flipper's profit margin. This means a wholesaler's business model depends on completing a high volume of deals relatively quickly, since a smaller margin per deal must be multiplied across more transactions to produce meaningful income, rather than a flipper's model of fewer, larger-margin deals held over a longer period.

This is why the primary risk in wholesaling is failing to find an end buyer before the original purchase contract's closing deadline. If the wholesaler cannot assign the contract in time, they may be forced to either close on the purchase themselves (requiring capital and expertise they may not have planned for) or default on the contract, potentially losing their earnest money deposit and damaging their reputation with sellers and title companies in that market.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: A wholesaler puts a distressed property under contract for $120,000 with a 30-day closing deadline, using a $2,000 earnest money deposit. Within two weeks, they assign the contract to a fix-and-flip investor for a $10,000 assignment fee, and the end buyer closes directly with the original seller. The wholesaler never took ownership of the property and invested only two weeks of sourcing effort and a refundable-if-structured-properly $2,000 deposit to earn the $10,000 fee — but if no end buyer had been found within the 30-day window, the wholesaler could have faced losing that deposit or scrambling to close the purchase themselves.

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** Wholesaling is built around contract assignment, distinguishing it fundamentally from flipping or long-term ownership strategies.

**Q2.** Lower capital commitment and shorter involvement generally correspond to a smaller profit per transaction compared to a full renovation and resale.

**Q3.** Smaller per-deal margins must be offset by higher transaction volume and faster turnaround to produce comparable overall income.

**Q4.** Failing to assign the contract in time can force an unplanned purchase closing or a default with financial and reputational consequences.

**Q5.** The wholesaler's profit came entirely from the assignment fee paid by the end buyer for the rights to the original purchase contract.

## Key Takeaways
- Wholesaling assigns a purchase contract to an end buyer for a fee, without the wholesaler taking ownership.
- Smaller per-deal margins mean the wholesaling business model depends on speed and transaction volume.
- The core risk is failing to find an end buyer before the contract's closing deadline.
- Wholesaling and flipping represent different points on the capital-commitment and margin-per-deal spectrum.
