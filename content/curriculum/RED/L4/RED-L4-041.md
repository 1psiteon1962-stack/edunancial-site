# Commercial vs Residential Analysis: Why Lease Terms Change Risk Profile

## Learning Objectives
- Compare typical lease term lengths between residential and commercial property.
- Explain why longer commercial leases can provide more income stability.
- Explain why longer leases also introduce their own risk if a tenant defaults or the market shifts.
- Apply lease-term risk thinking to compare a residential and commercial opportunity.

## Core Content
Residential leases are typically one year, with regular turnover and the ability to reset rent to market relatively frequently. Commercial leases, particularly for retail, office, and industrial space, often run five, ten, or even twenty years, sometimes with built-in rent escalations over the term. VERIFIED FACT: this fundamental difference in typical lease length shapes much of the risk and analysis difference between the two property types.

Here is the why longer commercial leases affect risk in both directions: on one hand, a long-term lease with a creditworthy tenant provides highly predictable, bond-like income for years at a time, reducing the vacancy and turnover risk that residential landlords face annually. On the other hand, that same long lease term is a double-edged sword — if the tenant defaults or the space becomes vacant, the landlord faces a potentially long and costly search for a replacement commercial tenant (commercial vacancies often take much longer to fill than residential ones), and if market rents rise substantially during a long lease term, the landlord is locked into the old, now below-market rent until the lease expires or an escalation clause catches up.

This is why commercial real estate risk is often described as more binary than residential — long stretches of highly stable, predictable income punctuated by higher-stakes, longer-duration vacancy or tenant-default events — while residential real estate risk is more frequent but individually smaller, given the shorter lease cycles and larger number of independent tenants typically spread across a residential portfolio.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: A commercial landlord signs a 10-year lease with a national retail tenant at a fixed rent with modest annual escalations. For years, this provides highly predictable income with no leasing activity required. In year six, the tenant unexpectedly closes that location and defaults on the remaining lease term. The landlord now faces a costly, potentially lengthy search for a new commercial tenant, possibly requiring significant tenant improvement allowances to attract one, a very different risk experience than a residential landlord facing a single vacant unit with a much larger, faster-moving pool of prospective tenants.

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** This difference in typical lease duration is a foundational distinction between the two property types.

**Q2.** Locking in a long-term tenant provides extended, predictable income compared to the more frequent turnover cycle typical of residential leases.

**Q3.** The same long-term structure that provides stability also means a default can trigger an extended, costly vacancy period.

**Q4.** A long fixed-term lease can prevent the landlord from capturing rising market rents until the lease term ends or a scheduled escalation applies.

**Q5.** The contrast between long stable periods and high-impact vacancy or default events characterizes commercial risk differently than residential's more frequent, incremental turnover risk.

## Key Takeaways
- Commercial leases are typically much longer than residential leases, often five to twenty years versus about one year.
- Long-term commercial leases provide highly predictable income but create longer, costlier risk if a tenant defaults.
- Long fixed-term leases can also leave a landlord locked into below-market rent during periods of rising rents.
- Commercial risk tends to be more binary (stable then high-stakes), while residential risk is more frequent but individually smaller.
