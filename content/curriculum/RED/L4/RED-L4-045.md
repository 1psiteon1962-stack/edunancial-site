# Environmental Risk Analysis: Why Due Diligence Includes the Ground, Not Just the Building

## Learning Objectives
- Explain what an environmental Phase I assessment evaluates.
- Explain why environmental liability can attach to a property owner even if they did not cause the contamination.
- Identify property types and histories that carry elevated environmental risk.
- Explain why environmental due diligence should occur before, not after, a purchase closes.

## Core Content
Environmental due diligence, most commonly beginning with a Phase I Environmental Site Assessment, investigates a property's history and current condition for signs of potential contamination — from prior industrial use, underground storage tanks, dry cleaning operations, or nearby contaminated sites, among other sources. VERIFIED FACT: a Phase I assessment is a records review and site inspection; if it identifies red flags, a Phase II assessment (which involves actual soil or groundwater testing) is typically the next step to determine whether contamination is actually present.

Here is the why this matters as a distinct risk category, separate from the building-focused analysis covered elsewhere in this level: in many jurisdictions, environmental cleanup liability can attach to the current property owner regardless of whether they caused the contamination, meaning an investor who unknowingly purchases a contaminated property can become financially responsible for cleanup costs that can run into the hundreds of thousands of dollars or more, a risk far outside the scope of the operational and financial analysis covered in earlier lessons.

This is why environmental due diligence must occur before closing, not after, and why property types with a history of industrial, agricultural, automotive, or dry-cleaning use, or proximity to known contaminated sites, warrant heightened environmental scrutiny before a purchase decision is finalized. Obtaining appropriate environmental due diligence can also, in many jurisdictions, help establish a legal defense against certain liability if contamination is later discovered despite a properly conducted assessment — REGION-SPECIFIC RULE, confirm applicable law with a qualified environmental attorney.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: An investor is evaluating a commercial property that previously housed a dry-cleaning business decades earlier, an industry commonly associated with soil and groundwater contamination risk from cleaning solvents. A Phase I assessment flags this prior use as a recognized environmental condition, prompting a Phase II assessment involving soil testing, which reveals contamination requiring remediation. Armed with this information before closing, the investor can factor remediation costs into their offer, require the seller to address the issue, or walk away entirely — all far better outcomes than discovering the same contamination after already owning the property.

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** A Phase I assessment is primarily a documentary and visual investigation; actual sampling occurs in a subsequent Phase II if red flags are identified.

**Q2.** The potential for significant, owner-attached liability regardless of fault makes this a uniquely serious risk category distinct from typical operational or financial risks.

**Q3.** These use categories are commonly associated with contamination risk from chemicals, solvents, fuels, or pesticides used in those industries.

**Q4.** Pre-closing due diligence preserves the buyer's options, while post-closing discovery leaves the new owner facing the liability with fewer options.

**Q5.** Proper due diligence can support certain legal defenses in many jurisdictions, though the specifics depend on applicable law and should be verified with qualified counsel.

## Key Takeaways
- Environmental due diligence, starting with a Phase I assessment, investigates a property's history for signs of contamination risk.
- Cleanup liability can attach to the current owner in many jurisdictions regardless of who caused the contamination.
- Properties with industrial, agricultural, automotive, or dry-cleaning history warrant heightened environmental scrutiny.
- Environmental due diligence should always occur before closing, and may help establish legal defenses depending on jurisdiction.
