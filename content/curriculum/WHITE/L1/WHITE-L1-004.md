## Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Define a fund and explain why diversification reduces risk
- Distinguish between ETFs, index funds, and actively managed mutual funds
- Explain the significance of the expense ratio and how costs compound over time
- Identify the most common index funds and what they track
- Describe the principle of passive investing and why it outperforms active management in the long run

## Core Content

### What Is a Fund?

A fund is a pooled investment vehicle. Hundreds or thousands of investors contribute money, and the fund uses that pool to purchase a diversified portfolio of securities. Each investor owns a proportional share of the fund's holdings.

Diversification is the core advantage. A single stock or bond can lose its entire value. A fund holding 500 companies cannot. The gains of the winners offset the losses of the laggards. Over time, diversification dramatically reduces the risk of catastrophic loss without sacrificing long-term returns.

### The Three Main Fund Types

**Index funds** track a market index — a predefined list of securities designed to represent a market or segment. The S&P 500 index tracks 500 large U.S. companies. A total market index tracks thousands of U.S. companies. The fund holds the same securities as the index, in the same proportions. No judgment required. No active decisions.

**Exchange-traded funds (ETFs)** are funds that trade on a stock exchange throughout the day, like individual stocks. ETFs can track indexes, sectors, commodities, or strategies. They are purchased through a brokerage account and bought or sold at market prices in real time.

**Actively managed mutual funds** employ professional portfolio managers who research securities and make active buy/sell decisions in an attempt to outperform the market. They trade less frequently than ETFs and are priced once per day after market close.

Most index funds are also structured as mutual funds or ETFs — the terms overlap. The critical distinction is passive (index) vs. active (managed) management.

### The Expense Ratio

The expense ratio is the annual fee charged by a fund, expressed as a percentage of your investment. It is deducted automatically — you never write a check, but you feel it in your returns.

| Fund Type | Typical Expense Ratio |
|-----------|----------------------|
| Passive index ETF | 0.03% – 0.10% |
| Actively managed mutual fund | 0.50% – 1.50% |

This difference compounds dramatically over time. An investor with $100,000 invested for 30 years at 8% return:
- Pays 0.05% annual expenses: ends with approximately $985,000
- Pays 1.00% annual expenses: ends with approximately $761,000

The expense ratio difference costs $224,000 over 30 years — on a $100,000 initial investment.

### The Most Important Indexes

**S&P 500** — 500 large U.S. companies. Often used as a proxy for the U.S. market. Funds: SPY, VOO, IVV.

**Total U.S. Market** — virtually all publicly traded U.S. companies. Broader than S&P 500. Funds: VTI, ITOT.

**Total International** — companies outside the U.S. For global diversification. Funds: VXUS, EFA.

**U.S. Aggregate Bond Index** — broad U.S. bond market. Funds: BND, AGG.

### Why Passive Investing Outperforms Active Management

Research consistently demonstrates that the majority of actively managed funds underperform their benchmark index over 10-year and 20-year periods, even before accounting for fees.

The reasons are structural:
- Active managers charge fees that eat into returns
- Every trade has a cost (bid-ask spread, commissions)
- Market prices already reflect available information — it is extremely difficult to consistently find mispriced securities

The passive investor does not try to beat the market. They own the market and capture the market return, minus a tiny fee.
