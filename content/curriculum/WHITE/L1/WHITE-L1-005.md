## Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Explain the difference between tax-deferred and tax-free accounts
- Describe how a 401(k) works, including employer matching
- Distinguish between a Traditional IRA and a Roth IRA
- Identify the 2026 contribution limits for each account type
- Determine which account type is most advantageous given your current tax situation

## Core Content

### Why Tax-Advantaged Accounts Matter

When you invest in a standard brokerage account, you pay taxes on:
- Dividends received each year
- Capital gains when you sell an appreciated position

These taxes reduce the amount you reinvest and therefore compound over time. Tax-advantaged accounts eliminate or defer this tax drag — allowing the full return to compound undisturbed for decades.

The impact is substantial. The same investment in a tax-advantaged account versus a taxable account, held for 30 years, can produce tens of thousands of additional dollars in final value for the average investor.

### The 401(k) Plan

A 401(k) is an employer-sponsored retirement plan. Contributions are made through payroll deduction before your paycheck. Your taxable income is reduced by the amount you contribute.

**Key features:**
- Contributions are pre-tax (Traditional 401k) or post-tax (Roth 401k option at some employers)
- Earnings grow tax-deferred until withdrawal
- 2026 employee contribution limit: $23,000 (under age 50); $30,500 with catch-up contribution (age 50+)
- Withdrawals in retirement are taxed as ordinary income (Traditional) or tax-free (Roth)
- Early withdrawals before age 59½ trigger a 10% penalty plus income tax

**Employer matching:** Many employers match employee contributions — often 50%–100% of contributions up to 3%–6% of salary. This match is free money. Always contribute at least enough to capture the full employer match before investing anywhere else.

### The Individual Retirement Account (IRA)

An IRA is a personal retirement account you open independently of your employer. It holds the same types of investments (stocks, bonds, funds) but offers significant tax advantages.

**Traditional IRA:**
- Contributions may be tax-deductible depending on income and 401k participation
- Earnings grow tax-deferred
- Withdrawals in retirement are taxed as ordinary income
- 2026 contribution limit: $7,000 ($8,000 with catch-up, age 50+)

**Roth IRA:**
- Contributions are made with after-tax dollars (no deduction)
- Earnings grow tax-free
- Withdrawals in retirement are completely tax-free
- 2026 income limits phase out Roth eligibility at higher incomes

### Traditional vs. Roth: Which Is Better?

The choice depends on when your taxes will be higher: now or in retirement.

**Prefer Traditional (tax now) if:**
- You are in a high tax bracket today
- You expect to be in a lower bracket in retirement

**Prefer Roth (tax later) if:**
- You are in a low or moderate tax bracket today
- You expect higher income or tax rates in retirement
- You are young and have decades for tax-free compounding

For most people in the early stages of their career, the Roth IRA is the most powerful tool available. Tax-free compounding over 30+ years is extraordinarily valuable.

### The Optimal Investment Order

For most working Americans, the priority order for investing is:

1. **Contribute to 401(k) up to the employer match** — capture free money first
2. **Max out Roth IRA** — $7,000 per year of tax-free growth
3. **Return to 401(k) and contribute to the annual limit** — $23,000 per year
4. **Invest in taxable brokerage account** — no limits, full flexibility

This order maximizes tax efficiency before deploying capital into taxable accounts.
