## Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Define asset allocation and explain why it is the primary driver of portfolio outcomes
- Describe how to construct a simple three-fund portfolio
- Explain diversification across asset classes, geographies, and time
- Identify how your risk tolerance and time horizon should influence your allocation
- Describe the concept of rebalancing and why it is necessary

## Core Content

### What Is Asset Allocation?

Asset allocation is the process of dividing your investment portfolio among different asset categories — primarily equities, fixed income, and cash equivalents. Studies consistently show that asset allocation explains more than 90% of the variation in long-term portfolio performance. Security selection and market timing account for the rest.

In other words: deciding how much to hold in stocks vs. bonds matters far more than deciding which stocks to buy.

### The Three-Fund Portfolio

The three-fund portfolio is the simplest, most evidence-based portfolio construction method available to individual investors. It consists of:

1. **Total U.S. Stock Market fund** (e.g., VTI) — all publicly traded U.S. companies
2. **Total International Stock Market fund** (e.g., VXUS) — all publicly traded non-U.S. companies
3. **Total U.S. Bond Market fund** (e.g., BND) — broad U.S. investment-grade bonds

Three funds. Thousands of securities. Minimal cost. Maximum diversification. This portfolio, held for decades with regular contributions, has produced wealth for millions of ordinary investors.

### Determining Your Allocation

Your allocation between equities and fixed income should reflect:

**Time horizon:** How many years before you need the money?
- 20+ years: aggressive allocation is appropriate (80–100% equities)
- 10–20 years: moderate allocation (60–80% equities)
- Under 10 years: conservative allocation (40–60% equities)
- Under 5 years: capital preservation is primary (20–40% equities)

**Risk tolerance:** How much temporary loss can you handle emotionally without selling?
- High tolerance: higher equity allocation
- Low tolerance: higher fixed income allocation

**Risk capacity:** How much loss can you afford financially?
- If you have no emergency fund and no job security, you cannot afford large losses even if you are young

A simple guideline: your age as the percentage held in bonds (40 years old → 40% bonds). Use this as a starting point for thinking, not as a rigid prescription.

### Geographic Diversification

Holding only U.S. stocks concentrates your portfolio in one country's economic performance. Total international funds provide exposure to hundreds of companies in dozens of countries.

A commonly cited allocation: 60–70% U.S. / 30–40% international for the equity portion. The exact split is less important than having meaningful international exposure.

### Rebalancing

As markets move, your allocation drifts. If equities outperform bonds, your portfolio becomes more equity-heavy than intended — taking more risk than you planned.

Rebalancing restores your target allocation by selling the outperforming asset class and buying the underperforming one. It enforces buying low and selling high systematically.

Rebalance once per year or when any allocation drifts more than 5–10 percentage points from target. Do not rebalance too frequently — it generates unnecessary taxes and transaction costs.
