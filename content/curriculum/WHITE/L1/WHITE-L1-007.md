## Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Explain how stock exchanges function as marketplaces
- Distinguish between primary and secondary markets
- Identify the four most common order types and when to use each
- Explain what causes short-term price volatility
- Describe the role of market makers and liquidity

## Core Content

### The Stock Exchange as a Marketplace

A stock exchange is an organized marketplace where buyers and sellers meet to trade securities. The two largest U.S. exchanges are:

- **New York Stock Exchange (NYSE)** — the largest equity exchange by market capitalization
- **NASDAQ** — a fully electronic exchange; home to many technology companies

Exchanges operate continuously during market hours (9:30 AM – 4:00 PM Eastern, Monday through Friday) and provide a transparent, regulated environment for price discovery.

### Primary vs. Secondary Markets

**Primary market:** Where securities are initially issued and sold. When a company goes public through an initial public offering (IPO), it sells new shares to investors for the first time. The company receives the proceeds.

**Secondary market:** Where investors buy and sell existing shares among themselves. When you buy Apple stock through a brokerage account, you are buying from another investor — not from Apple. Apple receives no money from secondary market transactions.

Most investing occurs in the secondary market. Understanding this distinction helps explain why stock price movements do not directly affect the company's cash position.

### Order Types

**Market order:** Execute immediately at the current market price. Guarantees execution but not price. Best for liquid securities when speed matters more than price precision.

**Limit order:** Execute only at your specified price or better. You set the maximum price you will pay (buying) or the minimum price you will accept (selling). May not execute if the price never reaches your limit.

**Stop order (stop-loss):** Triggers a market order when the price falls to a specified level. Used to limit losses on a position. Converts to a market order when triggered — execution price is not guaranteed.

**Stop-limit order:** Triggers a limit order when the price hits a specified level. More precise than a stop order but may not execute in a fast-moving market.

For most long-term investors buying low-cost index funds, market orders or limit orders set near the current price are appropriate. Complex order types matter more for active traders.

### What Causes Price Movements?

In the short term, stock prices move because of:
- **Earnings announcements** — quarterly results vs. analyst expectations
- **Economic data** — inflation, employment, GDP growth
- **Company news** — leadership changes, product launches, lawsuits
- **Market sentiment** — investor fear and optimism, often disconnected from fundamentals

In the long term, stock prices follow earnings. Companies that grow their earnings over decades produce growing stock prices. Short-term noise is temporary. Long-term fundamental growth is permanent.

This distinction is critical: long-term investors should not react to short-term price movements. The investor who sells during a correction locks in their loss. The investor who holds — or buys more — captures the recovery.

### Market Makers and Liquidity

Market makers are financial firms that continuously stand ready to buy and sell a security. They provide liquidity — the ability to buy or sell quickly without moving the price significantly.

The bid-ask spread (the difference between the price you can sell at and the price you can buy at) is how market makers earn their compensation. For highly liquid securities like major ETFs, spreads are fractions of a penny.
