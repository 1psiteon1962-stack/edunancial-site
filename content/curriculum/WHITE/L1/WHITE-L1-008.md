## Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Identify the three primary financial statements and what each measures
- Read a basic income statement and identify key line items
- Read a basic balance sheet and calculate net worth
- Explain why cash flow is often more important than reported earnings
- Define common financial ratios used to evaluate stocks

## Core Content

### Why Financial Statements Matter for Investors

Investing without reading financial statements is speculation — you are guessing about outcomes. Reading financial statements gives you evidence about a company's revenue growth, profitability, debt load, and cash generation. Evidence-based investment decisions produce better outcomes than hope-based ones.

Public companies are required to file financial statements with the SEC quarterly (10-Q) and annually (10-K). These filings are freely available at sec.gov and on any major financial data website.

### The Income Statement

The income statement reports what a company earned and spent over a specific period (quarter or year).

Key line items:
- **Revenue (Sales):** Total money received from customers
- **Cost of Goods Sold (COGS):** Direct costs of producing goods or services
- **Gross Profit:** Revenue minus COGS
- **Operating Expenses:** SG&A (selling, general, and administrative costs), R&D
- **Operating Income (EBIT):** Gross profit minus operating expenses
- **Net Income:** The "bottom line" — profit after all expenses, interest, and taxes

**Trend matters more than a single number.** A company with growing revenue and expanding margins over five years is more compelling than one with one good quarter.

### The Balance Sheet

The balance sheet is a snapshot of what a company owns (assets) and owes (liabilities) at a specific point in time.

**Assets** = Cash + Receivables + Inventory + Property + Investments + Intangibles

**Liabilities** = Accounts payable + Debt + Other obligations

**Shareholders' Equity** = Assets minus Liabilities (the "book value" of the company)

The balance sheet is governed by the fundamental equation:
**Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders' Equity**

A company with more assets than liabilities is solvent. A company with significant debt relative to equity carries higher financial risk.

### The Cash Flow Statement

The cash flow statement tracks actual cash moving in and out of the business. Unlike the income statement (which uses accrual accounting), the cash flow statement reflects real cash.

Three sections:
- **Operating activities:** Cash generated by core business operations
- **Investing activities:** Cash spent on or received from long-term assets
- **Financing activities:** Cash from borrowing, repaying debt, or issuing/repurchasing shares

**Free cash flow (FCF)** = Operating cash flow − Capital expenditures

Free cash flow is the cash available to grow the business, pay dividends, or repurchase shares. Many experienced investors consider free cash flow more important than reported net income, because earnings can be manipulated through accounting choices while cash is harder to fake.

### Common Investor Ratios

**Price-to-Earnings (P/E):** Share price divided by earnings per share. A P/E of 20 means investors are paying $20 for every $1 of annual earnings. Higher P/E implies higher growth expectations or overvaluation.

**Price-to-Free Cash Flow (P/FCF):** Share price divided by free cash flow per share. Often preferred over P/E for capital-intensive businesses.

**Debt-to-Equity (D/E):** Total debt divided by shareholders' equity. High D/E indicates financial leverage — higher potential returns but also higher bankruptcy risk.

**Return on Equity (ROE):** Net income divided by shareholders' equity. Measures how efficiently management uses invested capital.

## Application

Before proceeding to the next lesson, complete the following:

1. Download the most recent annual report (10-K) for any major publicly traded company you are familiar with. Locate the income statement. Write down revenue, gross profit, and net income for the most recent year and the year before.
2. Calculate the P/E ratio for the same company using the current stock price and the most recent annual earnings per share.
3. Find the free cash flow for the same company (operating cash flow minus capital expenditures from the cash flow statement). Determine whether it is positive or negative and write a one-sentence interpretation.
