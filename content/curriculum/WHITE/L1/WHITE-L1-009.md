## Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Distinguish between systematic and unsystematic risk
- Explain volatility as a measure of investment risk
- Describe how diversification eliminates unsystematic risk
- Identify behavioral risks that destroy investor returns
- Apply a risk management framework to your own portfolio

## Core Content

### The Two Categories of Investment Risk

**Systematic risk (market risk):** Risk that affects the entire market and cannot be eliminated through diversification. Examples include recessions, rising interest rates, geopolitical events, and pandemics. You accept this risk when you invest in the market. The expected return of the market compensates you for accepting it.

**Unsystematic risk (specific risk):** Risk specific to an individual company or sector. A product recall, CEO scandal, or accounting fraud can destroy a single company's value. This risk can be eliminated through diversification. Holding 500 stocks means a single company's failure is a rounding error on your portfolio.

**The practical implication:** Never concentrate your portfolio in a single stock or sector unless you have a specific reason and understand the risk. Diversification eliminates unsystematic risk at no cost.

### Volatility as a Risk Measure

Volatility measures how much an investment's price fluctuates over time. Standard deviation is the statistical measure of price dispersion from the average.

High volatility = large price swings. This is uncomfortable psychologically but acceptable financially for long-term investors, because the expected return compensates for the discomfort.

**Beta** measures a security's volatility relative to the market. A beta of 1.0 means the security moves with the market. A beta of 1.5 means it moves 50% more than the market (up or down). A beta of 0.5 means it moves 50% less.

High-beta stocks offer higher potential returns and higher potential losses. Long-term investors can afford high beta when they have time to recover from downturns.

### Behavioral Risk: The Investor's Greatest Enemy

Research by DALBAR and others consistently shows that individual investors earn significantly less than the funds they invest in — because they make emotional decisions at the wrong times.

The behavioral pattern:
1. Markets rise → investors feel confident → they add money at high prices
2. Markets fall → investors feel fearful → they sell at low prices
3. Markets recover → investors buy back in at higher prices

This buy-high-sell-low cycle destroys wealth systematically. The fund returned 8% per year. The investor earned 4% per year — because they kept making emotional decisions.

The solution is an investment policy statement: a written plan describing your asset allocation, contribution schedule, and rebalancing rules — created in calm conditions and followed in turbulent ones.

### Concentration Risk

Holding too much of your net worth in a single position — especially your employer's stock — is one of the most common and dangerous mistakes individual investors make.

Employees at Enron, WorldCom, and Lehman Brothers held large portions of their retirement savings in company stock. When those companies failed, employees lost both their jobs and their retirement simultaneously.

Rule: Never hold more than 5–10% of your portfolio in any single company's stock, including your employer's.

### Sequence of Returns Risk

For investors near or in retirement, the sequence of investment returns matters as much as the average return. A large loss early in retirement — when you are withdrawing from the portfolio — can permanently impair your ability to recover, even if later returns are strong.

Managing sequence of returns risk requires maintaining a cash buffer (one to two years of living expenses) and adjusting withdrawal rates during significant market downturns.

## Application

Before proceeding to the next lesson, complete the following:

1. Calculate the percentage of your total net worth held in any single investment (stock, fund, or account). If any single holding exceeds 20%, identify it and write a plan to reduce concentration over the next 12 months.
2. Write an investment policy statement — a single paragraph describing your target allocation, how often you will contribute, and one rule you commit to following when markets fall.
3. Look up the 20-year annualized return of the S&P 500 and the 20-year average return of individual equity investors (from DALBAR's annual report or similar source). Write down the difference and a one-sentence explanation of why the gap exists.
