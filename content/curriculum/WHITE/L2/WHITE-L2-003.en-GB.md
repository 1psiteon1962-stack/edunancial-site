## WHITE-L2-003
### The Value Lens: Buying Assets for Less Than They're Worth

**1. Learning Objectives**
By the end of this lesson, the learner will be able to:
- Explain the general premise of value investing.
- Identify common metrics associated with the value lens.
- Recognise risks and criticisms associated with this approach.

**2. Core Content**
Value investing generally involves seeking assets that appear to be trading for less than some estimate of their underlying or "intrinsic" worth, on the premise that the market price will eventually move closer to that estimated worth. Common metrics associated with this lens include price-to-earnings ratio (comparing a stock's price to its earnings) and price-to-book ratio (comparing price to the company's accounting net asset value), among others — though no single metric tells the whole story, and value investors typically combine several factors with broader research into a company's fundamentals.

A central risk with this lens is the "value trap" — an asset that appears cheap by these metrics but is cheap for a legitimate, lasting reason (a declining business, an outdated product, deteriorating fundamentals), meaning the price may never recover to reflect a supposedly higher intrinsic worth. Distinguishing a genuine value opportunity from a value trap generally requires understanding *why* the asset is priced as it is, not just noting that a metric looks favourable in isolation.

**3. Worked Example**
Applying a value lens, Rosalind identifies a company trading at a low price-to-earnings ratio relative to its industry peers. Rather than buying immediately based on that single metric, she researches further and finds the company has a temporary, resolvable issue (a one-time legal settlement affecting recent earnings) rather than a structural decline in its underlying business — supporting the case that this is a genuine value opportunity rather than a value trap.

Her colleague, applying the same lens less carefully, buys a different company with an even lower price-to-earnings ratio without investigating further, only to later discover the company's core business was in genuine, ongoing decline — an example of the value trap this lesson warns against.

**4. Practice Quiz**
1. What is the general premise of value investing?
   a) Buying only fast-growing companies
   b) Seeking assets trading for less than an estimated intrinsic worth
   c) Avoiding all research
   d) Focusing exclusively on dividend payments
2. What is a "value trap"?
   a) A guaranteed profitable opportunity
   b) An asset that looks cheap by certain metrics but is cheap for a lasting, legitimate reason
   c) A type of bond
   d) A tax penalty
3. True or False: A low price-to-earnings ratio alone is sufficient proof that an asset is a good value investment.
4. Why did Rosalind's approach avoid a potential value trap, unlike her colleague's?
   a) She ignored the price-to-earnings ratio entirely
   b) She researched the underlying reason for the low valuation before deciding
   c) She bought without any research
   d) She only considered dividend history

**5. Answer Key**
1. b  2. b  3. False  4. b

**6. Answers and Explanations**
1. Value investing seeks assets priced below an estimated intrinsic worth, expecting the market to eventually reflect that worth.
2. A value trap is an asset that appears cheap by certain metrics but for a legitimate, lasting reason that may prevent price recovery.
3. A single favourable metric does not, by itself, confirm a genuine value opportunity versus a value trap.
4. Rosalind's deeper research into the reason behind the low valuation distinguished a genuine opportunity from a potential trap.

**7. Key Takeaways**
- Value investing seeks assets priced below an estimated intrinsic worth.
- Common metrics include price-to-earnings and price-to-book ratios, though no single metric is sufficient alone.
- A "value trap" is a cheap-looking asset that is cheap for a lasting, legitimate reason.
- Distinguishing genuine value from a value trap requires understanding the underlying reason for the low price.
