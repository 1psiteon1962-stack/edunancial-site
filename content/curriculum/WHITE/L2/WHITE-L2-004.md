## WHITE-L2-004
### The Growth Lens: Paying Up for Future Potential

**1. Learning Objectives**
By the end of this lesson, the learner will be able to:
- Explain the general premise of growth investing.
- Contrast the growth lens with the value lens.
- Identify risks associated with paying a premium for expected future growth.

**2. Core Content**
Growth investing generally involves seeking assets expected to grow revenue, earnings, or market presence at an above-average rate, with investors often willing to pay a higher price relative to current earnings (a higher price-to-earnings ratio, contrasting with Lesson 3's value lens) in anticipation of that future growth materializing.

Where the value lens asks "is this priced below what it's currently worth," the growth lens asks "is this likely to become worth significantly more in the future, justifying today's higher price." Both lenses involve estimating something uncertain about the future, but growth investing places more weight on a company's trajectory and expansion story than on its current, measurable fundamentals.

A central risk of this lens is that growth expectations may not materialize, or may materialize more slowly than the price already assumes — a company can grow its actual business substantially and still see its stock price fall, if the growth achieved falls short of what was already priced in.

**3. Worked Example**
Applying a growth lens, Marcus invests in a company with a high current price-to-earnings ratio, based on strong projected revenue growth from an expanding product line. Two years later, the company's revenue does grow — but at a slower pace than the market had originally priced in, and the stock price falls despite genuine business growth having occurred.

Marcus's experience illustrates the specific risk of the growth lens: the underlying business did not fail, but the price had already assumed a level of growth that was not fully met, disappointing investors who had bought in anticipating a faster trajectory.

**4. Practice Quiz**
1. What does growth investing generally prioritize?
   a) Current, measurable fundamentals over future trajectory
   b) A company's expected future growth trajectory, often at a higher current price
   c) Avoiding any price premium
   d) Dividend history exclusively
2. What risk is specifically associated with the growth lens?
   a) None — growth investing carries no risk
   b) Growth expectations may not materialize as quickly as the price assumes
   c) The company can never grow
   d) It always guarantees a loss
3. True or False: A company that genuinely grows its business will always see its stock price rise.
4. Why did Marcus's investment lose value despite the company's real revenue growth?
   a) The company's revenue actually declined
   b) The growth achieved fell short of what the price had already assumed
   c) He sold at the wrong time for unrelated reasons
   d) The company went bankrupt

**5. Answer Key**
1. b  2. b  3. False  4. b

**6. Answers and Explanations**
1. Growth investing prioritizes expected future growth, often accepting a higher current price in anticipation.
2. The core growth-lens risk is that anticipated growth may not materialize as fully or quickly as the price assumes.
3. Stock prices reflect expectations, so even genuine growth can disappoint if it falls short of what was already priced in.
4. Marcus's outcome resulted from growth falling short of the market's prior expectations, not an actual business failure.

**7. Key Takeaways**
- Growth investing seeks assets expected to grow significantly, often at a premium current price.
- The growth lens contrasts with the value lens by prioritizing future trajectory over current fundamentals.
- A key risk is that anticipated growth may not materialize as fully or as quickly as the price assumes.
- Genuine business growth does not guarantee stock price appreciation if it falls short of prior expectations.
