## WHITE-L2-005
### The Income Lens: Investing for Cash Flow

**1. Learning Objectives**
By the end of this lesson, the learner will be able to:
- Explain the general premise of income investing.
- Identify common income-generating paper assets.
- Compare the income lens's priorities to the value and growth lenses.

**2. Core Content**
Income investing generally prioritises assets that produce regular cash flow — dividends from stocks (Lesson 21), interest from bonds (Lessons 9–12), or distributions from certain funds — over assets expected to appreciate significantly in price but produce little or no current cash flow. This lens often appeals to investors seeking supplemental or retirement income, echoing the RED-track discussion of real estate cash flow versus appreciation (RED-L2-006), a genuine parallel structure across colours.

Where the growth lens accepts a higher price for future potential, and the value lens seeks a discount to current worth, the income lens prioritises the reliability and size of ongoing cash distributions, sometimes accepting slower price appreciation in exchange for that steady income stream.

A risk associated with this lens: high current income can sometimes signal underlying business stress rather than strength — a company facing declining prospects might maintain a high dividend temporarily even as its ability to sustain that dividend weakens, an issue sometimes called a "dividend trap," echoing the value trap concept from Lesson 3.

**3. Worked Example**
Seeking supplemental income for her semi-retirement, Beatrice builds a portfolio weighted toward companies and funds with established histories of steady dividend payments, prioritising consistency over the highest possible current yield. She specifically avoids a company offering an unusually high dividend yield after researching and finding that yield was elevated primarily because the company's stock price had fallen sharply due to genuine business struggles — a potential dividend trap rather than a sign of unusual opportunity.

**4. Practice Quiz**
1. What does income investing generally prioritise?
   a) Rapid price appreciation only
   b) Regular cash flow through dividends, interest, or distributions
   c) Avoiding all cash flow
   d) Only very young, unproven companies
2. What is a "dividend trap"?
   a) A guaranteed profitable dividend
   b) An unusually high dividend that may signal underlying business stress rather than opportunity
   c) A type of bond rating
   d) A tax benefit
3. True or False: A very high current dividend yield is always a sign of a healthy, strong company.
4. Why did Beatrice avoid the company with an unusually high dividend yield?
   a) She disliked dividends generally
   b) Research suggested the high yield stemmed from business struggles, a potential dividend trap
   c) The company had no dividend history
   d) She preferred growth investing instead

**5. Answer Key**
1. b  2. b  3. False  4. b

**6. Answers and Explanations**
1. Income investing prioritises regular cash flow from dividends, interest, or distributions.
2. A dividend trap is an unusually high yield that may reflect business stress rather than a genuine opportunity.
3. An unusually high yield can sometimes reflect a falling stock price due to underlying problems, not strength.
4. Beatrice's research into the reason behind the high yield revealed a potential dividend trap, prompting her to avoid it.

**7. Key Takeaways**
- Income investing prioritises regular cash flow over rapid price appreciation.
- Common income sources include stock dividends, bond interest, and fund distributions.
- This lens parallels the RED-track cash flow discussion, illustrating a genuine cross-colour pattern.
- A "dividend trap" — unusually high yield masking business stress — is a key risk to research before investing.
