## WHITE-L2-010
### How Interest Rates and Bond Prices Interact

**1. Learning Objectives**
By the end of this lesson, the learner will be able to:
- Explain the general inverse relationship between interest rates and existing bond prices.
- Describe why this relationship exists conceptually.
- Recognize how bond maturity length affects sensitivity to rate changes.

**2. Core Content**
Existing bond prices and prevailing interest rates generally move in opposite directions — a foundational, widely observed relationship in fixed-income markets. When prevailing interest rates rise, newly issued bonds offer higher coupon rates to match, making existing bonds with lower, already-fixed coupon rates comparatively less attractive; their market price generally falls to compensate a potential buyer for that lower relative payment. When prevailing rates fall, existing bonds with higher, already-fixed coupon rates become comparatively more attractive, and their market price generally rises.

This does not affect a bond held to maturity in terms of its promised payments (assuming no default) — the issuer still owes the original face value and coupon payments regardless of market price movements in between. It primarily matters for an investor who might need or want to sell a bond before maturity, or for anyone evaluating a bond fund, whose share price reflects the current market value of its underlying bond holdings.

Generally, bonds with longer maturities are more sensitive to interest rate changes than bonds with shorter maturities, since a change in rates affects a longer stream of future fixed payments more significantly in present-value terms.

**3. Worked Example**
An investor holds a bond paying a 3% coupon rate. Shortly after purchase, prevailing interest rates rise, and newly issued similar bonds now offer a 5% coupon rate. If this investor needed to sell their existing 3% bond before maturity, they would generally need to accept a lower price than they paid, since a rational buyer would prefer a new 5% bond unless compensated with a discount on the older, lower-paying bond.

If, instead, this investor holds the bond to maturity, they still receive their original 3% coupon payments and the full face value at maturity, unaffected by this market price fluctuation in between — illustrating why this relationship matters differently depending on whether a bond is held to maturity or potentially sold earlier.

**4. Practice Quiz**
1. What generally happens to existing bond prices when interest rates rise?
   a) They generally rise
   b) They generally fall
   c) They are unaffected
   d) They automatically default
2. Why does this inverse relationship generally exist?
   a) Existing lower-coupon bonds become less attractive compared to new, higher-coupon bonds
   b) Bond prices are set randomly
   c) Interest rates have no effect on bond attractiveness
   d) All bonds pay the same coupon rate
3. True or False: A bond held to maturity is directly affected in its promised payments by interim market price fluctuations.
4. Which bonds are generally more sensitive to interest rate changes?
   a) Shorter-maturity bonds
   b) Longer-maturity bonds
   c) All bonds are equally sensitive
   d) Bonds have no sensitivity to rates

**5. Answer Key**
1. b  2. a  3. False  4. b

**6. Answers and Explanations**
1. Rising interest rates generally cause existing (lower-coupon) bond prices to fall.
2. Existing lower-coupon bonds become less attractive relative to newly issued higher-coupon bonds, pressuring their price down.
3. A bond held to maturity still receives its originally promised payments, regardless of interim market price swings.
4. Longer-maturity bonds are generally more sensitive to interest rate changes than shorter-maturity bonds.

**7. Key Takeaways**
- Existing bond prices and prevailing interest rates generally move in opposite directions.
- This relationship stems from newly issued bonds' coupon rates changing to match prevailing rates.
- A bond held to maturity still receives its original promised payments regardless of interim price swings.
- Longer-maturity bonds are generally more sensitive to interest rate changes than shorter-maturity bonds.
