## WHITE-L2-013
### Mutual Funds vs. ETFs: Structural Differences

**1. Learning Objectives**
By the end of this lesson, the learner will be able to:
- Compare mutual funds and ETFs across trading mechanics, pricing, and typical minimums.
- Explain how each structure affects an investor's practical experience.
- Recognise that both can pursue similar underlying investment strategies.

**2. Core Content**
Mutual funds and exchange-traded funds (ETFs) both pool investor money into a diversified basket of underlying assets, but their structures differ in practical ways. Mutual fund shares are typically bought and sold once per day at a single calculated price (net asset value, or NAV) determined after markets close, and some mutual funds carry minimum investment requirements. ETF shares, by contrast, typically trade throughout the day on an exchange like a stock, with prices fluctuating continuously based on supply and demand (which generally tracks the underlying assets' value closely, though not perfectly at every moment), and often without a minimum investment requirement beyond the price of a single share (or fractional share, where available).

Both structures can pursue similar underlying strategies — a mutual fund and an ETF can each track the same index, or each pursue an actively managed value or growth strategy. The choice between them often comes down to trading flexibility, cost structure (Lesson 15), and specific fund availability, rather than one structure being universally superior for every strategy.

**3. Worked Example**
Comparing two funds tracking the same broad market index, one structured as a mutual fund and one as an ETF, Renee notices the mutual fund only allows trades at the end-of-day NAV price, while the ETF can be bought or sold at any point during market hours at its current trading price. For her specific situation — occasional smaller purchases throughout the month rather than one large transaction — she finds the ETF's flexibility and lack of minimum investment requirement better suited to her practical needs, even though both funds track the identical underlying index.

**4. Practice Quiz**
1. When are mutual fund shares typically priced and traded?
   a) Continuously throughout the day
   b) Once per day, at a calculated net asset value after markets close
   c) Never — they cannot be traded
   d) Only once per year
2. How do ETF shares typically trade?
   a) Only once per day
   b) Throughout the day on an exchange, like a stock
   c) Only through a bank
   d) They cannot be bought or sold
3. True or False: Mutual funds and ETFs can never pursue the same or similar underlying investment strategy.
4. Why did Renee prefer the ETF structure for her specific situation?
   a) It was the only option available
   b) Its intraday trading flexibility and lack of minimum suited her smaller, more frequent purchases
   c) The mutual fund was illegal
   d) ETFs guarantee higher returns

**5. Answer Key**
1. b  2. b  3. False  4. b

**6. Answers and Explanations**
1. Mutual funds are typically priced and traded once daily at a calculated NAV after markets close.
2. ETFs typically trade throughout the day on an exchange, with continuously fluctuating prices.
3. Both structures can pursue similar or even identical underlying strategies, such as tracking the same index.
4. Renee's practical trading pattern was better served by the ETF's intraday flexibility and lack of minimum investment.

**7. Key Takeaways**
- Mutual funds trade once daily at a calculated NAV; ETFs trade throughout the day like stocks.
- ETFs often lack minimum investment requirements beyond a single share's price; some mutual funds have minimums.
- Both structures can pursue similar or identical underlying investment strategies.
- The right choice often depends on trading flexibility needs and cost structure, not universal superiority.
