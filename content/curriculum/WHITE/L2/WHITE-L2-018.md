## WHITE-L2-018
### Rebalancing: Keeping the Portfolio on Target

**1. Learning Objectives**
By the end of this lesson, the learner will be able to:
- Explain the general concept of portfolio rebalancing.
- Describe why a portfolio's allocation can drift over time without rebalancing.
- Identify common approaches to rebalancing.

**2. Core Content**
Because different asset classes perform differently over time, a portfolio's actual allocation naturally drifts away from its original target allocation (Lesson 16) as some holdings grow faster than others. Rebalancing is the periodic process of adjusting a portfolio back toward its target allocation — typically by selling a portion of holdings that have grown to exceed their target percentage and buying more of holdings that have fallen below their target percentage.

Common rebalancing approaches include calendar-based rebalancing (reviewing and adjusting at set intervals, such as annually) and threshold-based rebalancing (adjusting whenever an allocation drifts beyond a specified percentage from its target, regardless of timing). Rebalancing serves a risk-management purpose as much as a return-seeking one: without it, a portfolio can gradually become more aggressive (if stocks have outperformed and grown to dominate the allocation) or more conservative (if the opposite occurs) than originally intended, independent of any deliberate decision by the investor.

**3. Worked Example**
Sanjay originally set a target allocation of 70% stocks and 30% bonds. After several years of strong stock performance, he checks his portfolio and finds it has drifted to 82% stocks and 18% bonds — not because he made any active decision to become more aggressive, but simply because stocks grew faster than bonds during that period.

Recognizing this drift as inconsistent with his original risk tolerance and plan, Sanjay rebalances by selling a portion of his stock holdings and buying bonds, restoring his portfolio closer to its original 70/30 target — a deliberate, disciplined action rather than simply "riding the wave" of recent stock performance.

**4. Practice Quiz**
1. Why does a portfolio's allocation drift over time without rebalancing?
   a) It never drifts
   b) Different asset classes perform differently, causing some to grow faster than others
   c) All asset classes always grow at exactly the same rate
   d) Rebalancing happens automatically without any action
2. What does rebalancing generally involve?
   a) Ignoring the portfolio entirely
   b) Selling holdings that exceed their target allocation and buying those below it
   c) Only ever buying, never selling
   d) Eliminating all bonds
3. True or False: A portfolio's allocation always stays exactly at its original target without any action from the investor.
4. Why did Sanjay's portfolio drift to 82% stocks despite no active decision on his part?
   a) He deliberately chose that allocation
   b) Stocks grew faster than bonds during that period, shifting the allocation naturally
   c) His bonds were sold automatically
   d) His original target was already 82% stocks

**5. Answer Key**
1. b  2. b  3. False  4. b

**6. Answers and Explanations**
1. Different asset classes' differing performance causes a portfolio's actual allocation to drift from its original target over time.
2. Rebalancing involves selling over-target holdings and buying under-target holdings to restore the intended allocation.
3. Without active rebalancing, allocation naturally drifts due to differing asset class performance.
4. Sanjay's allocation drifted purely due to differential stock and bond performance, not any active decision.

**7. Key Takeaways**
- Portfolio allocation naturally drifts over time as asset classes perform differently.
- Rebalancing restores a portfolio toward its original target allocation.
- Common approaches include calendar-based and threshold-based rebalancing.
- Rebalancing manages risk by preventing unintended allocation drift, independent of deliberate investor decisions.
