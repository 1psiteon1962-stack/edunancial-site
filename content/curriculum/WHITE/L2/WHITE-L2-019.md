## WHITE-L2-019
### Dollar-Cost Averaging: Investing Through Volatility

**1. Learning Objectives**
By the end of this lesson, the learner will be able to:
- Define dollar-cost averaging at a conceptual level.
- Explain how this strategy interacts with market volatility.
- Recognize the psychological and practical appeal of this approach.

**2. Core Content**
Dollar-cost averaging (DCA) involves investing a fixed dollar amount at regular intervals (such as monthly), regardless of the asset's price at each interval, rather than attempting to invest a lump sum all at once or trying to time purchases around perceived favorable price points. Because the fixed dollar amount buys more shares when prices are lower and fewer shares when prices are higher, this approach can result in a lower average cost per share over time compared to investing the same total amount at a single, potentially poorly timed point — though this benefit is not guaranteed in every scenario, particularly in a steadily rising market (addressed further in the next lesson).

Beyond the purely mathematical effect, DCA carries meaningful psychological and practical appeal: it removes the pressure of trying to identify the "perfect" time to invest (echoing the paradox of choice from Lesson 1), and it fits naturally with how many people actually receive money — through regular paychecks — making it a practical default approach for ongoing contributions, such as retirement account contributions from each paycheck.

**3. Worked Example**
Contributing a fixed amount to her retirement account from every paycheck throughout a volatile year, Layla ends up purchasing more shares during months when prices dipped and fewer shares during months when prices rose, without needing to make any active timing decisions herself. Reviewing her results at year-end, she finds her average cost per share came in lower than the year's average price level, a mathematical consequence of her consistent, unemotional purchasing pattern through the market's ups and downs — while also recognizing this outcome reflects that specific volatile year's pattern, not a guarantee for every future year.

**4. Practice Quiz**
1. What does dollar-cost averaging generally involve?
   a) Investing a lump sum all at once
   b) Investing a fixed dollar amount at regular intervals, regardless of price
   c) Only investing when prices are falling
   d) Avoiding investing entirely
2. Why can dollar-cost averaging result in a lower average cost per share, in some scenarios?
   a) It always guarantees the lowest possible price
   b) A fixed dollar amount buys more shares when prices are low and fewer when prices are high
   c) It has no relationship to price
   d) It only works when prices always rise
3. True or False: Dollar-cost averaging guarantees better results than a lump sum investment in every possible market scenario.
4. What practical appeal does DCA have beyond its mathematical effect?
   a) It requires large sums of money upfront
   b) It removes timing pressure and fits naturally with regular paycheck contributions
   c) It eliminates all investment risk
   d) It requires daily active trading decisions

**5. Answer Key**
1. b  2. b  3. False  4. b

**6. Answers and Explanations**
1. Dollar-cost averaging involves investing a fixed amount at regular intervals, regardless of price at each interval.
2. Buying more shares at lower prices and fewer at higher prices can lower average cost per share, though this is not guaranteed in every market pattern.
3. DCA's benefit relative to a lump sum depends on the specific price pattern that unfolds, not a guarantee in every scenario.
4. DCA removes timing pressure and aligns naturally with how many people receive and invest money through regular paychecks.

**7. Key Takeaways**
- Dollar-cost averaging invests a fixed amount at regular intervals, regardless of price.
- This can lower average cost per share in volatile or declining markets, though not guaranteed in every scenario.
- DCA removes timing pressure, addressing the paradox of choice discussed in Lesson 1.
- It fits naturally with regular income patterns, such as retirement contributions from each paycheck.
