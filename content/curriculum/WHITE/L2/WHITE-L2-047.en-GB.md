## WHITE-L2-047
### Common First-Time Investor Mistakes in Paper Assets

**1. Learning Objectives**
By the end of this lesson, the learner will be able to:
- Identify several common first-time investor mistakes in paper assets.
- Connect each mistake back to specific earlier lessons in this level.
- Reinforce this level's cumulative structure, echoing the RED-track mistakes lesson.

**2. Core Content**
Echoing RED-L2-048's format, this lesson reviews common first-time investor mistakes specific to paper assets, each connected to concepts already covered. Chasing recent performance (buying whatever has recently gone up, rather than following a coherent strategy) often stems from ignoring the philosophy/lens framework (Lessons 2–7) in favour of reactive decisions. Ignoring fees (Lessons 15, 42) often stems from focusing only on advertised returns without accounting for the compounding drag of costs. Panic-selling during downturns (Lesson 28, 45) often stems from inadequate advance planning and an honest risk-tolerance assessment (Lesson 26) never having been done. Inadequate diversification despite holding "many" individual stocks (Lessons 17, 24) often stems from not checking actual sector or asset-class concentration.

As in the RED track, a broader pattern underlies most of these: acting reactively in the moment rather than following a framework and plan established during a calmer, more rational period — precisely the discipline Lessons 43 and 45 were designed to build.

**3. Worked Example**
Reflecting on her own early investing mistakes, Priyanka (introduced in Lesson 1) recognises, years later, that she once chased a rapidly rising stock purely because of its recent performance, without applying any of the lens framework from Lessons 2–7, and sold it in a panic during a subsequent decline without ever having assessed her own actual risk tolerance in advance. Mapping these specific mistakes back to the concepts covered throughout this level, she recognises how a coherent framework — established from the very first lesson's discussion of narrowing choice — would have prevented both errors.

1. Chasing recent performance often reflects reactive decision-making without a coherent lens or philosophy framework.
2. Inadequate diversification often results from failing to check actual sector or asset-class concentration, despite a large number of holdings.

**7. Key Takeaways**
- Common paper-asset mistakes include chasing performance, ignoring fees, panic-selling, and inadequate diversification.
- Each mistake traces back to a specific concept already covered earlier in this level.
- This lesson echoes the RED-track mistakes lesson, reinforcing a consistent curriculum-wide structure.
