# WHITE-L3-026: Why Behavioural Biases Cost Investors More Than Fees Do

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
- Explain what the "behaviour gap" refers to in investing
- Explain why investor behaviour can cost more than fees, based on this lesson
- Preview the specific biases covered in the next two lessons

## Core Content

Lesson 22 showed how fees compound against returns. This lesson introduces a related, and in some analyses even larger, drag on real-world investor returns: behaviour itself.

The **behaviour gap** refers to the difference between the returns an investment (like a fund or the market as a whole) actually generates, and the returns the average investor in that same investment actually earns. [VERIFIED FACT] Various industry studies analysing this gap have found that average investor returns often lag the underlying investment's own returns, largely attributed to poorly timed buying and selling driven by emotional reactions to market movements — buying after a fund or market has already risen (performance chasing, Lesson 25) and selling after it has already fallen (echoing Lesson 1's core challenge).

Here's the "why" this matters more than a single fee comparison: a fund with a low expense ratio doesn't protect an investor from this behaviour gap — an investor who panic-sells during a downturn and buys back in after a recovery has already begun can underperform the fund's own reported returns by a wide margin, regardless of how low that fund's fees were.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] A broad market index fund reports a strong average annual return over a 20-year period. [ASSUMPTION] Studies of average investor behaviour in that same fund, however, might show investors as a group earning a meaningfully lower average return over the same period — not because the fund performed differently for different investors, but because many investors moved money in and out at unfavorable times, missing some of the fund's best days by being out of the market during recoveries and capturing more of its worst days by being invested during downturns.

## Practice Quiz

1. What does the "behaviour gap" refer to?
2. Why can a low-fee fund still result in a poor real-world outcome for an investor?
3. What two specific behaviours does this lesson identify as commonly driving the behaviour gap?

## Answer Key

1. The difference between an investment's own reported returns and the actual returns earned by the average investor in that investment.
2. Because the fund's low fees don't protect against poorly timed buying and selling driven by emotional reactions to market movements — behaviour, not fees, can be the larger drag on real-world results.
3. Buying after a fund or market has already risen (performance chasing) and selling after it has already fallen, driven by emotional reactions rather than a consistent plan.

## Answers and Explanations

1. This is the foundational definition for the lesson.
2. This is the central insight distinguishing the behaviour gap from the fee-based cost covered in Lesson 22.
3. This previews the specific biases explored in more depth in the next two lessons.

## Key Takeaways
- The behaviour gap describes the difference between an investment's actual returns and what the average investor in it actually earns.
- Studies have generally found this gap is often attributed to poorly timed buying and selling driven by emotional reactions.
- Low fees alone don't protect against this gap — investor behaviour can be an equally or more significant drag on real-world returns.
