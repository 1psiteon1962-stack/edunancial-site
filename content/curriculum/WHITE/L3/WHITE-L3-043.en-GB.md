# WHITE-L3-043: Why Market Orders and Limit Orders Serve Different Purposes

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
- Explain the difference between a market order and a limit order
- Explain why each order type prioritizes a different variable — speed versus price
- Connect this concept to the liquidity discussion from Lesson 42

## Core Content

A **market order** instructs a broker to buy or sell a security immediately at the best currently available price. A **limit order** instructs a broker to buy or sell only at a specified price or better, potentially waiting to execute (or not executing at all) if that price isn't available.

Here's the "why" the choice between them comes down to what you're prioritising: a market order prioritizes speed and certainty of execution — it will almost always fill quickly, but you don't know the exact price in advance, especially in a less liquid security (connecting directly to Lesson 42's bid-ask spread discussion) or during fast-moving conditions. A limit order prioritizes price control — you know the worst price you'll accept — but you give up certainty of execution, since the order might not fill at all if the market never reaches your specified price.

[VERIFIED FACT] For highly liquid securities with narrow bid-ask spreads, the practical difference between a market order and a reasonably placed limit order is often small; the distinction becomes more meaningful for less liquid securities, larger orders, or during periods of high volatility, where price uncertainty with a market order can be more significant.

## Worked Example

[ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE] An investor wants to buy shares of a thinly traded stock (as in Lesson 42's example). Using a market order guarantees the purchase happens quickly, but the wider bid-ask spread means she might pay a noticeably higher price than the last traded price suggested. Using a limit order instead, set at a specific maximum price she's willing to pay, protects her from an unexpectedly high execution price — but if the stock's price moves away from her limit before enough shares are available at that price, her order might not fill at all, and she could miss the opportunity to buy.

2. It prioritizes speed and certainty of execution, giving up certainty about the exact execution price.
3. Less liquid securities, larger order sizes, or periods of high volatility — situations where price uncertainty with a market order becomes more significant, connecting to the liquidity discussion in Lesson 42.

## Key Takeaways
- A market order prioritizes speed and certainty of execution over price certainty.
- A limit order prioritizes price control but risks not executing at all.
- This trade-off becomes especially meaningful for less liquid securities, large orders, or volatile market conditions.
