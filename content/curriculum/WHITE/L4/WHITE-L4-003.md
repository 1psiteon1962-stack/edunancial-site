# Bond Duration Analysis: Why Interest Rate Risk Isn't Equal Across Bonds

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a central bank raising its benchmark interest rate sharply and repeatedly over a short stretch of months to fight rising inflation. Bond investors across the market do not all feel the pain equally. This lesson explains why duration, not just how long until a bond matures, determines how hard any given bond gets hit.

## Learning Objectives
- Explain what bond duration measures.
- Explain why longer-duration bonds are more sensitive to interest rate changes.
- Distinguish between maturity and duration.
- Apply duration thinking to compare interest rate risk across two bonds.

## Core Content
Bond duration measures a bond's price sensitivity to changes in interest rates, expressed in years, but it is not simply the bond's time to maturity. VERIFIED FACT: duration accounts for the timing and size of all a bond's cash flows (coupon payments plus principal repayment), not just when the final payment arrives, which is why two bonds with the same maturity date can have different durations if their coupon rates differ.

Here is the "why" this matters when rates rise sharply, as in the scenario above: a bond with a higher duration will see its price fall more sharply for a given interest rate increase than a bond with a lower duration, because more of that bond's value is tied up in cash flows received further in the future, and those distant cash flows lose relatively more present value when the discount rate (interest rates) rises. This is the fixed-income mirror of the cap-rate-and-interest-rate relationship covered in real estate: as rates rise, the "price" of a fixed future income stream falls, and the effect is larger the further out that income stream extends.

This is why an investor worried about rising rates should pay attention to a bond fund's or bond's specific duration, not just whether it is labeled "short-term" or "long-term," and why a zero-coupon bond (all cash flow received at maturity, no interim coupons) has a duration equal to its full maturity — the maximum possible duration for a bond of that maturity — making it especially sensitive to rate changes.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: Bond A has a duration of 3 years; Bond B has a duration of 10 years. If interest rates rise by 1 percentage point, Bond A's price falls by roughly 3% while Bond B's price falls by roughly 10% — a rough approximation, since duration estimates the percentage price change for a given change in rates. An investor expecting rates to keep rising, per the scenario's rate-hiking cycle, would generally prefer holding Bond A's shorter duration to limit price declines, even though both bonds might carry similar credit quality.

**Q5. Why does a zero-coupon bond have the maximum possible duration for its maturity?**

D) Duration does not apply to zero-coupon bonds

## Answer Key
Q1: B
Q2: B
Q3: B
Q4: C
Q5: A

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** Duration captures how much a bond's price will move for a given change in interest rates, based on the full pattern of its cash flows.

**Q2.** Coupon size shifts how much value is received earlier versus later, which changes duration independent of the final maturity date.

**Q3.** Distant cash flows are discounted more heavily when rates rise, making longer-duration bonds more price-sensitive to rate changes.

**Q4.** Duration approximates the percentage price change for a 1 percentage point change in rates, so a duration of 7 suggests roughly a 7% price decline.

**Q5.** With no interim coupon payments, all value depends on the single final payment, making zero-coupon bonds maximally sensitive to rate changes for their maturity.

## Key Takeaways
- Bond duration measures price sensitivity to interest rate changes, factoring in the timing and size of all cash flows, not just maturity.
- Higher-duration bonds experience larger price swings for a given change in interest rates than lower-duration bonds.
- Two bonds with identical maturities can have different durations if their coupon structures differ.
- Zero-coupon bonds carry the maximum duration for their maturity, making them especially sensitive to rate changes.
