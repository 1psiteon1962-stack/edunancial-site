# Interest Rate Sensitivity of Bonds vs Stocks: Why They React Differently

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a central bank raising its benchmark interest rate sharply and repeatedly over a short stretch of months to fight rising inflation. When a central bank raises rates aggressively, bond prices and stock prices do not always move in the same direction or by the same magnitude. This lesson unpacks the different mechanisms at work in each market.

## Learning Objectives
- Explain how rising interest rates directly affect bond prices.
- Explain how rising interest rates affect stock valuations through the discount rate mechanism.
- Explain why growth stocks are typically more rate-sensitive than value or dividend-paying stocks.
- Apply this understanding to anticipate how a rate-hiking cycle might affect a mixed portfolio.

## Core Content
Recall from Lesson 3 that bond prices move inversely to interest rates through the duration mechanism — existing fixed-rate bonds become less attractive, and therefore fall in price, when new bonds are issued at higher rates. VERIFIED FACT: this is a direct, mechanical relationship, since a bond's fixed coupon payments are worth less relative to new, higher-yielding alternatives.

Here is the "why" stocks respond to rate changes through a different, less direct mechanism: stock valuations are often modeled as the present value of a company's expected future cash flows, discounted back to today using a discount rate that rises and falls with prevailing interest rates. When rates rise, as in the scenario above, the present value of those future cash flows falls, all else equal — and this effect is larger for companies whose value depends heavily on cash flows expected far in the future (many growth companies) than for companies generating substantial cash flow today (many value and dividend-paying companies).

This is why growth stocks, similar to long-duration bonds from Lesson 3, tend to be more sensitive to rising rates than value stocks — the mechanism is conceptually parallel (more of the value sits further out in time, so it is discounted more heavily), even though the underlying assets (equity ownership versus fixed debt claims) are fundamentally different. This is why a rate-hiking cycle can pressure both bonds and growth stocks simultaneously, even though the specific mechanism differs between the two asset classes.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: During a rate-hiking cycle, a long-duration government bond fund falls 12% due to the direct discounting mechanism from Lesson 3, while a growth-stock-heavy index falls 18% over the same period as investors apply a higher discount rate to companies whose profits are expected mostly in future years, and a value-stock-heavy index, generating substantial current cash flow, falls only 4% over the same period — illustrating how the same rate-hiking event affects different asset types through related but distinct mechanisms and to different degrees.

Q2: A
Q3: B

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** The fixed coupon on existing bonds becomes comparatively less attractive as new bonds offer higher yields, pushing existing bond prices down.

**Q2.** Higher discount rates reduce the present value of future cash flows, which is a core driver of most stock valuation models.

**Q3.** Similar to long-duration bonds, growth stocks' value is concentrated further in the future, making them more sensitive to changes in the discount rate.

**Q4.** The differing sensitivity stemmed from how much of each index's value depended on future, versus current, cash flows.

**Q5.** Though the specific mechanisms differ, both bonds and growth stocks are affected by the broader principle that rising discount rates reduce the present value of future cash flows.

## Key Takeaways
- Bond prices fall as rates rise because existing fixed coupons become less attractive relative to new, higher-yielding bonds.
- Stock valuations are pressured by rising rates through a higher discount rate applied to expected future cash flows.
- Growth stocks tend to be more rate-sensitive than value stocks because more of their value sits further in the future.
- A rate-hiking cycle can pressure both bonds and growth stocks simultaneously through related but distinct discounting mechanisms.
