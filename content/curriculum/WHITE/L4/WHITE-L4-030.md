# Market Capitalization Analysis: Why Company Size Changes Risk and Return Expectations

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a widespread public health disruption halting global shipping and manufacturing for an extended stretch, snarling supply chains across many industries at once. As a widespread disruption halts global shipping and manufacturing, small, less-established companies and large, well-resourced companies do not weather the disruption equally. This lesson examines why company size itself has historically been associated with distinct risk and return patterns.

## Learning Objectives
- Explain what market capitalization measures.
- Distinguish between small-cap, mid-cap, and large-cap companies.
- Explain why small-cap companies have historically exhibited higher volatility and, over some long periods, a size premium in average returns.
- Explain why smaller companies were more vulnerable during the disruption described in the scenario.

## Core Content
Market capitalization ("market cap") equals a company's current share price multiplied by its total number of outstanding shares, representing the total market value of the company's equity. VERIFIED FACT: companies are commonly grouped into size categories — small-cap, mid-cap, and large-cap — based on market capitalization thresholds that are widely used, though the exact dollar boundaries can vary somewhat between different classification systems and change over time as overall market values grow.

Here is the "why" company size has historically been associated with distinct risk and return characteristics: smaller companies generally have less access to capital, less diversified revenue streams (often concentrated in fewer products, customers, or markets), thinner cash reserves, and less established competitive positions than large-cap companies — all of which tend to make them more vulnerable during periods of broad economic stress, such as the supply chain disruption described in the scenario above, which can hit smaller companies with less financial cushion much harder than large, well-capitalized competitors.

This is why small-cap stocks have historically exhibited higher volatility than large-cap stocks, and some long-term academic research has identified a historical "size premium" — a tendency, over some long periods, for small-cap stocks to deliver higher average returns than large-cap stocks, theoretically compensating investors for this additional risk. OPINION and important caveat: this historical size premium has not been consistent across all time periods, and some researchers have debated its persistence and magnitude in more recent decades, so it should be treated as one historically observed, imperfectly reliable pattern among the various stock characteristics discussed in this level, not a guaranteed feature of small-cap investing going forward.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: During a severe global supply chain disruption, a large, well-capitalized company with diversified suppliers across multiple regions and substantial cash reserves weathers the disruption with only a modest, temporary earnings impact. A smaller competitor in the same industry, reliant on a single supplier and carrying thinner cash reserves, struggles to source materials, burns through its limited cash cushion, and sees its stock price fall far more sharply and take much longer to recover — illustrating the greater vulnerability smaller companies can face during broad periods of economic stress.

## Key Takeaways
- Market capitalization measures a company's total equity value, and it is commonly used to sort companies into size categories.
- Smaller companies generally have less access to capital, less diversified revenue, and thinner cash reserves than large-cap companies.
- This has historically been associated with higher small-cap volatility and, in some periods, a size premium in average returns.
- The size premium is a historically observed, imperfectly reliable pattern, not a guaranteed feature of small-cap investing.
