# Currency Risk Analysis: Why International Investing Adds a Second Variable

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a mid-sized economy's currency losing a large share of its value against major reserve currencies over a few months, driven by capital fleeing the country and eroding investor confidence. An investor holding shares of a strong, well-performing company based in a country whose currency is losing significant value discovers that their investment return, measured in their own home currency, tells a very different story than the company's local stock performance alone. This lesson explains why.

## Learning Objectives
- Explain how currency exchange rate movements affect the return on an international investment.
- Distinguish between local currency return and home currency (investor's own currency) return.
- Explain why a strong local investment can still produce a poor return for a foreign investor.
- Explain the basic concept and tradeoff of currency hedging.

## Core Content
When an investor based in one country purchases an asset denominated in a different country's currency, their total return depends on two separate factors: the asset's performance in its local currency, and the change in the exchange rate between that local currency and the investor's home currency over the holding period. VERIFIED FACT: these two factors combine (approximately multiplicatively) to determine the investor's actual return measured in their own home currency, meaning a gain in one factor can be partially or fully offset, or amplified, by a movement in the other.

Here is the "why" this creates a genuine second variable, distinct from anything covered in the domestic-focused lessons earlier in this level: an investor could correctly identify a strong, well-performing foreign company (getting the underlying investment analysis right) and still experience a disappointing or even negative return in their own home currency if that country's currency loses significant value against the investor's home currency during the same period, as in the scenario above — the local stock gain gets diluted or entirely offset by the currency's depreciation once converted back to the investor's home currency.

This is why some international investment products offer currency hedging — using financial instruments to offset or reduce exposure to exchange rate movements, isolating the investor's return closer to the underlying asset's local currency performance. This comes with a real tradeoff, however: hedging has a cost (reducing net returns to pay for the hedge) and eliminates the currency risk in both directions, meaning a hedged investor also gives up any potential benefit if the foreign currency instead strengthens against their home currency during the holding period. Whether to hedge currency exposure is a deliberate decision requiring judgment about currency outlook and risk tolerance, not a universally correct choice in either direction.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: A U.S.-based investor buys shares of a company based in a country whose currency subsequently loses 15% of its value against the U.S. dollar over the holding period. The stock itself rises 20% in its local currency terms — a strong result by local standards. But once converted back to U.S. dollars, the investor's actual return is closer to 20% minus roughly 15%, or approximately 5% (an approximation, since the two factors combine multiplicatively rather than through simple subtraction) — a far more modest outcome than the local 20% gain would suggest, illustrating how currency movement can significantly erode an otherwise strong underlying investment result.

## Practice Quiz
**Q1. What two factors combine to determine an investor's total return on a foreign investment, measured in their own home currency?**
A) The asset's local currency performance and the domestic stock market's overall performance
B) The asset's local currency performance and the change in the exchange rate between the local currency and the investor's home currency
C) Only the exchange rate movement, with no relevance from the asset's own performance
D) The investor's personal tax bracket and the asset's dividend yield

**Q2. Why can a strong local stock performance still produce a poor return for a foreign investor?**
A) Local stock performance is the only factor that determines a foreign investor's return
B) If the local currency depreciates significantly against the investor's home currency during the same period, that depreciation can offset or dilute the local gain once converted back
C) Currency movements have no effect on international investment returns
D) Foreign investors are always guaranteed the same return as local investors

**Q3. What does currency hedging attempt to do?**
A) Guarantee a fixed, unusually high return regardless of any market conditions
B) Use financial instruments to offset or reduce exposure to exchange rate movements, isolating returns closer to the underlying asset's local currency performance
C) Eliminate all risk associated with the underlying stock investment itself
D) Increase an investor's exposure to currency fluctuations

**Q4. What is the main tradeoff of using currency hedging?**
A) There is no tradeoff; hedging is always a purely beneficial strategy with no downside
B) Hedging has a cost that reduces net returns, and it also eliminates any potential benefit if the foreign currency instead strengthens against the investor's home currency
C) Hedging always guarantees a higher return than not hedging
D) Currency hedging is illegal in most international investment products

**Q5. In the example, approximately what was the investor's actual U.S. dollar return given a 20% local gain and a 15% currency depreciation?**
A) Approximately 35%
B) Approximately 20%
C) Approximately 5%
D) Approximately -15%

## Answer Key
Q1: B
Q2: B
Q3: B
Q4: B
Q5: C

## Answers and Explanations
**Q1.** Both the underlying local performance and the currency conversion effect jointly determine the final return in the investor's home currency.

**Q2.** The currency conversion effect can substantially reduce, or even negate, a strong result achieved in the underlying local market.

**Q3.** Hedging specifically targets the currency component of international investment risk, aiming to reduce its influence on the investor's overall return.

**Q4.** Because hedging removes currency risk in both directions, it also forgoes any potential upside from favorable currency movement, in addition to its explicit cost.

**Q5.** The local gain and currency depreciation approximately offset in combination, leaving a much more modest home-currency return of roughly 5%.

## Key Takeaways
- An international investor's total return depends on both the asset's local currency performance and the exchange rate movement.
- A strong local investment can still produce a disappointing home-currency return if the local currency depreciates significantly.
- Currency hedging can reduce exposure to exchange rate movements but comes at a cost and forgoes potential upside from favorable currency shifts.
- Whether to hedge currency exposure is a deliberate decision based on currency outlook and risk tolerance, not a universally correct choice.
