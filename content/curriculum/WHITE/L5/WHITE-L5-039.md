# Family Investment Committee Decision Rights: Orchestrating Governance Clarity Across Family Members

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a mid-sized sovereign government signaling it may be unable to make an upcoming bond payment, raising default risk on its debt. As a mid-sized sovereign government's debt troubles create genuine market turbulence requiring fast investment decisions, a family managing a shared paper asset portfolio with genuinely unclear decision rights among its members discovers this ambiguity produces exactly the kind of costly confusion a comparable, clearly governed family committee would have avoided.

## Learning Objectives
- Extend this curriculum's earlier RED-L5 Lesson 39 multi-investor decision rights discussion directly to a family investment committee overseeing a shared paper asset portfolio.
- Explain why a family investment committee should specifically tier decision authority — distinguishing routine rebalancing or reinvestment decisions from major strategic allocation changes — rather than requiring unanimous consent for every decision.
- Explain why requiring unanimous consent for every decision can itself become a genuine liability during a period requiring fast decision-making, mirroring the earlier discussion's parallel finding.
- Explain why this decision rights structure should be established explicitly, well before any specific fast-decision scenario actually arises, echoing this curriculum's broader organizational readiness discussion.

## Core Content
Extending this curriculum's earlier RED-L5 Lesson 39 multi-investor decision rights discussion directly to a family investment committee overseeing a shared paper asset portfolio, VERIFIED FACT, this same general governance principle — requiring a considerably more explicit, carefully tiered decision rights structure as the number of individual family members involved genuinely grows — applies with equal force to this paper asset context, since a genuinely larger number of individual family members introduces considerably more potential for genuine disagreement and considerably more potential points of ambiguity regarding who actually holds authority for any specific kind of investment decision.

Here is the "why" a family investment committee should specifically tier decision authority: precisely mirroring the earlier multi-investor real estate discussion's finding, a genuinely sound family investment governance structure generally distinguishes between routine decisions (such as ordinary rebalancing or reinvesting dividend income, which might reasonably be delegated to a specific designated family member or a simple majority) and major, strategic decisions (such as a significant change to the overall target allocation, which might reasonably require a higher voting threshold given their genuinely greater significance), meaning this kind of tiered structure allows routine decisions to proceed efficiently while still providing appropriately elevated protection for genuinely more significant decisions. This is why requiring unanimous consent for every decision can itself become a genuine liability during a period requiring fast decision-making, mirroring the earlier discussion's parallel finding: a uniformly high consent requirement means even a single dissenting or hard-to-reach family member can completely block an otherwise urgently needed decision, meaning this kind of uniformly high requirement, applied indiscriminately regardless of a specific decision's actual significance, can become a genuine liability precisely during the kind of fast-moving, genuinely urgent situation this lesson's opening scenario describes, when an efficient, timely decision actually matters most. This is why this decision rights structure should be established explicitly, well before any specific fast-decision scenario actually arises, echoing this curriculum's broader organizational readiness discussion: precisely echoing the earlier discussion's finding regarding organizational readiness needing to be built in advance rather than assembled reactively, a family investment committee's tiered decision rights structure should be explicitly established, ideally in a written family investment policy statement, from the committee's own outset, rather than family members attempting to negotiate this same governance structure for the first time only once an actual, urgent decision scenario has already arisen.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: A family managing a shared paper asset portfolio explicitly establishes, within a written family investment policy statement, a tiered decision rights structure delegating routine rebalancing and reinvestment decisions to a specific designated family member, while requiring a supermajority vote among family members for major strategic allocation changes. When genuine market turbulence subsequently requires a fast decision, this explicit, previously established tiered structure allows the family to actually respond quickly through the appropriate decision-making tier, rather than discovering, only at this genuinely urgent moment, that its governance had never actually been made explicit.
