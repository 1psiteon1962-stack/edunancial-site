KEYWORDS: integrated marketing strategy, sales and finance, marketing orchestration, ORANGE Level 5
# Marketing at Level 5: Every Sales Decision Moves the Whole Board

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a central bank raising its benchmark interest rate sharply and repeatedly over a short stretch of months to fight rising inflation.

Apply it here: When interest rates rise sharply, customers borrow less and buy more carefully. Ask across colours: Should the company cut prices (ORANGE) or protect margins? How does a price cut affect cash flow and loan covenants (BLUE)? Does offering customer financing create legal duties (PURPLE)? A single marketing move touches all of them.

## Learning Objectives
- Explain why ORANGE decisions at Level 5 affect every other colour
- Identify the non-marketing consequences of a marketing decision
- Apply a cross-colour checklist before major sales moves

## Core Content
In Levels 1 through 4, ORANGE taught the pieces of sales and marketing one at a time: understanding customers, positioning, pricing, channels, negotiation, persuasion, and competitive strategy. At Level 4, you analysed why some strategies win and others fail, and learned the tactical, offence-versus-defence side of competition.

Level 5 asks a harder question: what happens to the rest of the business when you make a marketing move?

Think of the chess analogy. Early on, you learned how each piece moves. Now you coordinate them toward a goal. ORANGE is often the piece that starts the action. It brings in revenue, which every other colour depends on. But every ORANGE move pulls on the other pieces:
- BLUE: A new campaign costs cash before it earns cash. A large new customer may need credit terms that stretch working capital.
- GREEN: Discounts, rebates, and gifts can have tax effects. Selling into new regions can create new tax obligations. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE.
- PURPLE: Marketing claims must be truthful. Contracts with customers and distributors create obligations. Customer data must be handled lawfully.
- WHITE: Customer concentration and revenue quality affect how investors value the company.
- BLACK: Sales goals and compensation plans shape behaviour and culture. For UK readers: VAT-registered businesses must charge VAT on most sales, registration becomes compulsory once turnover passes the threshold, and selling to customers in the EU since Brexit can create separate VAT obligations there, so check with an accountant.

A Level 5 cross-colour checklist before any major sales move:
1. Cash: When does the money go out, and when does it come back?
2. Margin: After discounts, commissions, and taxes, what does the company actually keep?
3. Legal: What are we promising, and can we deliver it?
4. Risk: Does this make us too dependent on one customer, channel, or product?
5. People: What behaviour does this reward in our team?

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Many businesses fail not because they cannot sell, but because they sell in ways the rest of the business cannot support. Growth that outruns cash, margin, or legal capacity can be as dangerous as no growth at all.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: Grace runs a furniture store. Rates rise and sales slow. Her marketing manager proposes "0% financing for 24 months" to boost sales.

Grace runs the cross-colour checklist:
- Cash (BLUE): If the store finances purchases itself, it waits up to 24 months for full payment while paying suppliers now. Cash would run short within months.
- Margin (BLUE/GREEN): If a third-party lender provides the financing, the store pays the lender a fee of several per cent of each sale, cutting margin.
- Legal (PURPLE): Offering consumer credit may trigger lending disclosure rules. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE.
- Risk: Some customers may default, especially if the economy weakens.
- People (BLACK): Salespeople paid only on sales volume may push financing to customers who cannot afford it.

Grace's decision: Partner with a licensed third-party lender to offer 12-month financing, raise prices slightly on financed items to cover the fee, and pay commissions on paid sales rather than signed sales.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: The campaign still boosts sales, but in a way her cash flow, margins, and legal obligations can support.

===== ORANGE-L5-002
TITLE: Pricing Orchestrated with Cash Flow and Tax
SUMMARY: See how pricing decisions affect cash flow timing, profit margins, tax obligations, and customer perception, and how to set prices that work for the whole business.
KEYWORDS: pricing strategy cash flow, pricing and taxes, margin analysis, price setting business, contribution margin
# Pricing Orchestrated with Cash Flow and Tax

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a central bank raising its benchmark interest rate sharply and repeatedly over a short stretch of months to fight rising inflation.

Apply it here: As rates rise, a service business considers switching from monthly billing to annual prepaid plans at a discount. Ask across colours: How does this change cash flow (BLUE)? How and when is prepaid revenue taxed and recognised (GREEN)? What must the contract say about refunds (PURPLE)? Will customers see this as a good deal (ORANGE)?

## Learning Objectives
- Explain how pricing structure affects cash flow timing
- Connect pricing to tax and accounting treatment
- Design pricing that balances customer appeal and business health

## Core Content
At Level 3 and Level 4, ORANGE covered pricing strategies: cost-plus, value-based, competitive, and psychological pricing. At Level 5, pricing becomes an orchestration tool.

The same price can produce very different business results depending on its structure:
- Upfront vs. over time: Annual prepaid plans bring cash in early. Monthly plans spread it out. Payment on completion delays it.
- Deposits and milestones: Asking for a deposit or milestone payments reduces the risk of doing work and not being paid.
- Discounts for early payment: A small discount for paying within 10 days may cost less than borrowing to cover slow payers.
- Bundling: Selling products together can raise average order value but may hide which products are actually profitable.

How colours connect:
- BLUE: Cash timing affects how much the business must borrow. With high rates, getting paid earlier has real value.
- GREEN: REGION-SPECIFIC RULE: Prepaid revenue may be taxed and recognised differently from revenue earned over time. Some systems let businesses defer recognition of prepaid amounts until services are delivered; others do not. Sales taxes or VAT may apply at the time of payment.
- PURPLE: Prepaid plans need clear refund and cancellation terms. Consumer protection rules may limit automatic renewals. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE.
- WHITE: Predictable pricing models (like subscriptions) often support higher business valuations. For UK readers: VAT is usually due when an advance payment is received rather than when the service is delivered, while Corporation Tax generally follows accounts prepared under UK accounting standards, which recognise prepaid income as it is earned, so check the timing with an accountant.

The contribution margin concept helps here: price minus the variable costs of delivering one more unit. A price that looks profitable may not be after commissions, payment processing fees, discounts, and taxes on the sale.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Many small businesses set prices by looking at competitors and then wonder why they are always short on cash. Designing price structure around cash flow can matter as much as the price itself.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: Leila runs a bookkeeping service. She charges £300 per month, billed monthly. With rates rising, her credit line costs more.

She offers an annual plan: £3,240 paid upfront (a 10% discount from £3,600).

Analysis:
- Cash (BLUE): If 40 of her 100 clients switch, she receives £129,600 upfront instead of £12,000 per month from those clients. She pays down her credit line and saves interest.
- Margin: She gives up £360 per client per year, or £14,400 total. Her interest savings and reduced collection effort offset part of this.
- Tax (GREEN): Her CPA explains how prepaid income is treated in her jurisdiction and when it must be reported. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE. She sets aside cash for any tax due.
- Legal (PURPLE): Her new contract states a prorated refund policy if a client cancels.
- Customer appeal (ORANGE): Clients like the discount and the simplicity.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: The annual plan is not just a marketing offer. It is a financing decision, a tax decision, and a legal decision all at once. Leila made it work by checking all of them.

===== ORANGE-L5-003
TITLE: Customer Acquisition Cost, Lifetime Value, and Financing
SUMMARY: Learn how to compare the cost of acquiring customers with their lifetime value, and how financing, payback periods, and churn decide whether growth is sustainable.
KEYWORDS: customer acquisition cost, customer lifetime value, CAC LTV ratio, payback period, marketing ROI
# Customer Acquisition Cost, Lifetime Value, and Financing

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a systemically important financial institution suddenly revealing a large, previously undisclosed loss, raising fears about counterparty risk rippling through the broader banking system.

Apply it here: After a major bank's losses shake confidence, investors stop funding fast-growing startups that spend heavily on marketing. Ask across colours: How long does it take this company to earn back what it spends to win a customer (ORANGE/BLUE)? Can it survive without new investment (WHITE)? What happens if customers leave faster than expected?

## Learning Objectives
- Calculate customer acquisition cost and lifetime value
- Explain payback period and why it matters for cash
- Connect marketing spending to financing risk

## Core Content
Two numbers help decide whether marketing spending builds a business or drains it:

- Customer Acquisition Cost (CAC): Total sales and marketing spending in a period divided by the number of new customers gained.
- Customer Lifetime Value (LTV): The total gross profit a customer is expected to generate over the time they stay. A simple version: average monthly gross profit per customer x average number of months a customer stays.

ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE of the ratio: If CAC is £200 and LTV is £800, the LTV:CAC ratio is 4:1. Many businesses consider a ratio well above 1:1 necessary, though the right target depends on the industry. ASSUMPTION/OPINION.

But the ratio alone is not enough. Payback period matters too: how many months until a customer's gross profit repays their CAC. A business with a great LTV:CAC ratio but a 24-month payback needs a lot of cash to grow.

How colours connect:
- BLUE: Every new customer costs cash upfront. Faster growth means more cash out before cash comes back. Businesses often finance this gap with investor money or loans.
- WHITE: When investors pull back, as in the scenario, businesses that depend on outside money to cover long payback periods are at risk.
- GREEN: REGION-SPECIFIC RULE: Marketing costs are often deductible when incurred, which can help cash flow if the business is profitable.
- PURPLE: Subscription terms, cancellation rules, and auto-renewal laws affect how long customers actually stay.
- BLACK: Leaders must decide how fast to grow based on available cash, not just opportunity. For UK readers: marketing and advertising costs are generally deductible for Corporation Tax or Income Tax if they are incurred wholly and exclusively for the trade, although client entertaining is not, so check with an accountant.

Churn (the rate at which customers leave) is the hidden variable. If churn rises from 3% to 6% per month, average customer life can fall by about half, cutting LTV sharply.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: In tight funding markets, businesses with shorter payback periods and lower churn tend to survive. Growth that depends on constant new investment is fragile.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: Two meal-kit companies each spend £100,000 per month on marketing.

Company A: CAC = £150. Monthly gross profit per customer = £25. Average customer stays 12 months. LTV = £300. Payback period = 6 months. LTV:CAC = 2:1.

Company B: CAC = £150. Monthly gross profit per customer = £15. Average customer stays 30 months. LTV = £450. Payback period = 10 months. LTV:CAC = 3:1.

Company B looks better on the ratio. But when investors stop funding growth, Company B needs 10 months to recover each customer's cost, and its customers stay long only if churn stays low. Company A recovers costs in 6 months.

Then churn rises for both. Company B's average customer life falls from 30 months to 15, cutting LTV to £225 (1.5:1). Company A's falls from 12 to 8 months, cutting LTV to £200 (1.3:1), but it still pays back its CAC within 6 months.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Company A reduces marketing spending and survives on its own cash. Company B must raise money on poor terms. Payback period and churn mattered as much as the headline ratio.

# Marketing Real Estate: Positioning, Pricing, and Timing

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a populous nation's real estate and consumer credit markets, after years of rapid expansion, beginning to show signs of a sharp correction as defaults start rising.

Apply it here: A housing market begins to correct. An investor must decide whether to sell a property now or lease it and wait. Ask across colours: How should the property be priced and positioned (ORANGE)? How are buyers' financing conditions affecting demand (BLUE/RED)? What disclosures are required (PURPLE)? What are the tax consequences of selling now versus later (GREEN)?

## Learning Objectives
- Apply positioning and pricing to real estate sales and leasing
- Connect marketing timing to financing and tax conditions
- Include legal disclosure requirements in property marketing

## Core Content
RED teaches how to evaluate and finance property. ORANGE teaches how to sell or lease it effectively. At Level 5, they work together.

Key marketing decisions for property:
- Target buyer or tenant: First-time buyers, investors, families, businesses? Each responds to different features and messages.
- Positioning: What makes this property the best choice for that target? Location, condition, income potential, flexibility?
- Pricing: Based on comparable sales or rents (RED's valuation tools), current demand, and how quickly you need to sell. Overpricing in a falling market often leads to a longer sale and a lower final price.
- Timing: Seasonal patterns, interest rate trends, and local supply all affect demand.
- Presentation: Photos, descriptions, showings, and repairs before listing.

How colours connect:
- RED: Valuation, condition, and income potential.
- BLUE: Buyers' borrowing costs shape what they can afford. When rates rise, some buyers drop out, and others need seller concessions such as help with closing costs or seller financing.
- PURPLE: Many jurisdictions require sellers to disclose known defects. Marketing descriptions must not be misleading. Fair housing rules limit how properties can be advertised. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE.
- GREEN: REGION-SPECIFIC RULE: Holding period, depreciation recapture, and exchange rules may affect whether selling now or later is better after taxes.
- WHITE: Compare expected after-tax proceeds from selling with the expected return from holding and leasing. For UK readers: estate agents must give buyers material information about a property under consumer protection law, and the Equality Act 2010 prohibits discrimination when letting or selling homes, so check advertising wording with a solicitor or Trading Standards guidance.

Sell vs. lease: In a correcting market, leasing may produce income while waiting for recovery, but it brings management work, vacancy risk, and possible landlord obligations (PURPLE-L5-018 and -027 in the law track).

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: The biggest real estate marketing mistake in a falling market is anchoring on last year's price. Buyers price on today's conditions.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: Dmitri owns a townhouse he bought as an investment. The market is correcting, and rates are high.

Option 1: Sell now. Comparable sales suggest £420,000, down from £460,000 a year ago. Dmitri is tempted to list at £460,000.
Option 2: Lease and wait. Market rent is £2,300 per month.

His analysis:
- Pricing (ORANGE/RED): Listing at £460,000 would likely sit unsold. He lists at £425,000 with a clear marketing message targeting first-time buyers: move-in ready, near transit.
- Financing (BLUE): To help buyers facing high rates, he offers a closing cost credit instead of a price cut, which keeps his sale price stronger for future comparables.
- Legal (PURPLE): He discloses a past roof repair as required. His listing avoids any language that could violate fair housing rules.
- Tax (GREEN): His CPA confirms he has held long enough for favourable treatment and estimates depreciation recapture. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE.
- Compare (WHITE): After-tax sale proceeds invested elsewhere vs. holding with rental income. The numbers are close.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Dmitri sells for £418,000 after concessions within 40 days. Pricing to the current market, not his hopes, made the sale happen.

TITLE: Raising Capital Is a Sales Process
SUMMARY: Learn how pitching investors uses sales skills—research, positioning, storytelling, handling objections—while staying within securities rules and giving an honest picture of risk.

# Raising Capital Is a Sales Process

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a systemically important financial institution suddenly revealing a large, previously undisclosed loss, raising fears about counterparty risk rippling through the broader banking system.

Apply it here: After a banking scare, investors become cautious and ask harder questions. A founder must raise money to survive. Ask across colours: How should the pitch be positioned for nervous investors (ORANGE)? What claims can legally be made (PURPLE)? What terms protect the founder's control (BLUE)? How should risks be presented honestly (BLACK)?

## Learning Objectives
- Apply the sales process to fundraising
- Position a company for specific investor types
- Balance persuasion with legal and ethical honesty

## Core Content
Raising money is selling. The product is a share of the company's future. The customer is the investor. Many sales skills from earlier ORANGE levels apply directly:

1. Prospecting: Find investors whose interests match your company. Some focus on certain industries, stages, or regions. Pitching the wrong investors wastes time.
2. Research: Understand what each investor cares about: growth, income, impact, control, or exit timing.
3. Positioning: Explain why your company is the right investment for them, now.
4. Storytelling: Show the problem, your solution, your traction, your team, and how the investment will be used.
5. Handling objections: Prepare for hard questions about risks, competition, and assumptions.
6. Closing: Move to term sheets, due diligence, and documents.

How colours connect:
- PURPLE: Securities laws apply (PURPLE-L5-033 in the law track). Claims must be truthful. Projections should be clearly labelled as estimates with stated assumptions. Public advertising of an offering may be restricted. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE.
- BLUE: The terms matter as much as the amount: valuation, dilution, control rights, and investor protections.
- WHITE: Investors compare your company with other opportunities, including public markets. In cautious times, they want clearer paths to profit and lower risk.
- BLACK: Credibility is the founder's most important asset. Overpromising may win one round but damage every future relationship.
- GREEN: REGION-SPECIFIC RULE: Some jurisdictions offer tax incentives for investors in qualifying small businesses, which can be a genuine selling point. For UK readers: inviting people to invest is a financial promotion that generally must be made or approved by a firm authorised by the Financial Conduct Authority unless an exemption applies, so take advice before you publicise an offering.

In cautious markets, positioning often shifts from "fastest growth" to "efficient growth with a path to profitability."

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: The best investor pitches are honest about risks and explain how the team will handle them. Experienced investors trust founders who show they understand what could go wrong.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: Amara's company makes software for small clinics. She needs £1 million. After the banking scare, investors are nervous.

Her original pitch emphasised rapid growth: "We'll triple revenue next year!"

Her revised pitch:
- Prospecting: She targets investors who focus on healthcare software and have invested during downturns before.
- Positioning: "Efficient growth." She shows a 7-month customer payback period (see ORANGE-L5-003) and low churn.
- Story: Clinics save staff time, which matters more when labour costs rise.
- Honest risks: She names the two biggest risks (a large competitor and regulatory changes) and explains her plans for each.
- Projections: Labeled as estimates, with three scenarios (cautious, base, strong) and stated assumptions.
- Terms: She seeks a fair valuation rather than the highest possible, to avoid future down-round problems (see PURPLE-L5-007).

Two investors who passed on her growth pitch invest after the revised version. ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Her honesty about risk increased investor confidence rather than reducing it.

===== ORANGE-L5-006
TITLE: Negotiating a Business Acquisition with the Whole Board in View
SUMMARY: Use BATNA, anchoring, and trade-offs to negotiate a business purchase while coordinating price with financing, legal protections, tax structure, and post-closing leadership.
KEYWORDS: negotiating business acquisition, BATNA acquisition, deal negotiation, purchase price terms, earnout negotiation
# Negotiating a Business Acquisition with the Whole Board in View

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a large trading bloc suddenly imposing steep tariffs on a broad category of imported manufactured goods, raising costs for companies that depend on those imports as inputs.

Apply it here: New tariffs make a small import business less profitable, and its owner wants to sell. A buyer sees opportunity. Ask across colours: What is each side's best alternative (ORANGE)? How should price be split between cash, seller financing, and an earnout (BLUE)? Which legal protections matter most (PURPLE)? Which tax structure benefits both sides (GREEN)?

## Learning Objectives
- Apply BATNA and anchoring to an acquisition negotiation
- Trade across price, terms, protections, and structure
- Negotiate in a way that preserves the post-closing relationship

## Core Content
At Level 4, ORANGE covered negotiation fundamentals: BATNA (Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement), anchoring, reservation price, and creating value through trade-offs. At Level 5, you use them in one of the most complex negotiations in business: buying a company.

What can be negotiated in an acquisition:
- Price (ORANGE/WHITE): Headline value.
- Payment form (BLUE): Cash at closing, seller financing, earnouts tied to future performance, or equity in the buyer's company.
- Structure (GREEN/PURPLE): Asset or share purchase (PURPLE-L5-023 in the law track), which affects both sides' taxes.
- Protections (PURPLE): Reps, warranties, indemnities, escrows, and caps (PURPLE-L5-021).
- Transition (BLACK): How long the seller stays to help, and in what role.
- Non-compete (PURPLE): Whether the seller can compete afterward.
- Employees (BLACK): Whether key employees stay and on what terms.

Value creation through trade-offs: Because buyers and sellers value different terms differently, a deal can be better for both than a simple price haggle. A seller who cares most about total price may accept an earnout. A buyer who cares most about limiting risk may pay a higher price in exchange for a larger escrow.

BATNA in an acquisition:
- Seller's BATNA: Another buyer, continuing to run the business, or winding it down.
- Buyer's BATNA: Another acquisition target, starting from scratch, or not buying.

A stronger BATNA gives more leverage. The scenario's tariffs may weaken the seller's BATNA if continuing to operate is less attractive.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Squeezing a seller too hard can backfire. If the seller stays on for a transition or holds a seller note, a resentful seller can make the transition harder. Fair deals often produce better outcomes after closing. For UK readers: in an asset purchase, employees may transfer to the buyer automatically under the TUPE regulations, and asset and share deals are taxed very differently, so a solicitor and an accountant should review the structure.
## Worked Example

- Tomas: Two other businesses under consideration, though less attractive. BATNA is moderate.

Tomas's offer: £950,000, structured as:

- £250,000 seller note over 5 years at a fair rate.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: The earnout bridged their disagreement about the future. The seller believed recovery was likely; Tomas was unsure. The earnout let both be right.

- Targeted increases: Raise prices more on products where customers are less price-sensitive.

- Smaller, more frequent increases vs. one large increase: Each has trade-offs in customer perception.
- Adding value: Pair increases with improved service, features, or guarantees.

Communication principles:
- Give advance notice.
- Explain honestly (rising costs of materials, labour, or energy).
- Emphasise the value customers continue to receive.
- Avoid misleading tactics such as quietly shrinking package sizes without disclosure, which may raise legal and trust issues. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE. - BLUE: Shrinking margins can break loan covenants that require certain profit levels. Protecting margin protects financing.
- WHITE: Inflation reduces the real value of cash. Getting paid faster and holding less idle cash can help.
- GREEN: Higher nominal prices can raise tax bills even if real profit has not grown. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE.
- BLACK: Leaders must decide how much margin to sacrifice to keep customers.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Customers usually understand inflation. What damages trust is surprise, hidden changes, or increases that seem much larger than the costs behind them.

- Communication: Customers receive 45 days' notice with a letter explaining cost increases and listing ways to save, including the value line and bulk ordering.
- Finance (BLUE): She shows her bank the pricing plan and projected margins, and the bank agrees not to call the loan while the plan takes effect.

Communication principles:

- Prioritising long-term or contract customers.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Customers often remember how a business treated them during a crisis long after they forget the crisis itself.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: Hugo's company supplies packaging to food producers. A shipping disruption cuts his supply by 40% for three months.

- Updates: Weekly emails with new information.
- Pricing: He passes on only his actual extra costs for substitute materials, not a crisis premium.

After the disruption, most customers stay. Several praise his communication. A competitor who stayed silent and raised prices sharply loses several accounts to Hugo.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Hugo could not prevent the disruption. He could control how he treated customers during it.

SUMMARY: Learn how exchange-rate changes affect international prices and margins, and how pricing currency, contract terms, and financial tools can manage that risk.
KEYWORDS: currency risk pricing, exchange rate risk, international pricing strategy, pricing in foreign currency, hedging
# Currency Risk in International Pricing

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a mid-sized economy's currency losing a large share of its value against major reserve currencies over a few months, driven by capital fleeing the country and eroding investor confidence.

Apply it here: A company sells abroad and prices in its customers' currency. Then its customers' currency loses a third of its value. Ask across colours: What happens to the company's revenue when converted back home (WHITE)? Should prices be raised in the foreign market (ORANGE)? Do contracts allow price changes (PURPLE)? Can financial tools reduce this risk (BLUE)?

## Learning Objectives
- Explain how exchange-rate changes affect international sales
- Compare pricing in home currency vs. customer currency
- Use contract and financial tools to manage currency risk

## Core Content
When a business sells in another country, it faces currency risk: the chance that exchange-rate changes will reduce the value of its sales.

Pricing currency choices:
- Price in your home currency: You know exactly what you will receive. But customers bear the currency risk, and your prices may become unaffordable if their currency weakens.
- Price in the customer's currency: Easier for customers, often more competitive. But you bear the currency risk.
- Price in a third, stable reserve currency: Common in some international trade. Shifts risk to both sides in different ways.

What happens when the customer's currency falls (as in the scenario):
- If you price in their currency: Your revenue in home-currency terms drops.
- If you price in your currency: Your prices rise sharply for customers, and demand may fall.

Either way, someone absorbs the impact.

Tools for managing currency risk:
- Contract clauses (PURPLE): Currency adjustment clauses and shared bands (PURPLE-L5-039 in the law track).
- Pricing reviews (ORANGE): Regular price updates for foreign markets.
- Natural hedging (BLUE): Matching costs and revenues in the same currency, such as buying supplies or paying local staff in the currency you earn.
- Financial hedging (WHITE/BLUE): Tools such as forward contracts that lock in exchange rates for future transactions. These have costs and complexity and are often used with professional guidance.
- Payment timing: Collecting payment faster reduces exposure time.

GREEN connection: REGION-SPECIFIC RULE: Currency gains and losses may have tax consequences. For UK readers: for companies, foreign exchange gains and losses on trading transactions are usually taxed as part of trading profits, with special rules for loans and derivatives, so check the treatment with an accountant.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Small exporters often ignore currency risk until a large move hurts them. A simple policy, such as reviewing foreign prices quarterly and using contract bands, can prevent most painful surprises.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: Pedro's company exports specialty tools. He sells to customers in a foreign market, priced in their currency. At the start, 10 units of their currency equal 1 unit of his.

He sells £100,000 worth (in his currency) per quarter.

The foreign currency falls 33%, so 15 units now equal 1 unit of his currency. The same foreign-currency revenue now converts to about £67,000.

His options:
- Raise foreign prices 50% to restore his revenue. Customers may buy less.
- Keep prices and accept lower revenue.
- Mix: raise prices 20%, reduce costs, and accept some margin loss.

He chooses the mix. For the future, he:
- Adds contract clauses sharing currency moves beyond 10%.
- Opens a small local warehouse and pays some costs in the foreign currency (natural hedge).
- Uses forward contracts for large known orders, with guidance from his bank.
- Reviews foreign pricing quarterly.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Pedro could not prevent the currency move. But with these tools in place, the next move will hurt far less.

# Earnouts and Seller Notes from the Seller's Seat

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a widespread public health disruption halting global shipping and manufacturing for an extended stretch, snarling supply chains across many industries at once.

Apply it here: A business owner agrees to sell with part of the price paid through an earnout tied to future revenue. Then a long disruption hits, and revenue falls. ## Learning Objectives
- Explain the risks sellers take with earnouts and seller notes
- Negotiate protections for sellers
- Evaluate how deferred payments affect the seller's overall financial plan

## Core Content
ORANGE-L5-006 showed how earnouts and seller notes help buyers bridge price gaps. This lesson looks from the seller's seat, where these tools carry real risk.

Seller note risk: The seller becomes a lender (PURPLE-L5-024 in the law track). If the buyer runs the business poorly, the seller may not be paid. Protections include security, personal guarantees, covenants, and reporting requirements.

Earnout risk: Payment depends on future performance, usually revenue or profit. After the sale, the buyer controls the business. Risks include:
- The buyer changes strategy, cuts spending, or combines the business with others, making targets hard to reach.
- Accounting choices that reduce reported profit.
- Events outside anyone's control, like the scenario's disruption.
- Disputes about how results are measured.

Seller protections for earnouts:
- Clear metrics: Revenue is often easier to measure and harder to manipulate than profit.
- Defined accounting rules: Written in the agreement.
- Operating covenants: Buyer promises to run the business in the ordinary course and maintain reasonable resources.
- Information rights: Regular reports and audit rights.
- Acceleration: Full earnout paid if the buyer sells the business or breaches key terms.
- Shorter periods: 1 to 3 years reduces uncertainty.
- Dispute resolution: A named independent accountant for measurement disputes.

How colours connect:
- WHITE: Deferred payments concentrate the seller's wealth in the buyer's success. Sellers should consider diversification.
- GREEN: REGION-SPECIFIC RULE: Earnout and instalment payments may be taxed as received or differently, affecting timing and rates.
- BLACK: A seller who stays on during the earnout period needs clear authority.
- ORANGE: Negotiating leverage is strongest before signing. For UK readers: the right to receive an earnout can itself be treated as an asset for Capital Gains Tax, and earnouts paid in shares or loan notes have special rules, so take tax advice before agreeing the terms.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Sellers should value an earnout at less than its maximum, because of the real chance it will not be fully paid. Cash at closing is worth more than a promise.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: Farouk sells his software company for £4 million: £2.5 million at closing, a £500,000 seller note, and up to £1 million earnout over 2 years based on revenue.

His negotiated protections:
- Earnout metric: Revenue, not profit.
- Buyer covenant: Maintain the sales team at no fewer than 5 people and continue selling the product.
- Monthly revenue reports and annual audit rights.
- Acceleration: Full earnout if the buyer sells the business or discontinues the product.
- Seller note: Secured by business assets and personally guaranteed by the buyer's owner.
- Disputes: Independent accountant decides measurement issues.

The disruption hits in year 1. Revenue falls 20%. The earnout target is missed for year 1, reducing that year's payment. But the buyer also tries to cut the sales team to 3. Farouk points to the covenant, and the buyer restores the team.

In year 2, revenue recovers, and Farouk receives most of that year's earnout. His seller note is paid on schedule.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Farouk received about £3.7 million of the possible £4 million. Without the protections, he might have received far less.

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a central bank raising its benchmark interest rate sharply and repeatedly over a short stretch of months to fight rising inflation.

Apply it here: Inflation rises, and every business faces higher costs. Some can raise prices easily; others lose customers when they try. Ask across colours: What makes one company's customers stay (ORANGE)? Are its advantages legally protected (PURPLE)? How do investors value companies with strong moats (WHITE)? How does pricing power protect cash flow (BLUE)?

## Learning Objectives
- Define pricing power and competitive moats
- Identify common sources of durable advantage
- Connect moats to valuation, strategy, and inflation resilience

## Core Content
Pricing power is a business's ability to raise prices without losing many customers. It is one of the clearest signs of a strong business, especially during inflation.

Pricing power usually comes from a competitive moat: a durable advantage that protects a business from competitors, like a moat around a castle.

Common sources of moats:
- Brand: Customers trust and prefer the brand (ORANGE-L5-010).
- Switching costs: Changing to a competitor is costly, time-consuming, or risky for customers (for example, business software deeply built into operations).
- Network effects: The product becomes more valuable as more people use it (marketplaces, communication platforms).
- Cost advantages: The business can produce at lower cost due to scale, location, or processes.
- Legal protection: Patents, trademarks, licenses, or regulatory approvals (PURPLE).
- Unique assets: Locations, relationships, or data that competitors cannot easily copy.

How colours connect:
- WHITE: Investors often pay higher valuations for businesses with durable moats because future profits are more predictable. WHITE-track lessons on valuation apply here.
- BLUE: Pricing power protects margins and cash flow when costs rise.
- PURPLE: Legal protections can reinforce moats, but most expire or can be challenged.
- ORANGE: Marketing strengthens brand moats and communicates switching value.
- BLACK: Leaders must invest to maintain moats. Moats erode if neglected.

Moats are rarely permanent. Technology changes, competitors adapt, and customer preferences shift.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: A good test of pricing power: if you raised prices 10% tomorrow, what share of customers would leave? If very few, you likely have a moat. If many, your advantage may be weaker than you think.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: Three businesses face 12% cost increases from inflation.

Business B: A local bakery with a loyal following and a strong brand. It raises prices 8%. Most regulars stay; some price-sensitive customers buy less often.

Business C: A generic office supply reseller competing mainly on price. It tries raising prices 8% and loses many customers to cheaper online sellers. It reverses the increase and absorbs costs, cutting margins.

Analysis:
- Business A: Strong switching-cost moat.
- Business B: Moderate brand moat.
- Business C: Little or no moat.

An investor comparing the three would likely value A at the highest multiple of earnings, B in the middle, and C lowest (WHITE).

Business C's strategy response: build a moat by specialising in a niche (supplies for dental offices), offering automatic reordering that creates convenience and switching costs, and improving service. ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Moats can be built deliberately, but it takes time and investment.

KEYWORDS: crisis communication, reputation management, product recall communication, public relations crisis, brand crisis response
# Crisis Reputation Management

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a widespread public health disruption halting global shipping and manufacturing for an extended stretch, snarling supply chains across many industries at once.

Apply it here: During a widespread disruption, a company's product fails for many customers, and complaints spread online. Ask across colours: What should the company say publicly, and how fast (ORANGE)? What legal risks come with admitting fault (PURPLE)? What remedies can the company afford (BLUE)? Who in leadership takes responsibility (BLACK)?

## Learning Objectives
- Describe the stages of crisis response
- Balance honesty, speed, and legal considerations
- Coordinate remedies, communication, and leadership accountability

## Core Content
A reputation crisis can come from a product failure, a service breakdown, an employee's misconduct, a data breach, or a viral complaint. How a business responds often matters more than the original problem.

Stages of crisis response:
1. Detect: Monitor customer feedback, reviews, and social media to catch problems early.
2. Assess: Understand what happened, how many people are affected, and whether safety is involved.
3. Respond quickly: Acknowledge the issue publicly, even before you have all the answers.
4. Take responsibility where appropriate: Explain what you are doing to fix it.
5. Offer remedies: Refunds, replacements, repairs, or other fair solutions.
6. Update: Keep people informed as you learn more.
7. Learn and improve: Fix the root cause and share what changed.

Balancing honesty and legal risk:
- PURPLE: Lawyers may worry that admitting fault increases liability. Communication should be honest while avoiding speculation or statements beyond known facts. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE: Some jurisdictions have rules about apologies and admissions. Safety issues may trigger mandatory reporting or recall obligations.
- Coordinate legal and communication teams, but do not let legal caution cause silence, which often damages trust more. For UK readers: in England and Wales an apology does not in itself amount to an admission of negligence under the Compensation Act 2006, and Scotland has its own Apologies Act, but statements should still be reviewed by a solicitor.

How colours connect:
- ORANGE: Clear, human communication across channels.
- PURPLE: Legal obligations, insurance notification (PURPLE-L5-017 in the law track), and careful wording.
- BLUE: Remedies cost money. Insurance may cover some costs.
- BLACK: Visible leadership accountability.
- WHITE: Well-handled crises can limit damage to business value.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Customers often forgive mistakes. They rarely forgive silence, deflection, or dishonesty.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: Leilani's company sells portable heaters. During a disruption, a supplier's faulty part causes some heaters to overheat. No injuries occur, but videos spread online.

Her response:
- Hour 1 (Detect/Assess): Her team confirms the issue affects a specific production batch.
- Hour 4 (Respond): She posts a clear statement: "We've learned some heaters from batch 2204 may overheat. Please stop using them. We are investigating and will provide free replacements or full refunds."
- Legal (PURPLE): Her attorney confirms reporting obligations to the product safety regulator and helps coordinate a voluntary recall. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE. She notifies her insurer.
- Remedies (BLUE): Free replacements or refunds, with prepaid return shipping.
- Leadership (BLACK): Leilani records a video explaining what happened and what the company is doing.
- Updates: Daily updates for the first week.
- Improvement: She adds testing of supplier parts before assembly and shares this change publicly.

Months later, customer surveys show trust has largely recovered. Several customers praise the response publicly.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: The faulty part was not Leilani's fault directly, but taking responsibility quickly turned a crisis into a demonstration of trustworthiness.

TITLE: Founder Brand vs. Company Brand
SUMMARY: Explore how a founder's personal reputation can build a company's credibility, the risks of depending on one person, and how to balance personal and company brands for long-term value.
KEYWORDS: founder personal brand, company brand, key person risk marketing, thought leadership, founder credibility
# Founder Brand vs. Company Brand

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a populous nation's real estate and consumer credit markets, after years of rapid expansion, beginning to show signs of a sharp correction as defaults start rising.

Apply it here: In a market correction, customers become skeptical and prefer businesses they trust personally. A founder's personal reputation brings in clients. Ask across colours: Is the founder's brand an asset or a risk to the company (ORANGE/WHITE)? Who owns the founder's content and social accounts (PURPLE)? What happens if the founder leaves or becomes unavailable (BLACK)?

## Learning Objectives
- Explain how founder brands build credibility
- Identify key person risk in personal branding
- Balance founder and company brands for long-term business value

## Core Content
Many businesses, especially small ones, are built on the founder's personal reputation. Customers trust the founder's expertise, story, and values. The curriculum itself reflects this: credibility from lived experience over credentials is part of the voice and tone mandate.

Benefits of a strong founder brand:
- Trust: People buy from people they trust.
- Differentiation: A founder's story and perspective are hard to copy.
- Low-cost marketing: Content, speaking, and personal networks can attract customers.

Risks:
- Key person risk: If the business depends entirely on the founder, it may lose value if the founder leaves, becomes ill, or damages their reputation. Buyers may discount the business (WHITE).
- Scalability: The founder cannot be everywhere.
- Ownership confusion: Who owns content, social media accounts, and email lists built under the founder's name?

How colours connect:
- ORANGE: Founder content can be powerful marketing.
- PURPLE: Clear agreements on who owns accounts, content, trademarks, and name rights (PURPLE-L5-028 in the law track). If a founder sells the company, can the buyer use the founder's name?
- WHITE: Businesses dependent on one person often receive lower valuations.
- BLACK: Developing other visible experts and leaders spreads credibility across the team.
- BLUE: Key person insurance can protect against sudden loss (PURPLE-L5-017).

Balance: Use the founder's credibility to launch and build trust, then gradually build the company brand, systems, and team so the business can thrive beyond any one person. This connects to the systems epiphany: a business that depends entirely on its owner cannot scale.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: The founder's brand should open the door. The company's brand, systems, and team should keep customers inside.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: Raymond is a well-known tax advisor with a large following. His firm's clients come mainly because of him.

Problem: When Raymond considers selling his firm, buyers say its value depends too heavily on him. If he leaves, clients may follow.

His five-year plan:
- Brand: Gradually shifts marketing from "Raymond's advice" to "The firm's team of advisors," featuring other advisors in content and events.
- Team: Develops two senior advisors as visible experts with their own client relationships.
- Legal (PURPLE): Clarifies in writing that the firm owns its website, email list, and firm social accounts. Raymond keeps his personal accounts but agrees to a transition plan and a limited licence for the buyer to reference his name for a period after sale.
- Systems: Documents client service processes.
- Insurance: Buys key person insurance.

Five years later, less than half of new clients mention Raymond by name. A buyer values the firm significantly higher than before.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Raymond's personal brand built the firm. Building the firm's own brand made it valuable beyond him.

Some of the strongest marketing does not look like marketing. It looks like helping people. Communities, education, and mentorship can create trust, loyalty, and referrals.

Forms of community and mentorship marketing:

- Loyalty: Members feel connected to the community, not just the product.

- Referrals become her largest source of new customers, lowering acquisition costs.

TITLE: Marketing to a Global Audience with Different Purchasing Power
SUMMARY: Learn how to market and price for customers in economies with very different income levels, using purchasing power, local context, and the principle that success is relative to starting point.
KEYWORDS: purchasing power parity pricing, global pricing strategy, emerging markets marketing, regional pricing, relative success
# Marketing to a Global Audience with Different Purchasing Power

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a mid-sized economy's currency losing a large share of its value against major reserve currencies over a few months, driven by capital fleeing the country and eroding investor confidence.

Apply it here: A currency collapse sharply reduces local incomes in one country, while customers in another remain relatively wealthy. A company sells a digital course in both. Ask across colours: Should prices be the same everywhere (ORANGE)? How do exchange rates and purchasing power affect revenue (WHITE/BLUE)? What legal or tax issues arise from regional pricing (PURPLE/GREEN)? How should success stories be framed for each audience?

## Learning Objectives
- Explain purchasing power differences across markets
- Design regional pricing and messaging
- Apply the relativity of achievement principle to global marketing

## Core Content
A price that feels small in one country may be a week's income in another. Global marketing must account for these differences.

Purchasing power parity (PPP) is a concept used to compare what money can buy in different countries. VERIFIED FACT: Economists use PPP-adjusted measures to compare living standards because exchange rates alone do not reflect local prices.

Regional pricing approaches:
- Uniform pricing: Same price everywhere. Simple, but may exclude customers in lower-income markets.
- PPP-adjusted pricing: Lower prices in markets with lower purchasing power.
- Tiered products: Different versions at different prices.
- Local payment methods and smaller packages: Making purchases accessible.

Risks of regional pricing:
- Arbitrage: Customers from higher-priced regions may try to buy at lower regional prices. Businesses may use location verification or local payment methods.
- Legal: REGION-SPECIFIC RULE: Some jurisdictions restrict geographic price discrimination in certain situations.
- Tax: VAT and digital services taxes may apply differently by country (ORANGE-L5-033). For UK readers: since Brexit the EU geo-blocking rules no longer apply in Great Britain, so businesses can usually charge different prices in different countries, but price displays must still be clear and not misleading.

Messaging and relativity of achievement: The curriculum teaches that success is measured relative to starting point, not absolute scale. A small business earning a modest income in one economy may represent the same level of achievement and progress as a much larger business elsewhere. Marketing should reflect this:
- Use examples and success stories relevant to local conditions.
- Avoid implying that only large absolute numbers count as success.
- Respect local circumstances without assuming limitations.

How colours connect:
- ORANGE: Pricing and messaging.
- WHITE/BLUE: Currency and revenue effects (ORANGE-L5-015).
- PURPLE/GREEN: Legal and tax compliance.
- BLACK: Values and respect for diverse audiences.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Regional pricing can expand access and grow total revenue, especially for digital products where extra customers cost little to serve.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: Kwabena sells an online bookkeeping course for £120.

In Market A (higher incomes), sales are steady. In Market B, after a currency collapse, £120 represents a much larger share of monthly income. Sales there fall to almost zero.

His changes:
- PPP-adjusted pricing: Market B price set at £35, paid in local currency through a local payment method.
- Arbitrage protection: Regional pricing tied to local payment methods and billing location.
- Tax: VAT registration where required. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE.
- Messaging: Market B marketing features a local student who started a small bookkeeping service for market vendors, showing her progress from her own starting point. Market A marketing features a student who grew a regional accounting firm.
- Legal review: Confirms regional pricing complies with rules in his markets. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE.

Results: Market B sales grow strongly. Because the course is digital, each additional sale costs very little to deliver. Total revenue increases.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Kwabena did not lower the course's value. He matched price and message to each audience's reality.

===== ORANGE-L5-038
TITLE: Customer Research Before Committing Capital
SUMMARY: Learn why validating customer demand, costs, and competition before investing heavily can prevent costly mistakes, and why walking away from a weak opportunity can be a sound decision.
KEYWORDS: customer validation, market research before investing, minimum order quantity, competitive market evaluation, pivot decision
# Customer Research Before Committing Capital

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a large trading bloc suddenly imposing steep tariffs on a broad category of imported manufactured goods, raising costs for companies that depend on those imports as inputs.

Apply it here: New tariffs raise the cost of imported products just as an entrepreneur plans to launch a private-label product. Ask across colours: Has real customer demand been tested (ORANGE)? What minimum order and capital are required (BLUE)? How crowded is the market? Would a lower-capital model reduce risk while testing the idea?

## Learning Objectives
- Validate customer demand before large investments
- Evaluate minimum order quantities, capital needs, and competition
- Recognise when to pivot or walk away

## Core Content
Many businesses fail not because the founders worked too little, but because they invested heavily before confirming that enough customers would pay enough to cover costs.

Validation steps before committing capital:
1. Customer interviews: Talk to potential customers about their problems and current solutions.
2. Demand tests: Pre-orders, waitlists, landing pages, or small test sales.
3. Competition review: How many competitors exist? What do they charge? How are they positioned?
4. Unit economics: Price, cost per unit, margins, and customer acquisition cost (ORANGE-L5-003).
5. Capital requirements: Minimum order quantities (MOQs), inventory, equipment, and marketing costs.

Minimum order quantity (MOQ) is the smallest order a supplier will accept. For private-label products, MOQs can require large upfront inventory purchases, tying up capital and creating risk if products do not sell.

A curriculum case study, drawn from the author's family: a young founder planned a private-label dog food company. Research revealed high MOQs, significant capital requirements, and a crowded, competitive market with established brands. He made the sound decision to walk away from that model, and pivoted to a website and service-based business with much lower capital needs. This was not failure. It was disciplined decision-making.

How colours connect:
- ORANGE: Customer research and competitive positioning.
- BLUE: Capital requirements, MOQs, and cash flow. The BLUE track uses this case study for capital constraints and low-capital models.
- PURPLE: Supplier contracts, product regulations (especially for food, pet food, and health products), and labelling. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE.
- WHITE: Compare the expected return of the venture with the risk and alternative uses of capital.
- BLACK: The "fall back and regroup" principle applies. Walking away from a weak opportunity preserves resources for a better one. For UK readers: food labelling, including allergen information, is regulated and enforced with guidance from the Food Standards Agency, and pet food has its own rules, so check labels with Trading Standards or a specialist.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: The cheapest mistakes are the ones caught during research, before money is spent.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: Selena plans to launch a private-label line of reusable water bottles.

Her research:
- Customer interviews: 30 people. Most like the idea, but few say they would pay more than existing brands.
- Demand test: A landing page with a pre-order option. 2% of visitors pre-order.
- Competition: Dozens of established brands with strong reviews and low prices.
- MOQ: The manufacturer requires 5,000 units minimum. With tariffs, cost is £6 per unit: £30,000 upfront.
- Unit economics: At a competitive £18 price, after marketing, shipping, and platform fees, her margin per bottle is only about £3.

Her conclusion: It would take 10,000 sales to recover her investment, in a crowded market with weak demand signals.

Instead, she pivots to a lower-capital model: custom-printed bottles for local businesses and events, ordered only after customers pay deposits, using a supplier with low MOQs. Her upfront risk falls dramatically.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Selena's research saved her from a £30,000 inventory bet. Her pivot let her test demand with customers' money instead of her own.

===== ORANGE-L5-039
TITLE: Launch Sequencing and Capital Runway
SUMMARY: Learn how to sequence a product or market launch in stages to match available capital, reduce risk, and use early results to guide later investment.
KEYWORDS: launch sequencing, capital runway, staged product launch, minimum viable product, go to market phases
# Launch Sequencing and Capital Runway

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a central bank raising its benchmark interest rate sharply and repeatedly over a short stretch of months to fight rising inflation.

Apply it here: Rising interest rates make funding harder to find. A startup has enough money for 12 months. Ask across colours: Should it launch everywhere at once or in stages (ORANGE)? How long will the money last under each plan (BLUE)? What milestones would convince investors to fund the next stage (WHITE)? What contracts and legal steps are needed at each stage (PURPLE)?

## Learning Objectives
- Explain capital runway and burn rate
- Design a staged launch that matches available capital
- Use milestones to guide further investment

## Core Content
Runway is how long a business can operate before running out of money. It is calculated by dividing available cash by monthly burn rate (net cash spent per month).

ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: £300,000 in cash and a £25,000 monthly burn gives 12 months of runway.

Launching everything at once (all products, all markets, full marketing) burns cash quickly. If results disappoint, there may be no money left to adjust. Staged launches reduce this risk.

A staged launch approach:
1. Stage 1: Minimum viable offering in a small test market. Goal: prove customers will buy and learn what works.
2. Stage 2: Refine and expand to a larger market segment once key metrics are met.
3. Stage 3: Scale marketing, channels, and markets once unit economics are proven.

Each stage has milestones: specific results that must be achieved before moving on (for example, a target customer acquisition cost, repeat purchase rate, or monthly revenue).

How colours connect:
- BLUE: Runway, burn rate, and cash planning. Staging extends runway.
- WHITE: Investors often fund the next stage when milestones are met. In tight funding markets, proving results with less money is especially valuable.
- ORANGE: Each stage produces customer insight that improves the next.
- PURPLE: Each stage may require new contracts, registrations, or compliance steps (for example, entering a new region, ORANGE-L5-014).
- BLACK: Leaders must resist the temptation to scale before evidence supports it.

The fall back and regroup principle: If a stage misses its milestones, the business can adjust, pivot, or pause before spending everything.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: In uncertain funding environments, the goal is not to launch as big as possible. It is to learn as much as possible per dollar spent.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: Ines has £360,000 to launch a healthy snack brand. Funding markets are tight.

Plan A (launch everywhere): National online launch, three product lines, heavy advertising. Burn: £60,000/month. Runway: 6 months.

Plan B (staged):
- Stage 1 (months 1 to 4): One product line, sold online and in 10 local stores. Burn: £20,000/month. Milestones: 30% repeat purchase rate, customer acquisition cost under £25, and positive store feedback.
- Stage 2 (months 5 to 9): If milestones are met, add a second product and expand to 50 stores in the region. Burn: £35,000/month.

1. Understand the customer's needs first.
2. Recommend only what genuinely fits.
3. Explain costs and benefits clearly.
4. Make saying no easy.

- Staff bonuses are based on customer satisfaction scores and repeat visits, not number of add-ons.

After a year, Shop B's average sale is slightly lower than Shop A's, but its repeat customer rate and referrals are much higher. Its total revenue grows faster.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Shop B earned more by helping customers buy what they actually needed.

KEYWORDS: marketing ROI, return on marketing investment, marketing attribution, time value of money marketing, after-tax ROI
# Measuring Marketing ROI After Taxes and Time

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a central bank raising its benchmark interest rate sharply and repeatedly over a short stretch of months to fight rising inflation.

Apply it here: With interest rates high, a company must cut its budget and wants to keep only the marketing that truly pays. Ask across colours: Which campaigns bring profitable customers, not just sales (ORANGE)? How does the time until payback matter when money is expensive (WHITE/BLUE)? How do taxes affect the true cost and return (GREEN)?

## Learning Objectives
- Calculate marketing ROI using gross profit, not revenue
- Include customer lifetime, time value, and taxes
- Recognise attribution limits in marketing measurement

## Core Content
Many businesses measure marketing by revenue: "This campaign brought in £100,000 in sales." But revenue is not profit. Level 5 measurement includes the whole board.

Basic marketing ROI:
ROI = (Gross profit from campaign - Campaign cost) / Campaign cost

ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: A £10,000 campaign brings £50,000 in sales at a 40% gross margin. Gross profit = £20,000. ROI = (£20,000 - £10,000) / £10,000 = 100%.

Improvements for Level 5:
- Customer lifetime: If customers keep buying after the first purchase, include expected future gross profit (ORANGE-L5-003), adjusted for churn.
- Time value of money (WHITE): Profit received in two years is worth less than profit today, especially when interest rates are high. Discounting future profits gives a more realistic picture.
- Taxes (GREEN): REGION-SPECIFIC RULE: Marketing costs are often deductible, reducing their after-tax cost for profitable businesses. Profits are taxed. After-tax comparisons can change rankings between options.
- Payback period (BLUE): How long until the campaign's cost is recovered? Faster payback reduces cash strain. For UK readers: companies pay Corporation Tax on profits, with a lower rate for smaller profits, and marketing costs are generally deductible, so compare options after tax with an accountant.

Attribution challenges:
- Customers often see several marketing touches before buying. Which one gets credit?
- Some marketing (brand building, content) works slowly and is hard to measure directly.
- Tools that track attribution are imperfect and affected by privacy limits (ORANGE-L5-021).

Practical approaches: Test campaigns against control groups when possible, track customer source at purchase, and compare trends over time rather than trusting any single number.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Precise marketing ROI is often impossible. Directionally correct measurement, including profit, time, and taxes, is far better than precise measurement of the wrong thing (revenue alone).

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: Maria compares two campaigns, each costing £20,000.

Campaign 1: Discount ads. Brings £80,000 in first-month sales at a 25% gross margin (because of the discount). Gross profit: £20,000. Few customers return.
ROI (first purchase only): (£20,000 - £20,000) / £20,000 = 0%.

Campaign 2: Educational content and email series. Brings £40,000 in first-month sales at a 45% margin. Gross profit: £18,000. But customers return: expected additional gross profit over two years is £30,000.

Adjustments:
- Time value: With high rates, Maria discounts the future £30,000 to about £26,000 in today's value. (Illustrative; the actual figure depends on the rate used and timing.)
- Campaign 2 lifetime gross profit (today's value): £18,000 + £26,000 = £44,000.
- ROI: (£44,000 - £20,000) / £20,000 = 120%.
- Taxes: Both campaigns' costs are deductible and profits taxed in her jurisdiction, so the ranking holds after taxes. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE.

Revenue made Campaign 1 look better. Profit, lifetime, and time value show Campaign 2 is far better. ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Maria shifts her budget toward Campaign 2's approach.

- Coordinate marketing decisions with finance, legal, tax, and leadership
- Build a plan that adapts as results come in

## Core Content
A go-to-market (GTM) plan describes how a business will bring a product or service to customers. Level 5 GTM plans integrate every colour into one strategy.

Components of an integrated GTM plan:
1. Target customer (ORANGE): Who, what problem, and why now?
2. Positioning (ORANGE): Why choose us over alternatives?
3. Pricing (ORANGE/BLUE/GREEN): Price level, structure, cash timing, and taxes (ORANGE-L5-002).
4. Channels (ORANGE/PURPLE): Direct, distributors, marketplaces, and their contracts (ORANGE-L5-016).
5. Marketing and sales (ORANGE/BLACK): Campaigns, team, and compensation (ORANGE-L5-008).
6. Financing (BLUE/WHITE): Capital needed, runway, and staged milestones (ORANGE-L5-039).
7. Legal (PURPLE): Trademarks, claims compliance, privacy, contracts, and product regulations.
8. Tax (GREEN): Sales tax or VAT, cross-border obligations, and structure. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE.
9. Operations and supply (BLUE): Suppliers, inventory, and fulfilment (ORANGE-L5-040).
10. Metrics and decision rules (BLACK): What results trigger expansion, adjustment, or pause (ORANGE-L5-044).
11. Risks and contingencies: What could go wrong, and what is the plan for each? For UK readers: VAT, Making Tax Digital requirements and any overseas tax registrations should be part of the plan, so ask an accountant to review the structure before launch.

Why integration matters: A brilliant marketing plan fails if the business runs out of cash, violates advertising rules, cannot deliver products, or faces unexpected taxes. Each part depends on the others.

The chess analogy: Each component is a piece. The GTM plan coordinates them toward checkmate: customers buying profitably, sustainably, and legally.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: The best GTM plans are living documents. They set a clear starting strategy, then change as customer data and market conditions reveal what works.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: Hye-jin's company must launch a redesigned air purifier after export controls cut off a key component. She builds an integrated GTM plan:

- Target: Small offices and clinics in two new regional markets.

- Channels: Direct sales to clinics; regional distributor for small offices, with a contract specifying territory and customer ownership.
- Sales: Two salespeople with salary plus margin-based commission.

- Tax: VAT registration in both markets. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE.

- Metrics: Stage 2 begins when 50 clinics sign and subscription churn is below 3% monthly.
- Risks: Component delays (backup supplier), slow adoption (flexible pilots).

Six months in, clinic sales exceed targets while small-office sales lag. She shifts resources toward clinics. ASSUMPTION/OPINION: The plan worked because it was integrated from the start and flexible enough to adjust.

# Seizing a Confirmed Market Window with Full Commitment

## Scenario Opener

## Core Content
The knife-fight doctrine appeared in ORANGE-L4 lessons 46 to 49 as a tactical competitive angle: offence versus defence, recognising opportunity windows, full commitment once warranted, and the discipline to escalate or fall back and regroup. It also appears in BLACK-L4, RED-L5, WHITE-L5, BLUE-L5, GREEN-L5, GOLD-L5, and PURPLE-L5. At ORANGE-L5, it becomes orchestration: seizing an opportunity requires every colour to move together.

As always, the doctrine is scoped as a metaphor for competition, preparation, and resilience, never literal conflict.

The core idea: a purely defensive posture can avoid losses, but it cannot produce a win. Sometimes the market presents a genuine opportunity (carpe diem), and hesitation lets others take it.

- A new customer need appears suddenly (regulation, technology, or events).

- ORANGE: Targeted outreach to affected customers, clear positioning, and fast response.
- BLUE: Financing ready for inventory, staff, and working capital.
- PURPLE: Contracts ready, trademarks registered, compliance confirmed (PURPLE-L5-046 in the law track).
- BLACK: Leadership aligned, decision authority clear, operations able to deliver.
- GREEN: Tax implications of rapid expansion understood.

The corollary: if you commit, commit fully. Don't bring a "butter knife" (a weak campaign, underfunded inventory, or unclear contracts) to a moment that requires strength.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Most market windows close faster than expected. The businesses that capture them usually prepared before the window opened. - ORANGE: A targeted campaign to affected customers with a migration guide and a dedicated support team. Special onboarding, not deep discounts.
- BLUE: She draws her pre-arranged credit line to double inventory and hires four temporary technicians.
- PURPLE: Her standard contracts are ready. Her attorney confirms her sensors meet regional certification. Trademarks are already registered.
- BLACK: One executive leads the effort with authority to make quick decisions.

TITLE: Honest Reassessment of an Active Campaign
SUMMARY: Learn how to evaluate an ongoing marketing campaign or market push honestly, avoiding sunk cost and ego traps, and decide when to continue, adjust, scale back, or fall back and regroup.
KEYWORDS: campaign reassessment, marketing sunk cost, when to stop a campaign, market exit decision, strategy adjustment
# Honest Reassessment of an Active Campaign

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a central bank raising its benchmark interest rate sharply and repeatedly over a short stretch of months to fight rising inflation.

Apply it here: A company launched an aggressive expansion into a new market when rates were low. Now rates have spiked, results lag, and costs are rising. Ask across colours: What do the real numbers show (ORANGE)? Can the business afford to continue (BLUE)? Is leadership holding on because of pride or past spending (BLACK)? What contracts would need to be ended (PURPLE)?

## Learning Objectives
- Explain why active campaigns need regular honest review
- Identify sunk cost, ego, and anchoring traps in marketing
- Choose between continuing, adjusting, scaling back, or regrouping

## Core Content
The third part of the knife-fight doctrine is honest ongoing reassessment. Committing fully to a market push does not mean refusing to reconsider it as conditions and evidence change.

Why reassessment matters:
- Early results reveal whether assumptions were right.
- Market conditions change (rates, competition, customer behaviour).
- Costs often exceed plans.
- Opportunity costs grow: money and people spent here cannot be used elsewhere.

Common traps:
- Sunk cost: "We've spent £500,000 on this market; we can't stop now." Past spending is gone either way. Decide based on future costs and future value.
- Ego: Leaders who championed a campaign may resist admitting it is not working.
- Anchoring: Clinging to original projections despite new data.
- Vanity metrics: Focusing on impressions, followers, or revenue instead of profit and customer lifetime value (ORANGE-L5-043).

A reassessment routine:
1. Compare actual results with milestones (ORANGE-L5-039).
2. Update forecasts using current data, not original assumptions.
3. Estimate remaining costs and expected future value.
4. Consider opportunity costs.
5. Invite honest input, including from people who disagree.
6. Check contract commitments (PURPLE): leases, distributor agreements, and marketing contracts that affect exit costs.

Options:
- Continue as planned.
- Adjust (change targeting, channels, pricing, or messaging).
- Scale back to a smaller, profitable core.
- Fall back and regroup: pause or exit, preserve resources, and return later if conditions improve. The military analogy frames this as a legitimate tactic, not failure.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Knowing when to pull back is as important as knowing when to push forward. Both are part of disciplined competitive strategy.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: Andile's company expanded into a new city with a £600,000 marketing and staffing budget, projecting profitability within 12 months.

After 10 months:
- Spent: £520,000.
- Customer acquisition cost: £180 (projected £90).
- Monthly revenue: 40% of target.
- Rates have spiked, raising the cost of his credit line.

His team's first reaction: "We're almost at 12 months. Let's push harder."

Honest reassessment:
- Sunk cost: The £520,000 is gone regardless.
- Updated forecast: At current trends, profitability would take 30 more months and another £400,000.
- Opportunity cost: His home market has strong demand he has neglected.
- Contracts (PURPLE): His office lease in the new city has a termination option with 3 months' notice.

Decision: Scale back. He keeps a small team serving existing customers profitably, cuts marketing spending there, exercises the lease termination option and moves to a shared workspace, and redirects resources to his home market.

A year later, his home market grows strongly. He plans to revisit the new city when conditions improve. ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Falling back and regrouping preserved his company's strength for a better opportunity.

# Calculated Competitive Risk vs. Reckless Marketing Gambles

## Scenario Opener
ASSUMPTION/OPINION (hypothetical scenario for teaching purposes only): Picture a large trading bloc suddenly imposing steep tariffs on a broad category of imported manufactured goods, raising costs for companies that depend on those imports as inputs.

Apply it here: New tariffs shake up a market. One company considers starting a price war to drive out rivals; another considers a bold but well-researched product launch. Which leader is prepared to own the consequences (BLACK)?

## Learning Objectives
- Distinguish calculated competitive risk from reckless gambling
- Identify warning signs of reckless marketing moves
- Apply courage and consequence ownership to competitive decisions

## Core Content
The fourth part of the knife-fight doctrine separates calculated risk from reckless gambling and emphasises courage and consequence ownership.

Calculated competitive risk:
- Based on evidence: customer research, market data, and tested assumptions.
- Downside is understood and survivable.
- Resources are adequate for the move.
- Legal and ethical boundaries are respected.
- Clear exit criteria if results disappoint.

Reckless marketing gambles:
- Betting the company on untested assumptions.
- Price wars that the company cannot afford to sustain, destroying margins for everyone.
- Exaggerated or misleading claims to win customers quickly (PURPLE-L5-035 in the law track).
- Tactics that may violate competition law, such as agreements with competitors to fix prices, or in some jurisdictions pricing below cost intended to drive out rivals. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE.
- Spending borrowed money on campaigns with no measurement. For UK readers: price-fixing between competitors is prohibited under the Competition Act 1998, and a dominant business can break the law by pricing below cost to force out rivals, so take advice from a competition lawyer.

Courage in competition: Calculated risk takes courage. Launching a new product, entering a market, or challenging a larger competitor can be frightening. The doctrine encourages boldness when evidence supports it.

Consequence ownership: Leaders who take calculated risks accept responsibility for outcomes. They do not blame the team, the market, or advisors if a well-reasoned bet does not work. They communicate honestly with employees, investors, and customers.

How colours connect:
- ORANGE: Evidence-based competitive strategy.
- BLUE: Survivable downside.
- PURPLE: Legal boundaries, especially competition and advertising law.
- BLACK: Courage and accountability.
- WHITE: Risk relative to potential return.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: The line between bold and reckless is not the size of the move. It is whether the move is grounded in evidence, survivable if it fails, and within legal and ethical limits.

## Worked Example
ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE: Two companies respond to tariff-driven changes in the kitchen appliance market.

- Tests the concept with 200 customers and pre-orders.
- Budgets a launch that, if it fails, would cost 15% of reserves.
- Backs marketing claims with testing.
- Sets exit criteria: if sales are below 60% of target after 6 months, reduce investment.
- The CEO tells employees and investors about the risks openly.

Sales reach 80% of target. The product becomes profitable in year two.

Company B (reckless): The CEO decides to cut prices 40% below cost to drive smaller competitors out, funded by borrowed money. Marketing claims "the lowest prices forever." No research, no exit plan.

Within months, margins collapse. Competitors match some prices. A regulator begins reviewing whether the pricing and claims raise concerns. REGION-SPECIFIC RULE. The CEO blames the market.

ASSUMPTION/OPINION: Both companies acted boldly. Only Company A's boldness was grounded, survivable, lawful, and owned.
